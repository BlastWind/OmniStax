In this section, we consider centers of mass (also called centroids, under certain conditions) and moments. The basic idea of the center of mass is the notion of a balancing point. Many of us have seen performers who spin plates on the ends of sticks. The performers try to keep several of them spinning without allowing any of them to drop. If we look at a single plate (without spinning it), there is a sweet spot on the plate where it balances perfectly on the stick. If we put the stick anywhere other than that sweet spot, the plate does not balance and it falls to the ground. (That is why performers spin the plates; the spin helps keep the plates from falling even if the stick is not exactly in the right place.) Mathematically, that sweet spot is called the center of mass of the plate.

In this section, we first examine these concepts in a one-dimensional context, then expand our development to consider centers of mass of two-dimensional regions and symmetry. Last, we use centroids to find the volume of certain solids by applying the theorem of Pappus.

Center of Mass and Moments

Let’s begin by looking at the center of mass in a one-dimensional context. Consider a long, thin wire or rod of negligible mass resting on a fulcrum, as shown in Figure 6.62(a). Now suppose we place objects having masses $\km_{1}$ and $\km_{2}$ at distances $d_{1}$ and $d_{2}$ from the fulcrum, respectively, as shown in Figure 6.62(b).

The most common real-life example of a system like this is a playground seesaw, or teeter-totter, with children of different weights sitting at different distances from the center. On a seesaw, if one child sits at each end, the heavier child sinks down and the lighter child is lifted into the air. If the heavier child slides in toward the center, though, the seesaw balances. Applying this concept to the masses on the rod, we note that the masses balance each other if and only if $\km_{1}d_{1}=\km_{2}d_{2}.$

In the seesaw example, we balanced the system by moving the masses (children) with respect to the fulcrum. However, we are really interested in systems in which the masses are not allowed to move, and instead we balance the system by moving the fulcrum. Suppose we have two point masses, $\km_{1}$ and $\km_{2},$ located on a number line at points $x_{1}$ and $x_{2},$ respectively (Figure 6.63). The center of mass, $\kxbar,$ is the point where the fulcrum should be placed to make the system balance.

The expression in the numerator, $\km_{1}x_{1}+\km_{2}x_{2},$ is called the first moment of the system with respect to the origin. If the context is clear, we often drop the word first and just refer to this expression as the moment of the system. The expression in the denominator, $\km_{1}+\km_{2},$ is the total mass of the system. Thus, the center of mass of the system is the point at which the total mass of the system could be concentrated without changing the moment.

This idea is not limited just to two point masses. In general, if n masses, $\km_{1},\km_{2}\text{,\ldots},\km_{n},$ are placed on a number line at points $x_{1},x_{2}\text{,\ldots},x_{n},$ respectively, then the center of mass of the system is given by

$$\kxbar=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \km_{i}x_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \km_{i}}.$$
Theorem

Center of Mass of Objects on a Line

Let $\km_{1},\km_{2}\text{,\ldots},\km_{n}$ be point masses placed on a number line at points $x_{1},x_{2}\text{,\ldots},x_{n},$ respectively, and let $\km=\sum_{i=1}^{n} \km_{i}$ denote the total mass of the system. Then, the moment of the system with respect to the origin is given by

$$\kM=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\km_{i}x_{i}\tag{6.14}$$

and the center of mass of the system is given by

$$\kxbar=\frac{\kM}{\km}.\tag{6.15}$$

We apply this theorem in the following example.

Example 6.29 · Finding the Center of Mass of Objects along a Line

Suppose four point masses are placed on a number line as follows:

$$\begin{array}{llll}\km_{1}=30\;\text{kg,}\;\text{placed at}\;x_{1}=-2\;\text{m} & & & \km_{2}=5\;\text{kg,}\;\text{placed at}\;x_{2}=3\;\text{m} \\ \km_{3}=10\;\text{kg,}\;\text{placed at}\;x_{3}=6\;\text{m} & & & \km_{4}=15\;\text{kg,}\;\text{placed at}\;x_{4}=-3\;\text{m}.\end{array}$$

Find the moment of the system with respect to the origin and find the center of mass of the system.

Solution

First, we need to calculate the moment of the system:

$$\begin{aligned}\kM &=\sum_{i=1}^{4} \km_{i}x_{i} \\ &=-60+15+60-45=-30.\end{aligned}$$

Now, to find the center of mass, we need the total mass of the system:

$$\begin{aligned}m &=\sum_{i=1}^{4} \km_{i} \\ &=30+5+10+15=60\;\text{kg}.\end{aligned}$$

Then we have

$$\kxbar=\frac{\kM}{\km}=\frac{-30}{60}=-\frac{1}{2}.$$

The center of mass is located 1/2 m to the left of the origin.

We can generalize this concept to find the center of mass of a system of point masses in a plane. Let $\km_{1}$ be a point mass located at point $(x_{1},y_{1})$ in the plane. Then the moment $\kMx$ of the mass with respect to the x-axis is given by $\kMx=\km_{1}y_{1}.$ Similarly, the moment $\kMy$ with respect to the y-axis is given by $\kMy=\km_{1}x_{1}.$ Notice that the x-coordinate of the point is used to calculate the moment with respect to the y-axis, and vice versa. The reason is that the x-coordinate gives the distance from the point mass to the y-axis, and the y-coordinate gives the distance to the x-axis (see the following figure).

If we have several point masses in the xy-plane, we can use the moments with respect to the x- and y-axes to calculate the x- and y-coordinates of the center of mass of the system.

Theorem

Center of Mass of Objects in a Plane

Let $\km_{1},\km_{2}\text{,\ldots},\km_{n}$ be point masses located in the xy-plane at points $(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})\text{,\ldots},(x_{n},y_{n}),$ respectively, and let $\km=\sum_{i=1}^{n} \km_{i}$ denote the total mass of the system. Then the moments $\kMx$ and $\kMy$ of the system with respect to the x- and y-axes, respectively, are given by

$$\kMx=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\km_{i}y_{i}\;\text{and}\;\kMy=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\km_{i}x_{i}.\tag{6.16}$$

Also, the coordinates of the center of mass $(\kxbar,\kybar)$ of the system are

$$\kxbar=\frac{\kMy}{\km}\;\text{and}\;\kybar=\frac{\kMx}{\km}.\tag{6.17}$$

The next example demonstrates how to apply this theorem.

Example 6.30 · Finding the Center of Mass of Objects in a Plane

Suppose three point masses are placed in the xy-plane as follows (assume coordinates are given in meters):

$$\begin{gathered}\km_{1}=2\;\text{kg, placed at}\;(-1,3), \\ \km_{2}=6\;\text{kg, placed at}\;(1,1), \\ \km_{3}=4\;\text{kg, placed at}\;(2,-2).\end{gathered}$$

Find the center of mass of the system.

Solution

First we calculate the total mass of the system:

$$\km=\sum_{i=1}^{3} \km_{i}=2+6+4=12\;\text{kg}.$$

Next we find the moments with respect to the x- and y-axes:

$$\begin{gathered}\kMy=\sum_{i=1}^{3} \km_{i}x_{i}=-2+6+8=12, \\ \kMx=\sum_{i=1}^{3} \km_{i}y_{i}=6+6-8=4.\end{gathered}$$

Then we have

$$\kxbar=\frac{\kMy}{\km}=\frac{12}{12}=1\;\text{and}\;\kybar=\frac{\kMx}{\km}=\frac{4}{12}=\frac{1}{3}.$$

The center of mass of the system is $(1,1/3),$ in meters.

Center of Mass of Thin Plates

So far we have looked at systems of point masses on a line and in a plane. Now, instead of having the mass of a system concentrated at discrete points, we want to look at systems in which the mass of the system is distributed continuously across a thin sheet of material. For our purposes, we assume the sheet is thin enough that it can be treated as if it is two-dimensional. Such a sheet is called a lamina. Next we develop techniques to find the center of mass of a lamina. In this section, we also assume the density of the lamina is constant.

Laminas are often represented by a two-dimensional region in a plane. The geometric center of such a region is called its centroid. Since we have assumed the density of the lamina is constant, the center of mass of the lamina depends only on the shape of the corresponding region in the plane; it does not depend on the density. In this case, the center of mass of the lamina corresponds to the centroid of the delineated region in the plane. As with systems of point masses, we need to find the total mass of the lamina, as well as the moments of the lamina with respect to the x- and y-axes.

We first consider a lamina in the shape of a rectangle. Recall that the center of mass of a lamina is the point where the lamina balances. For a rectangle, that point is both the horizontal and vertical center of the rectangle. Based on this understanding, it is clear that the center of mass of a rectangular lamina is the point where the diagonals intersect, which is a result of the symmetry principle, and it is stated here without proof.

Theorem

The Symmetry Principle

If a region R is symmetric about a line l, then the centroid of R lies on l.

Let’s turn to more general laminas. Suppose we have a lamina bounded above by the graph of a continuous function $\kf(x),$ below by the x-axis, and on the left and right by the lines $x=a$ and $x=b,$ respectively, as shown in the following figure.

As with systems of point masses, to find the center of mass of the lamina, we need to find the total mass of the lamina, as well as the moments of the lamina with respect to the x- and y-axes. As we have done many times before, we approximate these quantities by partitioning the interval $[a,b]$ and constructing rectangles.

For $i=0,1,2\text{,\ldots},n,$ let $P=\{x_{i}\}$ be a regular partition of $[a,b].$ Recall that we can choose any point within the interval $[x_{i-1},x_{i}]$ as our $x_i^{*}.$ In this case, we want $x_i^{*}$ to be the x-coordinate of the centroid of our rectangles. Thus, for $i=1,2\text{,\ldots},n,$ we select $x_i^{*}\in [x_{i-1},x_{i}]$ such that $x_i^{*}$ is the midpoint of the interval. That is, $x_i^{*}=(x_{i-1}+x_{i})/2.$ Now, for $i=1,2\text{,\ldots},n,$ construct a rectangle of height $\kf(x_i^{*})$ on $[x_{i-1},x_{i}].$ The center of mass of this rectangle is $(x_i^{*},(\kf(x_i^{*}))/2),$ as shown in the following figure.

Next, we need to find the total mass of the rectangle. Let $\krho$ represent the density of the lamina (note that $\krho$ is a constant). In this case, $\krho$ is expressed in terms of mass per unit area. Thus, to find the total mass of the rectangle, we multiply the area of the rectangle by $\krho.$ Then, the mass of the rectangle is given by $\krho \kf(x_i^{*})\Delta x.$

Next, we calculate the moment of the lamina with respect to the x-axis. Returning to the representative rectangle, recall its center of mass is $(x_i^{*},(\kf(x_i^{*}))/2).$ Recall also that treating the rectangle as if it is a point mass located at the center of mass does not change the moment. Thus, the moment of the rectangle with respect to the x-axis is given by the mass of the rectangle, $\krho \kf(x_i^{*})\Delta x,$ multiplied by the distance from the center of mass to the x-axis: $(\kf(x_i^{*}))/2.$ Therefore, the moment with respect to the x-axis of the rectangle is $\krho({[\kf(x_i^{*})]}^{2}/2)\Delta x.$

We derive the moment with respect to the y-axis similarly, noting that the distance from the center of mass of the rectangle to the y-axis is $x_i^{*}.$

We find the coordinates of the center of mass by dividing the moments by the total mass to give $\kxbar=\kMy/\km\;\text{and}\;\kybar=\kMx/\km.$ If we look closely at the expressions for $\kMx,\kMy,\;\text{and}\;\km,$ we notice that the constant $\krho$ cancels out when $\kxbar$ and $\kybar$ are calculated.

We summarize these findings in the following theorem.

Theorem

Center of Mass of a Thin Plate in the xy-Plane

Let R denote a region bounded above by the graph of a continuous function $\kf(x),$ below by the x-axis, and on the left and right by the lines $x=a$ and $x=b,$ respectively. Let $\krho$ denote the density of the associated lamina. Then we can make the following statements:

  1. The mass of the lamina is $$\km=\krho\int _a^{b}\kf(x)dx.\tag{6.18}$$
  2. The moments $\kMx$ and $\kMy$ of the lamina with respect to the x- and y-axes, respectively, are $$\kMx=\krho\int_a^{b}\frac{{[\kf(x)]}^{2}}{2}dx\;\text{and}\;\kMy=\krho\int_a^{b}x\kf(x)dx.\tag{6.19}$$
  3. The coordinates of the center of mass $(\kxbar,\kybar)$ are $$\kxbar=\frac{\kMy}{\km}\;\text{and}\;\kybar=\frac{\kMx}{\km}.\tag{6.20}$$

In the next example, we use this theorem to find the center of mass of a lamina.

Example 6.31 · Finding the Center of Mass of a Lamina

Let R be the region bounded above by the graph of the function $\kf(x)=\sqrt{x}$ and below by the x-axis over the interval $[0,4].$ Find the centroid of the region.

Solution

The region is depicted in the following figure.

Since we are only asked for the centroid of the region, rather than the mass or moments of the associated lamina, we know the density constant $\krho$ cancels out of the calculations eventually. Therefore, for the sake of convenience, let’s assume $\krho=1.$

First, we need to calculate the total mass:

$$\begin{aligned}m &=\krho\int _a^{b}\kf(x)dx=\int_0^{4}\sqrt{x}\;dx \\ &={\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}|}_0^{4}=\frac{2}{3}[8-0]=\frac{16}{3}.\end{aligned}$$

Next, we compute the moments:

$$\begin{aligned}\kMx &=\krho\int_a^{b}\frac{{[\kf(x)]}^{2}}{2}dx \\ &=\int_0^{4}\frac{x}{2}dx={\frac{1}{4}x^{2}|}_0^{4}=4\end{aligned}$$

and

$$\begin{aligned}\kMy &=\krho\int_a^{b}x\kf(x)dx \\ &=\int_0^{4}x\sqrt{x}dx=\int_0^{4}x^{3/2}dx \\ &={\frac{2}{5}x^{5/2}|}_0^{4}=\frac{2}{5}[32-0]=\frac{64}{5}.\end{aligned}$$

Thus, we have

$$\kxbar=\frac{\kMy}{\km}=\frac{64/5}{16/3}=\frac{64}{5}\cdot \frac{3}{16}=\frac{12}{5}\;\text{and}\;\kybar=\frac{\kMx}{\km}=\frac{4}{16/3}=4\cdot \frac{3}{16}=\frac{3}{4}.$$

The centroid of the region is $(12/5,3/4).$

We can adapt this approach to find centroids of more complex regions as well. Suppose our region is bounded above by the graph of a continuous function $\kf(x),$ as before, but now, instead of having the lower bound for the region be the x-axis, suppose the region is bounded below by the graph of a second continuous function, $\kg(x),$ as shown in the following figure.

Again, we partition the interval $[a,b]$ and construct rectangles. A representative rectangle is shown in the following figure.

Note that the centroid of this rectangle is $(x_i^{*},(\kf(x_i^{*})+\kg(x_i^{*}))/2).$ We won’t go through all the details of the Riemann sum development, but let’s look at some of the key steps. In the development of the formulas for the mass of the lamina and the moment with respect to the y-axis, the height of each rectangle is given by $\kf(x_i^{*})-\kg(x_i^{*}),$ which leads to the expression $\kf(x)-\kg(x)$ in the integrands.

In the development of the formula for the moment with respect to the x-axis, the moment of each rectangle is found by multiplying the area of the rectangle, $\krho[\kf(x_i^{*})-\kg(x_i^{*})]\Delta x,$ by the distance of the centroid from the x-axis, $(\kf(x_i^{*})+\kg(x_i^{*}))/2,$ which gives $\krho(1/2)\{{[\kf(x_i^{*})]}^{2}-{[\kg(x_i^{*})]}^{2}\}\Delta x.$ Summarizing these findings, we arrive at the following theorem.

Theorem

Center of Mass of a Lamina Bounded by Two Functions

Let R denote a region bounded above by the graph of a continuous function $\kf(x),$ below by the graph of the continuous function $\kg(x),$ and on the left and right by the lines $x=a$ and $x=b,$ respectively. Let $\krho$ denote the density of the associated lamina. Then we can make the following statements:

  1. The mass of the lamina is $$\km=\krho\int _a^{b}[\kf(x)-\kg(x)]dx.\tag{6.21}$$
  2. The moments $\kMx$ and $\kMy$ of the lamina with respect to the x- and y-axes, respectively, are $$\kMx=\krho\int_a^{b}\frac{1}{2}({[\kf(x)]}^{2}-{[\kg(x)]}^{2})dx\;\text{and}\;\kMy=\krho\int_a^{b}x[\kf(x)-\kg(x)]dx.\tag{6.22}$$
  3. The coordinates of the center of mass $(\kxbar,\kybar)$ are $$\kxbar=\frac{\kMy}{\km}\;\text{and}\;\kybar=\frac{\kMx}{\km}.\tag{6.23}$$

We illustrate this theorem in the following example.

Example 6.32 · Finding the Centroid of a Region Bounded by Two Functions

Let R be the region bounded above by the graph of the function $\kf(x)=1-x^{2}$ and below by the graph of the function $\kg(x)=x-1.$ Find the centroid of the region.

Solution

The region is depicted in the following figure.

The graphs of the functions intersect at $(-2,-3)$ and $(1,0),$ so we integrate from −2 to 1. Once again, for the sake of convenience, assume $\krho=1.$

First, we need to calculate the total mass:

$$\begin{aligned}m &=\krho\int _a^{b}[\kf(x)-\kg(x)]dx \\ &=\int_{-2}^{1}[1-x^{2}-(x-1)]dx=\int _{-2}^{1}(2-x^{2}-x)dx \\ &={[2x-\frac{1}{3}x^{3}-\frac{1}{2}x^{2}]\;|}_{-2}^{1}=[2-\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{2}]-[-4+\frac{8}{3}-2]=\frac{9}{2}.\end{aligned}$$

Next, we compute the moments:

$$\begin{aligned}\kMx &=\krho\int_a^{b}\frac{1}{2}({[\kf(x)]}^{2}-{[\kg(x)]}^{2})dx \\ &=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-2}^{1}({(1-x^{2})}^{2}-{(x-1)}^{2})dx=\frac{1}{2}\int _{-2}^{1}(x^{4}-3x^{2}+2x)dx \\ &=\frac{1}{2}{[\frac{x^{5}}{5}-x^{3}+x^{2}]\;|}_{-2}^{1}=-\frac{27}{10}\end{aligned}$$

and

$$\begin{aligned}\kMy &=\krho\int_a^{b}x[\kf(x)-\kg(x)]dx \\ &=\int_{-2}^{1}x[(1-x^{2})-(x-1)]dx=\int_{-2}^{1}x[2-x^{2}-x]dx \\ =\int_{-2}^{1}(2x-x^{3}-x^{2})dx &\; \\ &={[x^{2}-\frac{x^{4}}{4}-\frac{x^{3}}{3}]\;|}_{-2}^{1}=-\frac{9}{4}.\end{aligned}$$

Therefore, we have

$$\kxbar=\frac{\kMy}{\km}=-\frac{9}{4}\cdot \frac{2}{9}=-\frac{1}{2}\;\text{and}\;\kybar=\frac{\kMx}{\km}=-\frac{27}{10}\cdot \frac{2}{9}=-\frac{3}{5}.$$

The centroid of the region is $(-(1/2),-(3/5)).$

The Symmetry Principle

We stated the symmetry principle earlier, when we were looking at the centroid of a rectangle. The symmetry principle can be a great help when finding centroids of regions that are symmetric. Consider the following example.

Example 6.33 · Finding the Centroid of a Symmetric Region

Let R be the region bounded above by the graph of the function $\kf(x)=4-x^{2}$ and below by the x-axis. Find the centroid of the region.

Solution

The region is depicted in the following figure.

The region is symmetric with respect to the y-axis. Therefore, the x-coordinate of the centroid is zero. We need only calculate $\kybar.$ Once again, for the sake of convenience, assume $\krho=1.$

First, we calculate the total mass:

$$\begin{aligned}m &=\krho\int _a^{b}\kf(x)dx \\ &=\int_{-2}^{2}(4-x^{2})dx \\ &={[4x-\frac{x^{3}}{3}]\;|}_{-2}^{2}=\frac{32}{3}.\end{aligned}$$

Next, we calculate the moments. We only need $\kMx\text{:}$

$$\begin{aligned}\kMx &=\krho\int_a^{b}\frac{{[\kf(x)]}^{2}}{2}dx \\ &=\frac{1}{2}\int _{-2}^{2}{[4-x^{2}]}^{2}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int _{-2}^{2}(16-8x^{2}+x^{4})dx \\ &=\frac{1}{2}{[\frac{x^{5}}{5}-\frac{8x^{3}}{3}+16x]\;|}_{-2}^{2}=\frac{256}{15}.\end{aligned}$$

Then we have

$$\kybar=\frac{\kMx}{\km}=\frac{256}{15}\cdot \frac{3}{32}=\frac{8}{5}.$$

The centroid of the region is $(0,8/5).$

Theorem of Pappus

This section ends with a discussion of the theorem of Pappus for volume, which allows us to find the volume of particular kinds of solids by using the centroid. (There is also a theorem of Pappus for surface area, but it is much less useful than the theorem for volume.)

Theorem

Theorem of Pappus for Volume

Let R be a region in the plane and let l be a line in the plane that does not intersect R. Then the volume of the solid of revolution formed by revolving R around l is equal to the area of R multiplied by the distance d traveled by the centroid of R.

Proof

We can prove the case when the region is bounded above by the graph of a function $\kf(x)$ and below by the graph of a function $\kg(x)$ over an interval $[a,b],$ and for which the axis of revolution is the y-axis. In this case, the area of the region is $\kA=\int _a^{b}[\kf(x)-\kg(x)]dx.$ Since the axis of rotation is the y-axis, the distance traveled by the centroid of the region depends only on the x-coordinate of the centroid, $\kxbar.$

□

Example 6.34 · Using the Theorem of Pappus for Volume

Let R be a circle of radius 2 centered at $(4,0).$ Use the theorem of Pappus for volume to find the volume of the torus generated by revolving R around the y-axis.

Solution

The region and torus are depicted in the following figure.

The region R is a circle of radius 2, so the area of R is $\kA=4\pi$ units2. By the symmetry principle, the centroid of R is the center of the circle. The centroid travels around the y-axis in a circular path of radius 4, so the centroid travels $d=8\pi$ units. Then, the volume of the torus is $A\cdot d=32\pi^{2}$ units3.
