In this section, we explore integrals containing expressions of the form $\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}},$ $\sqrt{a^{2}+x^{2}},$ and $\sqrt{x^{2}-a^{2}},$ where the values of $a$ are positive. We have already encountered and evaluated integrals containing some expressions of this type, but many still remain inaccessible. The technique of trigonometric substitution comes in very handy when evaluating these integrals. This technique uses substitution to rewrite these integrals as trigonometric integrals.

Integrals Involving $\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}}$

Before developing a general strategy for integrals containing $\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}},$ consider the integral $\int \sqrt{9-x^{2}} dx.$ This integral cannot be evaluated using any of the techniques we have discussed so far. However, if we make the substitution $x=3\;\sin \ktheta ,$ we have $dx=3\;\cos \ktheta d\theta .$ After substituting into the integral, we have

$$\int \sqrt{9-x^{2}}\;dx =\int \sqrt{9-{(3\;\sin \ktheta )}^{2}}3\;\cos \ktheta d\theta .$$

After simplifying, we have

$$\int \sqrt{9-x^{2}}\;dx=\int 9\sqrt{1-{\sin }^{2}\ktheta}\;\cos \ktheta d\theta .$$

Letting $1-{\sin }^{2}\ktheta ={\cos }^{2}\ktheta ,$ we now have

$$\int \sqrt{9-x^{2}}\;dx=\int 9\sqrt{{\cos }^{2}\ktheta}\;\cos \ktheta d\theta .$$

Assuming that $\cos \ktheta \ge 0,$ we have

$$\int \sqrt{9-x^{2}}\;dx=\int 9\;{\cos }^{2}\ktheta d\theta .$$

At this point, we can evaluate the integral using the techniques developed for integrating powers and products of trigonometric functions. Before completing this example, let’s take a look at the general theory behind this idea.

To evaluate integrals involving $\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}},$ we make the substitution $x=a\;\sin \ktheta$ and $dx=a\;\cos \ktheta .$ To see that this actually makes sense, consider the following argument: The domain of $\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}}$ is $[-a,a].$ Thus, $-a\le x\le a.$ Consequently, $-1\le \frac{x}{a}\le 1.$ Since the range of $\sin x$ over $[-(\pi /2),\pi /2]$ is $[-1,1],$ there is a unique angle $\ktheta$ satisfying $-(\pi /2)\le \ktheta \le \pi /2$ so that $\sin \ktheta =x/a,$ or equivalently, so that $x=a\;\sin \ktheta .$

We can see, from this discussion, that by making the substitution $x=a\;\sin \ktheta ,$ we are able to convert an integral involving a radical into an integral involving trigonometric functions. After we evaluate the integral, we can convert the solution back to an expression involving $x.$ To see how to do this, let’s begin by assuming that $0\lt x\lt a.$ In this case, $0\lt \ktheta \lt \frac{\pi}{2}.$ Since $\sin \ktheta =\frac{x}{a},$ we can draw the reference triangle in Figure 7.4 to assist in expressing the values of $\cos \ktheta ,$ $\tan \ktheta ,$ and the remaining trigonometric functions in terms of $x.$ It can be shown that this triangle actually produces the correct values of the trigonometric functions evaluated at $\ktheta$ for all $\ktheta$ satisfying $-\frac{\pi}{2}\le \ktheta \le \frac{\pi}{2}.$ It is useful to observe that the expression $\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}}$ actually appears as the length of one side of the triangle. Last, should $\ktheta$ appear by itself, we use $\ktheta ={\sin }^{-1}(\frac{x}{a}).$

The essential part of this discussion is summarized in the following problem-solving strategy.

Problem-Solving Strategy

Integrating Expressions Involving $\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}}$

  1. It is a good idea to make sure the integral cannot be evaluated easily in another way. For example, although this method can be applied to integrals of the form $\int \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}}}dx ,$ $\int \frac{x}{\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}}}dx ,$ and $\int x\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}}\;dx,$ they can each be integrated directly either by formula or by a simple u-substitution.
  2. Make the substitution $x=a\;\sin \ktheta$ and $dx=a\;\cos \ktheta d\theta .$ Note: This substitution yields $\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}}=a\;\cos \ktheta .$
  3. Simplify the expression.
  4. Evaluate the integral using techniques from the section on trigonometric integrals.
  5. Use the reference triangle from Figure 7.4 to rewrite the result in terms of $x.$ You may also need to use some trigonometric identities and the relationship $\ktheta ={\sin }^{-1}(\frac{x}{a}).$

The following example demonstrates the application of this problem-solving strategy.

Example 7.21 · Integrating an Expression Involving $\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}}$

Evaluate $\int \sqrt{9-x^{2}}\;dx.$

Solution

Begin by making the substitutions $x=3\;\sin \ktheta$ and $dx=3\;\cos \ktheta d\theta .$ Since $\sin \ktheta =\frac{x}{3},$ we can construct the reference triangle shown in the following figure.

Thus,

$$\begin{array}{rllll}\int \sqrt{9-x^{2}}\;dx & =\int \sqrt{9-{(3\;\sin \ktheta )}^{2}}3\;\cos \ktheta d\theta & & & \text{Substitute}\;x=3\;\sin \ktheta \;\text{and}\;dx=3\;\cos \ktheta d\theta . \\ & =\int \sqrt{9(1-{\sin }^{2}\ktheta )}3\;\cos \ktheta d\theta & & & \text{Simplify.} \\ & =\int \sqrt{9\;{\cos }^{2}\ktheta}3\;\cos \ktheta d\theta & & & \text{Substitute}\;{\cos }^{2}\ktheta =1-{\sin }^{2}\ktheta . \\ & =\int 3|\cos \ktheta |3\;\cos \ktheta d\theta & & & \text{Take the square root.} \\ & =\int 9\;{\cos }^{2}\ktheta d\theta & & & \begin{array}{l}\text{Simplify. Since}\;-\frac{\pi}{2}\le \ktheta \le \frac{\pi}{2},\;\cos \ktheta \ge 0\;\text{and} \\ |\cos \ktheta |=\cos \ktheta .\end{array} \\ & =\int 9(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\cos (2\ktheta ))d\theta & & & \begin{array}{l}\text{Use the strategy for integrating an even power} \\ \text{of}\;\cos \ktheta .\end{array} \\ & =\frac{9}{2}\ktheta +\frac{9}{4}\sin (2\ktheta )+C & & & \text{Evaluate the integral.} \\ & =\frac{9}{2}\ktheta +\frac{9}{4}(2\;\sin \ktheta \;\cos \ktheta )+C & & & \text{Substitute}\;\sin (2\ktheta )=2\;\sin \ktheta \;\cos \ktheta . \\ & =\frac{9}{2}{\sin }^{-1}(\frac{x}{3})+\frac{9}{2}\cdot \frac{x}{3}\cdot \frac{\sqrt{9-x^{2}}}{3}+C & & & \begin{array}{l}\text{Substitute}\;{\sin }^{-1}(\frac{x}{3})=\ktheta \;\text{and}\;\sin \ktheta =\frac{x}{3}.\;\text{Use} \\ \text{the reference triangle to see that} \\ \cos \ktheta =\frac{\sqrt{9-x^{2}}}{3}\;\text{and make this substitution.}\end{array} \\ & =\frac{9}{2}{\sin }^{-1}(\frac{x}{3})+\frac{x\sqrt{9-x^{2}}}{2}+C. & & & \text{Simplify.}\end{array}$$

Evaluate $\int \frac{\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}{x}dx.$

Solution

First make the substitutions $x=2\;\sin \ktheta$ and $dx=2\;\cos \ktheta d\theta .$ Since $\sin \ktheta =\frac{x}{2},$ we can construct the reference triangle shown in the following figure.

Thus,

$$\begin{array}{rllll}\int \frac{\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}{x}dx & =\int \frac{\sqrt{4-{(2\;\sin \ktheta )}^{2}}}{2\;\sin \ktheta}2\;\cos \ktheta d\theta & & & \text{Substitute}\;x=2\;\sin \ktheta \;\text{and}\;dx=2\;\cos \ktheta d\theta . \\ & =\int \frac{2\;{\cos }^{2}\ktheta}{\sin \ktheta}d\theta & & & \text{Substitute}\;{\cos }^{2}\ktheta =1-{\sin }^{2}\ktheta \;\text{and simplify.} \\ & =\int \frac{2(1-{\sin }^{2}\ktheta )}{\sin \ktheta}d\theta & & & \text{Substitute}\;{\sin }^{2}\ktheta =1-{\cos }^{2}\ktheta . \\ & =\int (2\;\csc \ktheta -2\;\sin \ktheta )d\theta & & & \begin{array}{l}\text{Separate the numerator, simplify, and use} \\ \csc \ktheta =\frac{1}{\sin \ktheta}.\end{array} \\ & =2\;\ln |\csc \ktheta -\cot \ktheta |+2\;\cos \ktheta +C & & & \text{Evaluate the integral.} \\ & =2\;\ln |\frac{2}{x}-\frac{\sqrt{4-x^{2}}}{x}|+\sqrt{4-x^{2}}+C. & & & \begin{array}{l}\text{Use the reference triangle to rewrite the} \\ \text{expression in terms of}\;x\;\text{and simplify.}\end{array}\end{array}$$

Example 7.23 · Integrating an Expression Involving $\sqrt{a^{2}-x^{2}}$ Two Ways

Evaluate $\int x^{3}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\;dx$ two ways: first by using the substitution $u=1-x^{2}$ and then by using a trigonometric substitution.

Solution

Method 1

Let $u=1-x^{2}$ and hence $x^{2}=1-u.$ Thus, $du=-2x\;dx.$ In this case, the integral becomes

$$\begin{array}{rllll}\int x^{3}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\;dx & =-\frac{1}{2}\int x^{2}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}(-2x\;dx) & & & \text{Make the substitution.} \\ & =-\frac{1}{2}\int (1-u)\sqrt{u}\;du & & & \text{Expand the expression.} \\ & =-\frac{1}{2}\int (u^{1/2}-u^{3/2})du & & & \text{Evaluate the integral.} \\ & =-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}-\frac{2}{5}u^{5/2})+C & & & \text{Rewrite in terms of}\;x. \\ & =-\frac{1}{3}{(1-x^{2})}^{3/2}+\frac{1}{5}{(1-x^{2})}^{5/2}+C. & & & \end{array}$$

Method 2

Let $x=\sin \ktheta .$ In this case, $dx=\cos \ktheta d\theta .$ Using this substitution, we have

$$\begin{array}{rllll}\int x^{3}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\;dx & =\int {\sin }^{3}\ktheta \;{\cos }^{2}\ktheta d\theta & & & \\ & =\int (1-{\cos }^{2}\ktheta ){\cos }^{2}\ktheta \;\sin \ktheta d\theta & & & \text{Let}\;u=\cos \ktheta .\;\text{Thus,}\;du=-\sin \ktheta d\theta . \\ & =\int (u^{4}-u^{2})du & & & \\ & =\frac{1}{5}u^{5}-\frac{1}{3}u^{3}+C & & & \text{Substitute}\;\cos \ktheta =u. \\ & =\frac{1}{5}{\cos }^{5}\ktheta -\frac{1}{3}{\cos }^{3}\ktheta +C & & & \begin{array}{l}\text{Use a reference triangle to see that} \\ \cos \ktheta =\sqrt{1-x^{2}}.\end{array} \\ & =\frac{1}{5}{(1-x^{2})}^{5/2}-\frac{1}{3}{(1-x^{2})}^{3/2}+C. & & & \end{array}$$

Example 7.24 · Integrating an Expression Involving $\sqrt{a^{2}+x^{2}}$

Evaluate $\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}$ and check the solution by differentiating.

Solution

Begin with the substitution $x=\tan \ktheta$ and $dx={\sec }^{2}\ktheta d\theta .$ Since $\tan \ktheta =x,$ draw the reference triangle in the following figure.

Thus,

$$\begin{array}{rllll}\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}} & =\int \frac{{\sec }^{2}\ktheta}{\sec \ktheta}d\theta & & & \begin{array}{l}\text{Substitute}\;x=\tan \ktheta \;\text{and}\;dx={\sec }^{2}\ktheta d\theta .\;\text{This} \\ \text{substitution makes}\;\sqrt{1+x^{2}}=\sec \ktheta .\;\text{Simplify.}\end{array} \\ & =\int \sec \ktheta d\theta & & & \text{Evaluate the integral.} \\ & =\ln |\sec \ktheta +\tan \ktheta |+C & & & \begin{array}{l}\text{Use the reference triangle to express the result} \\ \text{in terms of}\;x.\end{array} \\ & =\ln |\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x|+C. & & & \end{array}$$

To check the solution, differentiate:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\frac{d}{dx}(\ln |\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x|) & =\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}\cdot (\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}+1) \\ & =\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x}\cdot \frac{x+\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}} \\ & =\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}.\end{array}$$

Since $\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x\gt 0$ for all values of $x,$ we could rewrite $\ln |\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x|+C=\ln (\sqrt{1+x^{2}}+x)+C,$ if desired.

Example 7.25 · Evaluating $\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}$ Using a Different Substitution

Use the substitution $x=\operatorname{sinh}\;\ktheta$ to evaluate $\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}}.$

Solution

Because $\operatorname{sinh}\;\ktheta$ has a range of all real numbers, and $1+{\operatorname{sinh}}^{2}\ktheta ={\operatorname{cosh}}^{2}\ktheta ,$ we may also use the substitution $x=\operatorname{sinh}\;\ktheta$ to evaluate this integral. In this case, $dx=\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta d\theta .$ Consequently,

$$\begin{array}{rllll}\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}}} & =\int \frac{\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta}{\sqrt{1+{\operatorname{sinh}}^{2}\ktheta}}d\theta & & & \begin{array}{l}\text{Substitute}\;x=\operatorname{sinh}\;\ktheta \;\text{and}\;dx=\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta d\theta . \\ \text{Substitute}\;1+{\operatorname{sinh}}^{2}\ktheta ={\operatorname{cosh}}^{2}\ktheta .\end{array} \\ & =\int \frac{\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta}{\sqrt{{\operatorname{cosh}}^{2}\ktheta}}d\theta & & & \sqrt{{\operatorname{cosh}}^{2}\ktheta}=|\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta | \\ & =\int \frac{\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta}{|\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta |}d\theta & & & |\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta |=\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta \;\text{since}\;\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta \gt 0\;\text{for all}\;\ktheta . \\ & =\int \frac{\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta}{\operatorname{cosh}\;\ktheta}d\theta & & & \text{Simplify.} \\ & =\int 1d\theta & & & \text{Evaluate the integral.} \\ & =\ktheta +C & & & \text{Since}\;x=\operatorname{sinh}\;\ktheta ,\;\text{we know}\;\ktheta ={\operatorname{sinh}}^{-1}x. \\ & ={\operatorname{sinh}}^{-1}x+C. & & & \end{array}$$

Analysis

This answer looks quite different from the answer obtained using the substitution $x=\tan \ktheta .$ To see that the solutions are the same, set $y={\operatorname{sinh}}^{-1}x.$

We see that the two different methods produced equivalent solutions.

Example 7.26 · Finding an Arc Length

Find the length of the curve $y=x^{2}$ over the interval $[0,\frac{1}{2}].$

Solution

Because $\frac{dy}{dx}=2x,$ the arc length is given by

$$\int_0^{1/2}\sqrt{1+{(2x)}^{2}}\;dx =\int_0^{1/2}\sqrt{1+4x^{2}}\;dx .$$

To evaluate this integral, use the substitution $x=\frac{1}{2}\tan \ktheta$ and $dx=\frac{1}{2}{\sec }^{2}\ktheta d\theta .$ We also need to change the limits of integration. If $x=0,$ then $\ktheta =0$ and if $x=\frac{1}{2},$ then $\ktheta =\frac{\pi}{4}.$ Thus,

$$\begin{array}{rllll}\int_0^{1/2}\sqrt{1+4x^{2}}\;dx & =\int_0^{\pi /4}\sqrt{1+{\tan }^{2}\ktheta}\frac{1}{2}{\sec }^{2}\ktheta d\theta & & & \begin{array}{l}\text{After substitution,} \\ \sqrt{1+4x^{2}}=\tan \ktheta .\;\text{Substitute} \\ 1+{\tan }^{2}\ktheta ={\sec }^{2}\ktheta \;\text{and simplify.}\end{array} \\ & =\frac{1}{2}\int_0^{\pi /4}{\sec }^{3}\ktheta d\theta & & & \begin{array}{l}\text{We derived this integral in the} \\ \text{previous section.}\end{array} \\ & =\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}\sec \ktheta \;\tan \ktheta +\frac{1}{2}\ln |\sec \ktheta +\tan \ktheta |)|{}_{\begin{array}{l} \\ 0\end{array}}^{\begin{array}{l}\pi /4 \\ \end{array}} & & & \text{Evaluate and simplify.} \\ & =\frac{1}{4}(\sqrt{2}+\ln (\sqrt{2}+1)). & & & \end{array}$$
