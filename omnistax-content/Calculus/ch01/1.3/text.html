Trigonometric functions are used to model many phenomena, including sound waves, vibrations of strings, alternating electrical current, and the motion of pendulums. In fact, almost any repetitive, or cyclical, motion can be modeled by some combination of trigonometric functions. In this section, we define the six basic trigonometric functions and look at some of the main identities involving these functions.

Radian Measure

To use trigonometric functions, we first must understand how to measure the angles. Although we can use both radians and degrees, radians are a more natural measurement because they are related directly to the unit circle, a circle with radius 1. The radian measure of an angle is defined as follows. Given an angle $\ktheta,$ let $\ks$ be the length of the corresponding arc on the unit circle (Figure 1.30). We say the angle corresponding to the arc of length 1 has radian measure 1.

Since an angle of $360^{\circ}$ corresponds to the circumference of a circle, or an arc of length $2\pi,$ we conclude that an angle with a degree measure of $360^{\circ}$ has a radian measure of $2\pi.$ Similarly, we see that $180^{\circ}$ is equivalent to $\pi$ radians. Example 1.22 · Converting between Radians and Degrees

  1. Express $225^{\circ}$ using radians.
  2. Express $5\pi/3$ rad using degrees.

Solution

Use the fact that $180^{\circ}$ is equivalent to $\pi$ radians as a conversion factor: $1=\dfrac{\pi\;\text{rad}}{180^{\circ}}=\dfrac{180^{\circ}}{\pi\;\text{rad}}.$

  1. $225^{\circ}=225^{\circ}\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{180^{\circ}}=\dfrac{5\pi}{4}$ rad
  2. $\dfrac{5\pi}{3}$ rad $=\dfrac{5\pi}{3}\cdot\dfrac{180^{\circ}}{\pi}=300^{\circ}$

The Six Basic Trigonometric Functions

Trigonometric functions allow us to use angle measures, in radians or degrees, to find the coordinates of a point on any circle—not only on a unit circle—or to find an angle given a point on a circle. They also define the relationship among the sides and angles of a triangle.

To define the trigonometric functions, first consider the unit circle centered at the origin and a point $P=(x,y)$ on the unit circle. Let $\ktheta$ be an angle with an initial side that lies along the positive $x$-axis and with a terminal side that is the line segment $OP.$ An angle in this position is said to be in standard position (Figure 1.31). We can then define the values of the six trigonometric functions for $\ktheta$ in terms of the coordinates $x$ and $y.$

Definition

Let $P=(x,y)$ be a point on the unit circle centered at the origin $O.$ Let $\ktheta$ be an angle with an initial side along the positive $x$-axis and a terminal side given by the line segment $OP.$ The trigonometric functions are then defined as

$$\begin{array}{llll}\sin\ktheta=y & & & \csc\ktheta=\dfrac{1}{y}\\ \cos\ktheta=x & & & \sec\ktheta=\dfrac{1}{x}\\ \tan\ktheta=\dfrac{y}{x} & & & \cot\ktheta=\dfrac{x}{y}\end{array}\tag{1.9}$$

If $x=0,\ \sec\ktheta$ and $\tan\ktheta$ are undefined. If $y=0,$ then $\cot\ktheta$ and $\csc\ktheta$ are undefined.

We can see that for a point $P=(x,y)$ on a circle of radius $r$ with a corresponding angle $\ktheta,$ the coordinates $x$ and $y$ satisfy

$$\begin{aligned}\cos\ktheta&=\frac{x}{r}\\ x&=r\cos\ktheta\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}\sin\ktheta&=\frac{y}{r}\\ y&=r\sin\ktheta.\end{aligned}$$

The values of the other trigonometric functions can be expressed in terms of $x,y,$ and $r$ (Figure 1.32).

Example 1.23 · Evaluating Trigonometric Functions

Evaluate each of the following expressions.

  1. $\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right)$
  2. $\cos\left(-\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right)$
  3. $\tan\left(\dfrac{15\pi}{4}\right)$

Solution

  1. On the unit circle, the angle $\ktheta=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}$ corresponds to the point $\left(-\dfrac{1}{2},\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$. Therefore, $\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right)=y=\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}.$
  2. An angle $\ktheta=-\dfrac{5\pi}{6}$ corresponds to a revolution in the negative direction, as shown. Therefore, $\cos\left(-\dfrac{5\pi}{6}\right)=x=-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}.$
  3. An angle $\ktheta=\dfrac{15\pi}{4}=2\pi+\dfrac{7\pi}{4}.$ Therefore, this angle corresponds to more than one revolution, as shown. Knowing the fact that an angle of $\dfrac{7\pi}{4}$ corresponds to the point $\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2},-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right)$, we can conclude that $\tan\left(\dfrac{15\pi}{4}\right)=\dfrac{y}{x}=-1.$

As mentioned earlier, the ratios of the side lengths of a right triangle can be expressed in terms of the trigonometric functions evaluated at either of the acute angles of the triangle. Let $\ktheta$ be one of the acute angles. Let $A$ be the length of the adjacent leg, $O$ be the length of the opposite leg, and $H$ be the length of the hypotenuse. By inscribing the triangle into a circle of radius $H,$ as shown in Figure 1.33, we see that $A,H,$ and $O$ satisfy the following relationships with $\ktheta$:

$$\begin{array}{llll}\sin\ktheta=\dfrac{O}{H} & & & \csc\ktheta=\dfrac{H}{O}\\ \cos\ktheta=\dfrac{A}{H} & & & \sec\ktheta=\dfrac{H}{A}\\ \tan\ktheta=\dfrac{O}{A} & & & \cot\ktheta=\dfrac{A}{O}\end{array}$$

Example 1.24 · Constructing a Wooden Ramp

A wooden ramp is to be built with one end on the ground and the other end at the top of a short staircase. If the top of the staircase is $4$ ft from the ground and the angle between the ground and the ramp is to be $10^{\circ},$ how long does the ramp need to be?

Solution

Let $x$ denote the length of the ramp. In the following image, we see that $x$ needs to satisfy the equation $\sin(10^{\circ})=4/x.$ Solving this equation for $x,$ we see that $x=4/\sin(10^{\circ})\approx 23.035$ ft.

Trigonometric Identities

A trigonometric identity is an equation involving trigonometric functions that is true for all angles $\ktheta$ for which the functions are defined. We can use the identities to help us solve or simplify equations. The main trigonometric identities are listed next.

Rule

Trigonometric Identities

Reciprocal identities

$$\begin{array}{llll}\cot\ktheta=\dfrac{1}{\tan\ktheta} & & & \\ \csc\ktheta=\dfrac{1}{\sin\ktheta} & & & \sec\ktheta=\dfrac{1}{\cos\ktheta}\end{array}$$

Ratio identities

$$\begin{array}{llll}\tan\ktheta=\dfrac{\sin\ktheta}{\cos\ktheta} & & & \cot\ktheta=\dfrac{\cos\ktheta}{\sin\ktheta}\end{array}$$

Pythagorean identities

$$\sin^{2}\ktheta+\cos^{2}\ktheta=1\hspace{2em}1+\tan^{2}\ktheta=\sec^{2}\ktheta\hspace{2em}1+\cot^{2}\ktheta=\csc^{2}\ktheta$$

Addition and subtraction formulas

$$\sin(\alpha\pm\beta)=\sin\alpha\cos\beta\pm\cos\alpha\sin\beta$$ $$\cos(\alpha\pm\beta)=\cos\alpha\cos\beta\mp\sin\alpha\sin\beta$$

Double-angle formulas

$$\sin(2\ktheta)=2\sin\ktheta\cos\ktheta$$ $$\cos(2\ktheta)=2\cos^{2}\ktheta-1=1-2\sin^{2}\ktheta=\cos^{2}\ktheta-\sin^{2}\ktheta$$

Example 1.25 · Solving Trigonometric Equations

For each of the following equations, use a trigonometric identity to find all solutions.

  1. $1+\cos(2\ktheta)=\cos\ktheta$
  2. $\sin(2\ktheta)=\tan\ktheta$

Solution

a. Using the double-angle formula for $\cos(2\ktheta),$ we see that $\ktheta$ is a solution of

$$1+\cos(2\ktheta)=\cos\ktheta$$

if and only if

$$1+2\cos^{2}\ktheta-1=\cos\ktheta,$$

which is true if and only if

$$2\cos^{2}\ktheta-\cos\ktheta=0.$$

To solve this equation, it is important to note that we need to factor the left-hand side and not divide both sides of the equation by $\cos\ktheta.$ The problem with dividing by $\cos\ktheta$ is that it is possible that $\cos\ktheta$ is zero. In fact, if we did divide both sides of the equation by $\cos\ktheta,$ we would miss some of the solutions of the original equation. Factoring the left-hand side of the equation, we see that $\ktheta$ is a solution of this equation if and only if

$$\cos\ktheta(2\cos\ktheta-1)=0.$$

Since $\cos\ktheta=0$ when

$$\ktheta=\frac{\pi}{2},\ \frac{\pi}{2}\pm\pi,\ \frac{\pi}{2}\pm 2\pi,\ldots,$$

and $\cos\ktheta=1/2$ when

$$\ktheta=\frac{\pi}{3},\ \frac{\pi}{3}\pm 2\pi,\ldots\;\text{or}\;\ktheta=-\frac{\pi}{3},\ -\frac{\pi}{3}\pm 2\pi,\ldots,$$

we conclude that the set of solutions to this equation is

$$\ktheta=\frac{\pi}{2}+n\pi,\quad \ktheta=\frac{\pi}{3}+2n\pi,\;\text{and}\;\ktheta=-\frac{\pi}{3}+2n\pi,\quad n=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots.$$

b. Using the double-angle formula for $\sin(2\ktheta)$ and the ratio identity for $\tan(\ktheta),$ the equation can be written as

$$2\sin\ktheta\cos\ktheta=\frac{\sin\ktheta}{\cos\ktheta}.$$

To solve this equation, we multiply both sides by $\cos\ktheta$ to eliminate the denominator, and say that if $\ktheta$ satisfies this equation, then $\ktheta$ satisfies the equation

$$2\sin\ktheta\cos^{2}\ktheta-\sin\ktheta=0.$$

However, we need to be a little careful here. Even if $\ktheta$ satisfies this new equation, it may not satisfy the original equation because, to satisfy the original equation, we would need to be able to divide both sides of the equation by $\cos\ktheta.$ However, if $\cos\ktheta=0,$ we cannot divide both sides of the equation by $\cos\ktheta.$ Therefore, it is possible that we may arrive at extraneous solutions. So, at the end, it is important to check for extraneous solutions. Returning to the equation, it is important that we factor $\sin\ktheta$ out of both terms on the left-hand side instead of dividing both sides of the equation by $\sin\ktheta.$ Factoring the left-hand side of the equation, we can rewrite this equation as

$$\sin\ktheta(2\cos^{2}\ktheta-1)=0.$$

Therefore, the solutions are given by the angles $\ktheta$ such that $\sin\ktheta=0$ or $\cos^{2}\ktheta=1/2.$ The solutions of the first equation are $\ktheta=0,\pm\pi,\pm 2\pi,\ldots.$ The solutions of the second equation are $\ktheta=\pi/4,\ (\pi/4)\pm(\pi/2),\ (\pi/4)\pm\pi,\ldots.$ After checking for extraneous solutions, the set of solutions to the equation is

$$\ktheta=n\pi\;\text{and}\;\ktheta=\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{n\pi}{2},\quad n=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,\ldots.$$

Graphs and Periods of the Trigonometric Functions

We have seen that as we travel around the unit circle, the values of the trigonometric functions repeat. We can see this pattern in the graphs of the functions. Let $P=(x,y)$ be a point on the unit circle and let $\ktheta$ be the corresponding angle. Since the angle $\ktheta$ and $\ktheta+2\pi$ correspond to the same point $P,$ the values of the trigonometric functions at $\ktheta$ and at $\ktheta+2\pi$ are the same. Consequently, the trigonometric functions are periodic functions. The period of a function $\kf$ is defined to be the smallest positive value $p$ such that $\kf(x+p)=\kf(x)$ for all values $x$ in the domain of $\kf.$ The sine, cosine, secant, and cosecant functions have a period of $2\pi.$ Since the tangent and cotangent functions repeat on an interval of length $\pi,$ their period is $\pi$ (Figure 1.34).

Just as with algebraic functions, we can apply transformations to trigonometric functions. In particular, consider the following function:

$$\kf(x)=\kA\cos(\kB(x-\alpha))+C.\tag{1.10}$$

In Figure 1.35, the constant $\alpha$ causes a horizontal or phase shift. The factor $\kB$ changes the period. This transformed sine function will have a period $2\pi/|\kB|.$ The factor $\kA$ results in a vertical stretch by a factor of $|\kA|.$ We say $|\kA|$ is the “amplitude of $\kf.$” The constant $C$ causes a vertical shift.

Notice in Figure 1.34 that the graph of $y=\cos x$ is the graph of $y=\sin x$ shifted to the left $\pi/2$ units. Therefore, we can write $\cos x=\sin(x+\pi/2).$ Similarly, we can view the graph of $y=\sin x$ as the graph of $y=\cos x$ shifted right $\pi/2$ units, and state that $\sin x=\cos(x-\pi/2).$

A shifted sine curve arises naturally when graphing the number of hours of daylight in a given location as a function of the day of the year. For example, suppose a city reports that June 21 is the longest day of the year with $15.7$ hours and December 21 is the shortest day of the year with $8.3$ hours. It can be shown that the function

$$\kh(t)=3.7\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{365}(t-80.5)\right)+12$$

is a model for the number of hours of daylight $\kh$ as a function of day of the year $t$ (Figure 1.36).

Example 1.27 · Sketching the Graph of a Transformed Sine Curve

Sketch a graph of $\kf(x)=3\sin\left(2\left(x-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\right)+1.$

Solution

This graph is a horizontal compression by a factor of 2, a phase shift to the right by π/4 units, followed by a vertical stretch by a factor of 3, and then a vertical shift by 1 unit. The period of $\kf$ is $\pi.$
