# Calculus of Parametric Curves

## Learning Objectives {section:learning-objectives}
- Determine derivatives and equations of tangents for parametric curves.
- Find the area under a parametric curve.
- Use the equation for arc length of a parametric curve.
- Apply the formula for surface area to a volume generated by a parametric curve.

Now that we have introduced the concept of a parameterized curve, our next step is to learn how to work with this concept in the context of calculus. For example, if we know a parameterization of a given curve, is it possible to calculate the slope of a tangent line to the curve? How about the arc length of the curve? Or the area under the curve?
Another scenario: Suppose we would like to represent the location of a baseball after the ball leaves a pitcher’s hand. If the position of the baseball is represented by the plane curve $(x(t),y(t)),$ then we should be able to use calculus to find the speed of the ball at any given time. Furthermore, we should be able to calculate just how far that ball has traveled as a function of time.

## Derivatives of Parametric Equations
We start by asking how to calculate the slope of a line tangent to a parametric curve at a point. Consider the plane curve defined by the parametric equations

$x(t)=2t+3,\;y(t)=3t-4,\;−2\le t\le 3.$  {eq:fs-id1167793790815}

The graph of this curve appears in [ref:CNX_Calc_Figure_11_02_001]. It is a line segment starting at $(−1,−10)$ and ending at $(9,5).$

> FIGURE {fig:CNX_Calc_Figure_11_02_001} src=../../media/CNX_Calc_Figure_11_02_001.jpg
> alt: A straight line from (−1, −10) to (9, 5). The point (−1, −10) is marked t = −2, the point (3, −4) is marked t = 0, and the point (9, 5) is marked t = 3. There are three equations marked: x(t) = 2t + 3, y(t) = 3t – 4, and −2 ≤ t ≤ 3
> caption: Graph of the line segment described by the given parametric equations.

We can eliminate the parameter by first solving the equation $x(t)=2t+3$ for *t*:

$\begin{array}{rll}x(t) & = & 2t+3 \\ x-3 & = & 2t \\ t & = & \frac{x-3}{2}.\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167793820750}

Substituting this into $y(t),$ we obtain

$\begin{array}{rll}y(t) & = & 3t-4 \\ y & = & 3(\frac{x-3}{2})-4 \\ y & = & \frac{3x}{2}-\frac{9}{2}-4 \\ y & = & \frac{3x}{2}-\frac{17}{2}.\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167793783749}

The slope of this line is given by $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{3}{2}.$ Next we calculate ${x}^{'}(t)$ and ${y}^{'}(t).$ This gives ${x}^{'}(t)=2$ and ${y}^{'}(t)=3.$ Notice that $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy\text{/}dt}{dx\text{/}dt}=\frac{3}{2}.$ This is no coincidence, as outlined in the following theorem.

:::note [theorem] Derivative of Parametric Equations
Consider the plane curve defined by the parametric equations $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t).$ Suppose that ${x}^{'}(t)$ and ${y}^{'}(t)$ exist, and assume that ${x}^{'}(t)≠0.$ Then the derivative $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is given by

$$ \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy\text{/}dt}{dx\text{/}dt}=\frac{{y}^{'}(t)}{{x}^{'}(t)}. $$  {eq:fs-id1167793881791}

:::

### Proof
This theorem can be proven using the Chain Rule. In particular, assume that the parameter *t* can be eliminated, yielding a differentiable function $y=F(x).$ Then $y(t)=F(x(t)).$ Differentiating both sides of this equation using the Chain Rule yields

${y}^{'}(t)={F}^{'}(x(t)){x}^{'}(t),$  {eq:fs-id1167794100709}

so

${F}^{'}(x(t))=\frac{{y}^{'}(t)}{{x}^{'}(t)}.$  {eq:fs-id1167793905994}

But ${F}^{'}(x(t))=\frac{dy}{dx},$ which proves the theorem.
□
[ref:fs-id1167793881791] can be used to calculate derivatives of plane curves, as well as critical points. Recall that a critical point of a differentiable function $y=f(x)$ is any point $x={x}_{0}$ such that either ${f}^{'}({x}_{0})=0$ or ${f}^{'}({x}_{0})$ does not exist. [ref:fs-id1167793881791] gives a formula for the slope of a tangent line to a curve defined parametrically regardless of whether the curve can be described by a function $y=f(x)$ or not.

## Second-Order Derivatives
Our next goal is to see how to take the second derivative of a function defined parametrically. The second derivative of a function $y=f(x)$ is defined to be the derivative of the first derivative; that is,

$\frac{{d}^{2}y}{d{x}^{2}}=\frac{d}{dx}[\frac{dy}{dx}].$  {eq:fs-id1167794032334}

Since $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy\text{/}dt}{dx\text{/}dt},$ we can replace the $y$ on both sides of this equation with $\frac{dy}{dx}.$ This gives us

$$ \frac{{d}^{2}y}{d{x}^{2}}=\frac{d}{dx}(\frac{dy}{dx})=\frac{(d\text{/}dt)(dy\text{/}dx)}{dx\text{/}dt}. $$  {eq:fs-id1167794072159}

If we know $dy\text{/}dx$ as a function of *t,* then this formula is straightforward to apply.

:::example {ex:fs-id1167794044408} Finding a Second Derivative

:::exercise {fs-id1167794044410} type= 
PROBLEM:
Calculate the second derivative ${d}^{2}y\text{/}d{x}^{2}$ for the plane curve defined by the parametric equations $x(t)={t}^{2}-3,y(t)=2t-1,−3\le t\le 4.$
SOLUTION:
From [ref:fs-id1167793984213] we know that $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{2}{2t}=\frac{1}{t}.$ Using [ref:fs-id1167794072159], we obtain

$\frac{{d}^{2}y}{d{x}^{2}}=\frac{(d\text{/}dt)(dy\text{/}dx)}{dx\text{/}dt}=\frac{(d\text{/}dt)(1\text{/}t)}{2t}=\frac{\text{−}{t}^{−2}}{2t}=-\frac{1}{2{t}^{3}}.$  {eq:fs-id1167794063436}

:::

## Integrals Involving Parametric Equations
Now that we have seen how to calculate the derivative of a plane curve, the next question is this: How do we find the area under a curve defined parametrically? Recall the cycloid defined by the equations $x(t)=t-\text{sin}\;t,\;y(t)=1-\text{cos}\;t.$ Suppose we want to find the area of the shaded region in the following graph.

> FIGURE {fig:CNX_Calc_Figure_11_02_007} src=../../media/CNX_Calc_Figure_11_02_007.jpg
> alt: A series of half circles drawn above the x axis with x intercepts being multiples of 2π. The half circle between 0 and 2π is highlighted. On the graph there are also written two equations: x(t) = t – sin(t) and y(t) = 1 – cos(t).
> caption: Graph of a cycloid with the arch over $[0,2\pi ]$ highlighted.

To derive a formula for the area under the curve defined by the functions

$x=x(t),\;y=y(t),\;a\le t\le b,$  {eq:fs-id1167794030648}

we assume that $x(t)$ is increasing on the interval $t\in [a,b]$ and $x(t)$ is differentiable and start with an equal partition of the interval $a\le t\le b.$ Suppose ${t}_{0}=a<{t}_{1}<{t}_{2}<\text{⋯}<{t}_{n}=b$ and consider the following graph.

> FIGURE {fig:CNX_Calc_Figure_11_02_008} src=../../media/CNX_Calc_Figure_11_02_012.jpg
> alt: A curved line is drawn in the first quadrant. Below it are a series of rectangles marked that begin at the x axis and reach up to the curved line; the rectangle’s height is determined by the location of the curved line at the leftmost point of the rectangle. These lines are noted as x(t0), x(t1), …, x(tn).
> caption: Approximating the area under a parametrically defined curve.

We use rectangles to approximate the area under the curve. The height of the $i$th rectangle is $y({t}_{i-1})$, so an approximation to the area is

$\begin{array}{l}\begin{array}{ll} & \sum_{i=1}^{n} y({t}_{i-1})(x({t}_{i})-x({t}_{i-1})) \\ = & \sum_{i=1}^{n} y({t}_{i-1})\frac{(x({t}_{i})-x({t}_{i-1}))}{({t}_{i}-{t}_{i-1})}({t}_{i}-{t}_{i-1}) \\ & \to \int_{a}^{b}y(t)x'(t)dt\text{as max}\{({t}_{i}-{t}_{i-1})\}\to 0\end{array}\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167794065566}

This follows from results obtained in Calculus 1 for the function $y({t}_{i-1})\frac{(x({t}_{i})-x({t}_{i-1}))}{({t}_{i}-{t}_{i-1})}.$
Then a Riemann sum for the area is

${A}_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n} y(\bar{{t}_{i}})\;(x({t}_{i})-x({t}_{i-1})).$  {eq:fs-id1167794065654}

Multiplying and dividing each area by ${t}_{i}-{t}_{i-1}$ gives

${A}_{n}={\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{n} y(x({\bar{t}}_{i}))\;(\frac{x({t}_{i})-x({t}_{i-1})}{{t}_{i}-{t}_{i-1}})}({t}_{i}-{t}_{i-1})={\displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^{n} y(x({\bar{t}}_{i}))\;(\frac{x({t}_{i})-x({t}_{i-1})}{\text{Δ}t})}\text{Δ}t.$  {eq:fs-id1167794064704}

Taking the limit as $n$ approaches infinity gives

$A=\lim_{n\to \infty}{A}_{n}={\displaystyle \int_{a}^{b}y(t){x}^{'}(t)\;dt}.$  {eq:fs-id1167794000283}

If $x$ is a decreasing function for $a\le t\le b$, a similar derivation will show that the area is given by $\begin{array}{ll}-\int_{a}^{b}y(t)x'(t)dt & =\end{array}\int_{a}^{b}y(t)x'(t)dt$
This leads to the following theorem.

:::note [theorem] Area under a Parametric Curve
Consider the non-self-intersecting plane curve defined by the parametric equations

$x=x(t),\;y=y(t),\;a\le t\le b$  {eq:fs-id1167794038325}

and assume that $x(t)$ is differentiable. The area under this curve is given by

$$ A={\displaystyle \int_{a}^{b}y(t){x}^{'}(t)\;dt}. $$  {eq:fs-id1167794038394}

:::

:::example {ex:fs-id1167794038444} Finding the Area under a Parametric Curve

:::exercise {fs-id1167794038447} type= 
PROBLEM:
Find the area under the curve of the cycloid defined by the equations

$x(t)=t-\text{sin}\;t,\;y(t)=1-\text{cos}\;t,\;0\le t\le 2\pi .$  {eq:fs-id1167794038457}

SOLUTION:
Using [ref:fs-id1167794038394], we have

$\begin{array}{rl}A & ={\displaystyle \int_{a}^{b}y(t){x}^{'}(t)\;dt} \\ & ={\displaystyle \int_{0}^{2\pi}(1-\text{cos}\;t)(1-\text{cos}\;t)\;dt} \\ & ={\displaystyle \int_{0}^{2\pi}(1-2\;\text{cos}\;t+{\text{cos}}^{2}t)dt} \\ & ={\displaystyle \int_{0}^{2\pi}(1-2\;\text{cos}\;t+\frac{1+\text{cos}\;2t}{2})\;dt} \\ & ={\displaystyle \int_{0}^{2\pi}(\frac{3}{2}-2\;\text{cos}\;t+\frac{\text{cos}\;2t}{2})\;dt} \\ & ={\frac{3t}{2}-2\;\text{sin}\;t+\frac{\text{sin}\;2t}{4}|}_{0}^{2\pi} \\ & =3\pi .\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167794070833}

:::

## Arc Length of a Parametric Curve
In addition to finding the area under a parametric curve, we sometimes need to find the arc length of a parametric curve. In the case of a line segment, arc length is the same as the distance between the endpoints. If a particle travels from point *A* to point *B* along a curve, then the distance that particle travels is the arc length. To develop a formula for arc length, we start with an approximation by line segments as shown in the following graph.

> FIGURE {fig:CNX_Calc_Figure_11_02_009} src=../../media/CNX_Calc_Figure_11_02_008.jpg
> alt: A curved line in the first quadrant with points marked for x = 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. These points have values roughly 2.1, 2.7, 3, 2.7, and 2.1, respectively. The points for x = 1 and 5 are marked A and B, respectively.
> caption: Approximation of a curve by line segments.

Given a plane curve defined by the functions $x=x(t),y=y(t),a\le t\le b,$ we start by partitioning the interval $[a,b]$ into *n* equal subintervals: ${t}_{0}=a<{t}_{1}<{t}_{2}<\text{⋯}<{t}_{n}=b.$ The width of each subinterval is given by $\text{Δ}t=(b-a)\text{/}n.$ We can calculate the length of each line segment:

$\begin{array}{l} \\ {d}_{1}=\sqrt{{(x({t}_{1})-x({t}_{0}))}^{2}+{(y({t}_{1})-y({t}_{0}))}^{2}} \\ {d}_{2}=\sqrt{{(x({t}_{2})-x({t}_{1}))}^{2}+{(y({t}_{2})-y({t}_{1}))}^{2}}\;\text{etc}.\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167794025695}

Then add these up. We let *s* denote the exact arc length and ${s}_{n}$ denote the approximation by *n* line segments:

$$ s\approx {\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{n} {s}_{k}}={\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{n} \sqrt{{(x({t}_{k})-x({t}_{k-1}))}^{2}+{(y({t}_{k})-y({t}_{k-1}))}^{2}}}. $$  {eq:fs-id1167794027155}

If we assume that $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ are differentiable functions of *t,* then the Mean Value Theorem ([Introduction to the Applications of Derivatives](module:m53602)) applies, so in each subinterval $[{t}_{k-1},{t}_{k}]$ there exist ${\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k}$ and ${\tilde{t}}_{k}$ such that

$\begin{array}{l} \\ x({t}_{k})-x({t}_{k-1})={x}^{'}({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k})({t}_{k}-{t}_{k-1})={x}^{'}({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k})\text{Δ}t \\ y({t}_{k})-y({t}_{k-1})={y}^{'}({\tilde{t}}_{k})({t}_{k}-{t}_{k-1})={y}^{'}({\tilde{t}}_{k})\text{Δ}t.\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167793970821}

Therefore [ref:fs-id1167794027155] becomes

$\begin{array}{rl}s & \approx {\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{n} {s}_{k}} \\ & ={\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{n} \sqrt{{({x}^{'}({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k})\text{Δ}t)}^{2}+{({y}^{'}({\tilde{t}}_{k})\text{Δ}t)}^{2}}} \\ & ={\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{n} \sqrt{{({x}^{'}({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k}))}^{2}{(\text{Δ}t)}^{2}+{({y}^{'}({\tilde{t}}_{k}))}^{2}{(\text{Δ}t)}^{2}}} \\ & =({\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{n} \sqrt{{({x}^{'}({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k}))}^{2}+{({y}^{'}({\tilde{t}}_{k}))}^{2}}})\text{Δ}t.\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167793926605}

This is a Riemann sum that approximates the arc length over a partition of the interval $[a,b].$ If we further assume that the derivatives are continuous and let the number of points in the partition increase without bound, the approximation approaches the exact arc length. This gives

$\begin{array}{rl}s & =\lim_{n\to \infty}{\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{n} {s}_{k}} \\ & =\lim_{n\to \infty}({\displaystyle \sum_{k=1}^{n} \sqrt{{({x}^{'}({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k}))}^{2}+{({y}^{'}({\tilde{t}}_{k}))}^{2}}})\text{Δ}t \\ & ={\displaystyle \int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{{({x}^{'}(t))}^{2}+{({y}^{'}(t))}^{2}}dt}.\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167793424451}

When taking the limit, the values of ${\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k}$ and ${\tilde{t}}_{k}$ are both contained within the same ever-shrinking interval of width $\text{Δ}t,$ so they must converge to the same value.
We can summarize this method in the following theorem.

:::note [theorem] Arc Length of a Parametric Curve
Consider the plane curve defined by the parametric equations

$x=x(t),\;y=y(t),\;{t}_{1}\le t\le {t}_{2}$  {eq:fs-id1167794065188}

and assume that $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ are differentiable functions of *t.* Then the arc length of this curve is given by

$$ s={\displaystyle \int_{{t}_{1}}^{{t}_{2}}\sqrt{{(\frac{dx}{dt})}^{2}+{(\frac{dy}{dt})}^{2}}dt}. $$  {eq:fs-id1167794023197}

:::
At this point a side derivation leads to a previous formula for arc length. In particular, suppose the parameter can be eliminated, leading to a function $y=F(x).$ Then $y(t)=F(x(t))$ and the Chain Rule gives ${y}^{'}(t)={F}^{'}(x(t)){x}^{'}(t).$ Substituting this into [ref:fs-id1167794023197] gives

$\begin{array}{rl}s & ={\displaystyle \int_{{t}_{1}}^{{t}_{2}}\sqrt{{(\frac{dx}{dt})}^{2}+{(\frac{dy}{dt})}^{2}}dt} \\ & ={\displaystyle \int_{{t}_{1}}^{{t}_{2}}\sqrt{{(\frac{dx}{dt})}^{2}+{({F}^{'}(x)\frac{dx}{dt})}^{2}}dt} \\ & ={\displaystyle \int_{{t}_{1}}^{{t}_{2}}\sqrt{{(\frac{dx}{dt})}^{2}(1+{({F}^{'}(x))}^{2})}dt} \\ & ={\displaystyle \int_{{t}_{1}}^{{t}_{2}}{x}^{'}(t)\sqrt{1+{(\frac{dy}{dx})}^{2}}dt}.\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167794051442}

Here we have assumed that ${x}^{'}(t)>0,$ which is a reasonable assumption. The Chain Rule gives $dx={x}^{'}(t)\;dt,$ and letting $a=x({t}_{1})$ and $b=x({t}_{2})$ we obtain the formula

$s={\displaystyle \int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{1+{(\frac{dy}{dx})}^{2}}dx},$  {eq:fs-id1167794100345}

which is the formula for arc length obtained in the [Introduction to the Applications of Integration](module:m53638).

We now return to the problem posed at the beginning of the section about a baseball leaving a pitcher’s hand. Ignoring the effect of air resistance (unless it is a curve ball!), the ball travels a parabolic path. Assuming the pitcher’s hand is at the origin and the ball travels left to right in the direction of the positive *x*-axis, the parametric equations for this curve can be written as

$x(t)=140t,\;y(t)=−16{t}^{2}+2t$  {eq:fs-id1167794027857}

where *t* represents time. We first calculate the distance the ball travels as a function of time. This distance is represented by the arc length. We can modify the arc length formula slightly. First rewrite the functions $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ using *v* as an independent variable, so as to eliminate any confusion with the parameter *t*:

$x(v)=140v,\;y(v)=−16{v}^{2}+2v.$  {eq:fs-id1167794027956}

Then we write the arc length formula as follows:

$\begin{array}{rl}s(t) & ={\displaystyle \int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{{(\frac{dx}{dv})}^{2}+{(\frac{dy}{dv})}^{2}}dv} \\ & ={\displaystyle \int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(−32v+2)}^{2}}dv}.\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167794028015}

The variable *v* acts as a dummy variable that disappears after integration, leaving the arc length as a function of time *t.* To integrate this expression we can use a formula from [Appendix A](module:m54049),

${\displaystyle \int \sqrt{{a}^{2}+{u}^{2}}du}=\frac{u}{2}\sqrt{{a}^{2}+{u}^{2}}+\frac{{a}^{2}}{2}\text{ln}|u+\sqrt{{a}^{2}+{u}^{2}}|+C.$  {eq:fs-id1167793926200}

We set $a=140$ and $u=−32v+2.$ This gives $du=−32dv,$ so $dv=-\frac{1}{32}du.$ Therefore

$\begin{array}{rl}{\displaystyle \int \sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(−32v+2)}^{2}}dv} & =-\frac{1}{32}{\displaystyle \int \sqrt{{a}^{2}+{u}^{2}}du} \\ & =-\frac{1}{32}\left[\begin{array}{l}\frac{(−32v+2)}{2}\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(−32v+2)}^{2}} \\ +\frac{{140}^{2}}{2}\text{ln}|(−32v+2)+\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(−32v+2)}^{2}}|\end{array}\right]+C\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167794094341}

and

$\begin{array}{rl}s(t) & =-\frac{1}{32}[\frac{(−32t+2)}{2}\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(−32t+2)}^{2}}+\frac{{140}^{2}}{2}\text{ln}|(−32t+2)+\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(−32t+2)}^{2}}|] \\ & \;+\frac{1}{32}[\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{2}^{2}}+\frac{{140}^{2}}{2}\text{ln}|2+\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{2}^{2}}|] \\ & =(\frac{t}{2}-\frac{1}{32})\sqrt{1024{t}^{2}-128t+19604}-\frac{1225}{4}\text{ln}|(−32t+2)+\sqrt{1024{t}^{2}-128t+19604}| \\ & \;+\frac{\sqrt{19604}}{32}+\frac{1225}{4}\text{ln}(2+\sqrt{19604}).\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167794035513}

This function represents the distance traveled by the ball as a function of time. To calculate the speed, take the derivative of this function with respect to *t.* While this may seem like a daunting task, it is possible to obtain the answer directly from the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$\frac{d}{dx}{\displaystyle \int_{a}^{x}f(u)\;du}=f(x).$  {eq:fs-id1167794095966}

Therefore

$\begin{array}{rl}{s}^{'}(t) & =\frac{d}{dt}[s(t)] \\ & =\frac{d}{dt}[{\displaystyle \int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(−32v+2)}^{2}}dv}] \\ & =\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(−32t+2)}^{2}} \\ & =\sqrt{1024{t}^{2}-128t+19604} \\ & =2\sqrt{256{t}^{2}-32t+4901}.\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167794096026}

One third of a second after the ball leaves the pitcher’s hand, the distance it travels is equal to

$\begin{array}{rl}s(\frac{1}{3}) & =(\frac{1\text{/}3}{2}-\frac{1}{32})\sqrt{1024{(\frac{1}{3})}^{2}-128(\frac{1}{3})+19604} \\ & \;-\frac{1225}{4}\text{ln}|(−32(\frac{1}{3})+2)+\sqrt{1024{(\frac{1}{3})}^{2}-128(\frac{1}{3})+19604}| \\ & \;+\frac{\sqrt{19604}}{32}+\frac{1225}{4}\text{ln}(2+\sqrt{19604}) \\ & \approx 46.69\;\text{feet}.\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167794040888}

This value is just over three quarters of the way to home plate. The speed of the ball is

${s}^{'}(\frac{1}{3})=2\sqrt{256{(\frac{1}{3})}^{2}-16(\frac{1}{3})+4901}\approx 140.34\;\text{ft/s}.$  {eq:fs-id1167794135267}

This speed translates to approximately 95 mph—a major-league fastball.

## Surface Area Generated by a Parametric Curve
Recall the problem of finding the surface area of a volume of revolution. In [Curve Length and Surface Area](module:m53644), we derived a formula for finding the surface area of a volume generated by a function $y=f(x)$ from $x=a$ to $x=b,$ revolved around the *x*-axis:

$S=2\pi {\displaystyle \int_{a}^{b}f(x)\sqrt{1+{({f}^{'}(x))}^{2}}dx}.$  {eq:fs-id1167794135414}

We now consider a volume of revolution generated by revolving a parametrically defined curve $x=x(t),y=y(t),a\le t\le b$ around the *x*-axis as shown in the following figure.

> FIGURE {fig:CNX_Calc_Figure_11_02_011} src=../../media/CNX_Calc_Figure_11_02_010.jpg
> alt: A curve is drawn in the first quadrant with endpoints marked t = a and t = b. On this curve, there is a point marked (x(t), y(t)). There is a circle with an arrow drawn around the x axis that seems to indicate a rotation about the x axis, and there is a shape that accompanies that curve that seems to be what you would obtain if you rotated the curve about the x axis.
> caption: A surface of revolution generated by a parametrically defined curve.

The analogous formula for a parametrically defined curve is

$$ S=2\pi {\displaystyle \int_{a}^{b}y(t)\sqrt{{({x}^{'}(t))}^{2}+{({y}^{'}(t))}^{2}}dt} $$  {eq:fs-id1167794096436}

provided that $y(t)$ is not negative on $[a,b].$

When this curve is revolved around the *x*-axis, it generates a sphere of radius *r*. To calculate the surface area of the sphere, we use [ref:fs-id1167794096436]:

$\begin{array}{rl}S & =2\pi {\displaystyle \int_{a}^{b}y(t)\sqrt{{({x}^{'}(t))}^{2}+{({y}^{'}(t))}^{2}}dt} \\ & =2\pi {\displaystyle \int_{0}^{\pi}r\;\text{sin}\;t\sqrt{{(\text{−}r\;\text{sin}\;t)}^{2}+{(r\;\text{cos}\;t)}^{2}}dt} \\ & =2\pi {\displaystyle \int_{0}^{\pi}r\;\text{sin}\;t\sqrt{{r}^{2}{\text{sin}}^{2}t+{r}^{2}{\text{cos}}^{2}t}\;dt} \\ & =2\pi {\displaystyle \int_{0}^{\pi}r\;\text{sin}\;t\sqrt{{r}^{2}({\text{sin}}^{2}t+{\text{cos}}^{2}t)}dt} \\ & =2\pi {\displaystyle \int_{0}^{\pi}{r}^{2}\text{sin}\;t\;dt} \\ & =2\pi {r}^{2}({\text{−}\text{cos}\;t|}_{0}^{\pi}) \\ & =2\pi {r}^{2}(\text{−}\text{cos}\;\pi +\text{cos}\;0) \\ & =4\pi {r}^{2}.\end{array}$  {eq:fs-id1167794076251}

## Key Concepts {section:key-concepts}
- The derivative of the parametrically defined curve $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t)$ can be calculated using the formula $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{{y}^{'}(t)}{{x}^{'}(t)}.$ Using the derivative, we can find the equation of a tangent line to a parametric curve.
- The area between a parametric curve and the *x*-axis can be determined by using the formula $A={\displaystyle \int_{{t}_{1}}^{{t}_{2}}y(t){x}^{'}(t)\;dt}.$
- The arc length of a parametric curve can be calculated by using the formula $s={\displaystyle \int_{{t}_{1}}^{{t}_{2}}\sqrt{{(\frac{dx}{dt})}^{2}+{(\frac{dy}{dt})}^{2}}dt}.$
- The surface area of a volume of revolution revolved around the *x*-axis is given by $S=2\pi {\displaystyle \int_{a}^{b}y(t)\sqrt{{({x}^{'}(t))}^{2}+{({y}^{'}(t))}^{2}}dt}.$ If the curve is revolved around the *y*-axis, then the formula is $S=2\pi {\displaystyle \int_{a}^{b}x(t)\sqrt{{({x}^{'}(t))}^{2}+{({y}^{'}(t))}^{2}}dt}.$
