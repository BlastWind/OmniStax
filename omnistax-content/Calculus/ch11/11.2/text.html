Now that we have introduced the concept of a parameterized curve, our next step is to learn how to work with this concept in the context of calculus. For example, if we know a parameterization of a given curve, is it possible to calculate the slope of a tangent line to the curve? How about the arc length of the curve? Or the area under the curve?

Another scenario: Suppose we would like to represent the location of a baseball after the ball leaves a pitcher’s hand. If the position of the baseball is represented by the plane curve $(x(t),y(t)),$ then we should be able to use calculus to find the speed of the ball at any given time. Furthermore, we should be able to calculate just how far that ball has traveled as a function of time.

Derivatives of Parametric Equations

We start by asking how to calculate the slope of a line tangent to a parametric curve at a point. Consider the plane curve defined by the parametric equations

$$x(t)=2t+3,\;y(t)=3t-4,\;-2\le t\le 3.$$

The graph of this curve appears in Figure 11.16. It is a line segment starting at $(-1,-10)$ and ending at $(9,5).$

We can eliminate the parameter by first solving the equation $x(t)=2t+3$ for t:

$$\begin{array}{rll}x(t) & = & 2t+3 \\ x-3 & = & 2t \\ t & = & \frac{x-3}{2}.\end{array}$$

Substituting this into $y(t),$ we obtain

$$\begin{array}{rll}y(t) & = & 3t-4 \\ y & = & 3(\frac{x-3}{2})-4 \\ y & = & \frac{3x}{2}-\frac{9}{2}-4 \\ y & = & \frac{3x}{2}-\frac{17}{2}.\end{array}$$

The slope of this line is given by $\kdydx=\frac{3}{2}.$ Next we calculate $\kxprime(t)$ and $\kyprime(t).$ This gives $\kxprime(t)=2$ and $\kyprime(t)=3.$ Notice that $\kdydx=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}=\frac{3}{2}.$ This is no coincidence, as outlined in the following theorem.

Theorem

Derivative of Parametric Equations

Consider the plane curve defined by the parametric equations $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t).$ Suppose that $\kxprime(t)$ and $\kyprime(t)$ exist, and assume that $\kxprime(t)\ne 0.$ Then the derivative $\kdydx$ is given by

$$\kdydx=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}=\frac{\kyprime(t)}{\kxprime(t)}.\tag{11.1}$$

Proof

This theorem can be proven using the Chain Rule. In particular, assume that the parameter t can be eliminated, yielding a differentiable function $y=F(x).$ Then $y(t)=F(x(t)).$ Differentiating both sides of this equation using the Chain Rule yields

$$\kyprime(t)=F^{\prime}(x(t))\kxprime(t),$$

so

$$F^{\prime}(x(t))=\frac{\kyprime(t)}{\kxprime(t)}.$$

But $F^{\prime}(x(t))=\kdydx,$ which proves the theorem.

□

Equation 11.1 can be used to calculate derivatives of plane curves, as well as critical points. Recall that a critical point of a differentiable function $y=f(x)$ is any point $x=x_{0}$ such that either $f^{\prime}(x_{0})=0$ or $f^{\prime}(x_{0})$ does not exist. Equation 11.1 gives a formula for the slope of a tangent line to a curve defined parametrically regardless of whether the curve can be described by a function $y=f(x)$ or not.

Example 11.4 · Finding the Derivative of a Parametric Curve

Calculate the derivative $\kdydx$ for each of the following parametrically defined plane curves, and locate any critical points on their respective graphs.

$$\begin{array}{l}\kxprime(t)=2 \\ \kyprime(t)=3t^{2}-3.\end{array}$$

$$\begin{array}{l}\kdydx=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} \\ \kdydx=\frac{3t^{2}-3}{2}.\end{array}$$

This derivative is zero when $t=±1.$ When $t=-1$ we have

which corresponds to the point $(-1,6)$ on the graph. When $t=1$ we have

Example 11.5 · Finding a Tangent Line

Find an equation of the tangent line to the curve defined by the equations

$$x(t)=t^{2}-3,\;y(t)=2t-1,\;-3\le t\le 4\;\text{when}\;t=2.$$

Solution

First find the slope of the tangent line using Equation 11.1, which means calculating $\kxprime(t)$ and $\kyprime(t)\text{:}$

$$\begin{array}{l}\kxprime(t)=2t \\ \kyprime(t)=2.\end{array}$$

Next substitute these into the equation:

$$\begin{array}{l}\kdydx=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} \\ \kdydx=\frac{2}{2t} \\ \kdydx=\frac{1}{t}.\end{array}$$

When $t=2,$ $\kdydx=\frac{1}{2},$ so this is the slope of the tangent line. Calculating $x(2)$ and $y(2)$ gives

$$x(2)={(2)}^{2}-3=1\;\text{and}\;y(2)=2(2)-1=3,$$

which corresponds to the point $(1,3)$ on the graph (Figure 11.20). Now use the point-slope form of the equation of a line to find an equation of the tangent line:

$$\begin{array}{rll}y-y_{0} & = & m(x-x_{0}) \\ y-3 & = & \frac{1}{2}(x-1) \\ y-3 & = & \frac{1}{2}x-\frac{1}{2} \\ y & = & \frac{1}{2}x+\frac{5}{2}.\end{array}$$

Second-Order Derivatives

Our next goal is to see how to take the second derivative of a function defined parametrically. The second derivative of a function $y=f(x)$ is defined to be the derivative of the first derivative; that is,

$$\kdtwoydx=\frac{d}{dx}[\kdydx].$$

Since $\kdydx=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt},$ we can replace the $y$ on both sides of this equation with $\kdydx.$ This gives us

$$\kdtwoydx=\frac{d}{dx}(\kdydx)=\frac{(d/dt)(dy/dx)}{dx/dt}.\tag{11.2}$$

If we know $dy/dx$ as a function of t, then this formula is straightforward to apply.

Example 11.6 · Finding a Second Derivative

Calculate the second derivative $d^{2}y/dx^{2}$ for the plane curve defined by the parametric equations $x(t)=t^{2}-3,y(t)=2t-1,-3\le t\le 4.$

Solution

From Example 11.4 we know that $\kdydx=\frac{2}{2t}=\frac{1}{t}.$ Using Equation 11.2, we obtain

$$\kdtwoydx=\frac{(d/dt)(dy/dx)}{dx/dt}=\frac{(d/dt)(1/t)}{2t}=\frac{-t^{-2}}{2t}=-\frac{1}{2t^{3}}.$$

Integrals Involving Parametric Equations

Now that we have seen how to calculate the derivative of a plane curve, the next question is this: How do we find the area under a curve defined parametrically? Recall the cycloid defined by the equations $x(t)=t-\sin t,\;y(t)=1-\cos t.$ Suppose we want to find the area of the shaded region in the following graph.

To derive a formula for the area under the curve defined by the functions

$$x=x(t),\;y=y(t),\;a\le t\le b,$$

we assume that $x(t)$ is increasing on the interval $t\in [a,b]$ and $x(t)$ is differentiable and start with an equal partition of the interval $a\le t\le b.$ Suppose $t_{0}=a\lt t_{1}\lt t_{2}\lt \cdots \lt t_{n}=b$ and consider the following graph.

We use rectangles to approximate the area under the curve. The height of the $i$th rectangle is $y(t_{i-1})$, so an approximation to the area is

$$\begin{array}{l}\begin{array}{ll} & \sum_{i=1}^{n} y(t_{i-1})(x(t_{i})-x(t_{i-1})) \\ = & \sum_{i=1}^{n} y(t_{i-1})\frac{(x(t_{i})-x(t_{i-1}))}{(t_{i}-t_{i-1})}(t_{i}-t_{i-1}) \\ & \to \int_a^{b}y(t)\kxprime(t)dt\text{as max}\{(t_{i}-t_{i-1})\}\to 0\end{array}\end{array}$$

This follows from results obtained in Chapter 5 for the function $y(t_{i-1})\frac{(x(t_{i})-x(t_{i-1}))}{(t_{i}-t_{i-1})}.$

Then a Riemann sum for the area is

$$\kA_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n} y(\bar{t_{i}})\;(x(t_{i})-x(t_{i-1})).$$

Multiplying and dividing each area by $t_{i}-t_{i-1}$ gives

$$\kA_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n} y(x({\bar{t}}_{i}))\;(\frac{x(t_{i})-x(t_{i-1})}{t_{i}-t_{i-1}}) (t_{i}-t_{i-1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n} y(x({\bar{t}}_{i}))\;(\frac{x(t_{i})-x(t_{i-1})}{\Delta t}) \Delta t.$$

Taking the limit as $n$ approaches infinity gives

$$\kA=\lim_{n\to \infty}\kA_{n}=\int_a^{b}y(t)\kxprime(t)\;dt .$$

If $x$ is a decreasing function for $a\le t\le b$, a similar derivation will show that the area is given by $-\int_a^{b}y(t)\kxprime(t)dt=\int_a^{b}y(t)\kxprime(t)dt$

This leads to the following theorem.

Theorem

Area under a Parametric Curve

Consider the non-self-intersecting plane curve defined by the parametric equations

$$x=x(t),\;y=y(t),\;a\le t\le b$$

and assume that $x(t)$ is differentiable. The area under this curve is given by

$$\kA=\int_a^{b}y(t)\kxprime(t)\;dt .\tag{11.3}$$

Example 11.7 · Finding the Area under a Parametric Curve

Find the area under the curve of the cycloid defined by the equations

$$x(t)=t-\sin t,\;y(t)=1-\cos t,\;0\le t\le 2\pi .$$

Solution

Using Equation 11.3, we have

$$\begin{array}{rl}A & =\int_a^{b}y(t)\kxprime(t)\;dt \\ & =\int_0^{2\pi}(1-\cos t)(1-\cos t)\;dt \\ & =\int_0^{2\pi}(1-2\;\cos t+{\cos }^{2}t)dt \\ & =\int_0^{2\pi}(1-2\;\cos t+\frac{1+\cos 2t}{2})\;dt \\ & =\int_0^{2\pi}(\frac{3}{2}-2\;\cos t+\frac{\cos 2t}{2})\;dt \\ & ={\frac{3t}{2}-2\;\sin t+\frac{\sin 2t}{4}|}_0^{2\pi} \\ & =3\pi .\end{array}$$

Arc Length of a Parametric Curve

In addition to finding the area under a parametric curve, we sometimes need to find the arc length of a parametric curve. In the case of a line segment, arc length is the same as the distance between the endpoints. If a particle travels from point A to point B along a curve, then the distance that particle travels is the arc length. To develop a formula for arc length, we start with an approximation by line segments as shown in the following graph.

Given a plane curve defined by the functions $x=x(t),y=y(t),a\le t\le b,$ we start by partitioning the interval $[a,b]$ into n equal subintervals: $t_{0}=a\lt t_{1}\lt t_{2}\lt \cdots \lt t_{n}=b.$ The width of each subinterval is given by $\Delta t=(b-a)/n.$ We can calculate the length of each line segment:

$$\begin{array}{l} \\ d_{1}=\sqrt{{(x(t_{1})-x(t_{0}))}^{2}+{(y(t_{1})-y(t_{0}))}^{2}} \\ d_{2}=\sqrt{{(x(t_{2})-x(t_{1}))}^{2}+{(y(t_{2})-y(t_{1}))}^{2}}\;\text{etc}.\end{array}$$

Then add these up. We let s denote the exact arc length and $\ks_{n}$ denote the approximation by n line segments:

$$\ks\approx \sum_{k=1}^{n} \ks_{k} =\sum_{k=1}^{n} \sqrt{{(x(t_{k})-x(t_{k-1}))}^{2}+{(y(t_{k})-y(t_{k-1}))}^{2}} .\tag{11.4}$$

If we assume that $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ are differentiable functions of t, then the Mean Value Theorem (Introduction to the Applications of Derivatives) applies, so in each subinterval $[t_{k-1},t_{k}]$ there exist ${\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k}$ and ${\tilde{t}}_{k}$ such that

$$\begin{array}{l} \\ x(t_{k})-x(t_{k-1})=\kxprime({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k})(t_{k}-t_{k-1})=\kxprime({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k})\Delta t \\ y(t_{k})-y(t_{k-1})=\kyprime({\tilde{t}}_{k})(t_{k}-t_{k-1})=\kyprime({\tilde{t}}_{k})\Delta t.\end{array}$$

Therefore Equation 11.4 becomes

$$\begin{array}{rl}s & \approx \sum_{k=1}^{n} \ks_{k} \\ & =\sum_{k=1}^{n} \sqrt{{(\kxprime({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k})\Delta t)}^{2}+{(\kyprime({\tilde{t}}_{k})\Delta t)}^{2}} \\ & =\sum_{k=1}^{n} \sqrt{{(\kxprime({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k}))}^{2}{(\Delta t)}^{2}+{(\kyprime({\tilde{t}}_{k}))}^{2}{(\Delta t)}^{2}} \\ & =(\sum_{k=1}^{n} \sqrt{{(\kxprime({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k}))}^{2}+{(\kyprime({\tilde{t}}_{k}))}^{2}} )\Delta t.\end{array}$$

This is a Riemann sum that approximates the arc length over a partition of the interval $[a,b].$ If we further assume that the derivatives are continuous and let the number of points in the partition increase without bound, the approximation approaches the exact arc length. This gives

$$\begin{array}{rl}s & =\lim_{n\to \infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n} \ks_{k} \\ & =\lim_{n\to \infty}(\sum_{k=1}^{n} \sqrt{{(\kxprime({\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k}))}^{2}+{(\kyprime({\tilde{t}}_{k}))}^{2}} )\Delta t \\ & =\int_a^{b}\sqrt{{(\kxprime(t))}^{2}+{(\kyprime(t))}^{2}}dt .\end{array}$$

When taking the limit, the values of ${\overset{\wedge}{t}}_{k}$ and ${\tilde{t}}_{k}$ are both contained within the same ever-shrinking interval of width $\Delta t,$ so they must converge to the same value.

We can summarize this method in the following theorem.

Theorem

Arc Length of a Parametric Curve

Consider the plane curve defined by the parametric equations

$$x=x(t),\;y=y(t),\;t_{1}\le t\le t_{2}$$

and assume that $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ are differentiable functions of t. Then the arc length of this curve is given by

$$\ks=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{{(\kdxdt)}^{2}+{(\kdydt)}^{2}}dt .\tag{11.5}$$

At this point a side derivation leads to a previous formula for arc length. In particular, suppose the parameter can be eliminated, leading to a function $y=F(x).$ Then $y(t)=F(x(t))$ and the Chain Rule gives $\kyprime(t)=F^{\prime}(x(t))\kxprime(t).$ Substituting this into Equation 11.5 gives

$$\begin{array}{rl}s & =\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{{(\kdxdt)}^{2}+{(\kdydt)}^{2}}dt \\ & =\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{{(\kdxdt)}^{2}+{(F^{\prime}(x)\kdxdt)}^{2}}dt \\ & =\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{{(\kdxdt)}^{2}(1+{(F^{\prime}(x))}^{2})}dt \\ & =\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\kxprime(t)\sqrt{1+{(\kdydx)}^{2}}dt .\end{array}$$

Here we have assumed that $\kxprime(t)\gt 0,$ which is a reasonable assumption. The Chain Rule gives $dx=\kxprime(t)\;dt,$ and letting $a=x(t_{1})$ and $b=x(t_{2})$ we obtain the formula

$$\ks=\int_a^{b}\sqrt{1+{(\kdydx)}^{2}}dx ,$$

which is the formula for arc length obtained in the Introduction to the Applications of Integration.

Example 11.8 · Finding the Arc Length of a Parametric Curve

Find the arc length of the semicircle defined by the equations

$$x(t)=3\;\cos t,\;y(t)=3\;\sin t,\;0\le t\le \pi .$$

Solution

The values $t=0$ to $t=\pi$ trace out the red curve in Figure 11.23. To determine its length, use Equation 11.5:

$$\begin{array}{rl}s & =\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\sqrt{{(\kdxdt)}^{2}+{(\kdydt)}^{2}}dt \\ & =\int_0^{\pi}\sqrt{{(-3\;\sin t)}^{2}+{(3\;\cos t)}^{2}}dt \\ & =\int_0^{\pi}\sqrt{9\;{\sin }^{2}t+9\;{\cos }^{2}t}\;dt \\ & =\int_0^{\pi}\sqrt{9({\sin }^{2}t+{\cos }^{2}t)}dt \\ & =\int_0^{\pi}3dt ={3t|}_0^{\pi}=3\pi .\end{array}$$

Note that the formula for the arc length of a semicircle is $\pi r$ and the radius of this circle is 3. This is a great example of using calculus to derive a known formula of a geometric quantity.

We now return to the problem posed at the beginning of the section about a baseball leaving a pitcher’s hand. Ignoring the effect of air resistance (unless it is a curve ball!), the ball travels a parabolic path. Assuming the pitcher’s hand is at the origin and the ball travels left to right in the direction of the positive x-axis, the parametric equations for this curve can be written as

$$x(t)=140t,\;y(t)=-16t^{2}+2t$$

where t represents time. We first calculate the distance the ball travels as a function of time. This distance is represented by the arc length. We can modify the arc length formula slightly. First rewrite the functions $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ using v as an independent variable, so as to eliminate any confusion with the parameter t:

$$x(v)=140v,\;y(v)=-16v^{2}+2v.$$

Then we write the arc length formula as follows:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\ks(t) & =\int_0^{t}\sqrt{{(\frac{dx}{dv})}^{2}+{(\frac{dy}{dv})}^{2}}dv \\ & =\int_0^{t}\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(-32v+2)}^{2}}dv .\end{array}$$

The variable v acts as a dummy variable that disappears after integration, leaving the arc length as a function of time t. To integrate this expression we can use a formula from Appendix A,

$$\int \sqrt{a^{2}+u^{2}}du =\frac{u}{2}\sqrt{a^{2}+u^{2}}+\frac{a^{2}}{2}\ln |u+\sqrt{a^{2}+u^{2}}|+C.$$

We set $a=140$ and $u=-32v+2.$ This gives $du=-32dv,$ so $dv=-\frac{1}{32}du.$ Therefore

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int \sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(-32v+2)}^{2}}dv & =-\frac{1}{32}\int \sqrt{a^{2}+u^{2}}du \\ & =-\frac{1}{32}[\begin{array}{l}\frac{(-32v+2)}{2}\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(-32v+2)}^{2}} \\ +\frac{{140}^{2}}{2}\ln |(-32v+2)+\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(-32v+2)}^{2}}|\end{array}]+C\end{array}$$

and

$$\begin{array}{rl}\ks(t) & =-\frac{1}{32}[\frac{(-32t+2)}{2}\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(-32t+2)}^{2}}+\frac{{140}^{2}}{2}\ln |(-32t+2)+\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(-32t+2)}^{2}}|] \\ & \;+\frac{1}{32}[\sqrt{{140}^{2}+2^{2}}+\frac{{140}^{2}}{2}\ln |2+\sqrt{{140}^{2}+2^{2}}|] \\ & =(\frac{t}{2}-\frac{1}{32})\sqrt{1024t^{2}-128t+19604}-\frac{1225}{4}\ln |(-32t+2)+\sqrt{1024t^{2}-128t+19604}| \\ & \;+\frac{\sqrt{19604}}{32}+\frac{1225}{4}\ln (2+\sqrt{19604}).\end{array}$$

This function represents the distance traveled by the ball as a function of time. To calculate the speed, take the derivative of this function with respect to t. While this may seem like a daunting task, it is possible to obtain the answer directly from the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{x}f(u)\;du =f(x).$$

Therefore

$$\begin{array}{rl}\ksprime(t) & =\frac{d}{dt}[\ks(t)] \\ & =\frac{d}{dt}[\int_0^{t}\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(-32v+2)}^{2}}dv ] \\ & =\sqrt{{140}^{2}+{(-32t+2)}^{2}} \\ & =\sqrt{1024t^{2}-128t+19604} \\ & =2\sqrt{256t^{2}-32t+4901}.\end{array}$$

One third of a second after the ball leaves the pitcher’s hand, the distance it travels is equal to

$$\begin{array}{rl}\ks(\frac{1}{3}) & =(\frac{1/3}{2}-\frac{1}{32})\sqrt{1024{(\frac{1}{3})}^{2}-128(\frac{1}{3})+19604} \\ & \;-\frac{1225}{4}\ln |(-32(\frac{1}{3})+2)+\sqrt{1024{(\frac{1}{3})}^{2}-128(\frac{1}{3})+19604}| \\ & \;+\frac{\sqrt{19604}}{32}+\frac{1225}{4}\ln (2+\sqrt{19604}) \\ & \approx 46.69\;\text{feet}.\end{array}$$

This value is just over three quarters of the way to home plate. The speed of the ball is

$$\ksprime(\frac{1}{3})=2\sqrt{256{(\frac{1}{3})}^{2}-16(\frac{1}{3})+4901}\approx 140.34\;\text{ft/s}.$$

This speed translates to approximately 95 mph—a major-league fastball.

Surface Area Generated by a Parametric Curve

Recall the problem of finding the surface area of a volume of revolution. In Curve Length and Surface Area, we derived a formula for finding the surface area of a volume generated by a function $y=f(x)$ from $x=a$ to $x=b,$ revolved around the x-axis:

$$\kS=2\pi \int_a^{b}f(x)\sqrt{1+{(f^{\prime}(x))}^{2}}dx .$$

We now consider a volume of revolution generated by revolving a parametrically defined curve $x=x(t),y=y(t),a\le t\le b$ around the x-axis as shown in the following figure.

The analogous formula for a parametrically defined curve is

$$\kS=2\pi \int_a^{b}y(t)\sqrt{{(\kxprime(t))}^{2}+{(\kyprime(t))}^{2}}dt\tag{11.6}$$

provided that $y(t)$ is not negative on $[a,b].$

Example 11.9 · Finding Surface Area

Find the surface area of a sphere of radius r centered at the origin.

Solution

We start with the curve defined by the equations

$$x(t)=r\;\cos t,\;y(t)=r\;\sin t,\;0\le t\le \pi .$$

This generates an upper semicircle of radius r centered at the origin as shown in the following graph.

When this curve is revolved around the x-axis, it generates a sphere of radius r. To calculate the surface area of the sphere, we use Equation 11.6:

$$\begin{array}{rl}S & =2\pi \int_a^{b}y(t)\sqrt{{(\kxprime(t))}^{2}+{(\kyprime(t))}^{2}}dt \\ & =2\pi \int_0^{\pi}r\;\sin t\sqrt{{(-r\;\sin t)}^{2}+{(r\;\cos t)}^{2}}dt \\ & =2\pi \int_0^{\pi}r\;\sin t\sqrt{r^{2}{\sin }^{2}t+r^{2}{\cos }^{2}t}\;dt \\ & =2\pi \int_0^{\pi}r\;\sin t\sqrt{r^{2}({\sin }^{2}t+{\cos }^{2}t)}dt \\ & =2\pi \int_0^{\pi}r^{2}\sin t\;dt \\ & =2\pi r^{2}({-\cos t|}_0^{\pi}) \\ & =2\pi r^{2}(-\cos \pi +\cos 0) \\ & =4\pi r^{2}.\end{array}$$

This is, in fact, the formula for the surface area of a sphere.
