Imagine a mechanic turning a wrench to tighten a bolt. The mechanic applies a force at the end of the wrench. This creates rotation, or torque, which tightens the bolt. We can use vectors to represent the force applied by the mechanic, and the distance (radius) from the bolt to the end of the wrench. Then, we can represent torque by a vector oriented along the axis of rotation. Note that the torque vector is orthogonal to both the force vector and the radius vector.

In this section, we develop an operation called the cross product, which allows us to find a vector orthogonal to two given vectors. Calculating torque is an important application of cross products, and we examine torque in more detail later in the section. This material uses a 3 × 3 determinant of the form $|\begin{array}{lll}a & b & c \\ d & e & f \\ g & h & i\end{array}|$, which expands by minors to $a|\begin{array}{ll}e & f \\ h & i\end{array}|-b|\begin{array}{ll}d & f \\ g & i\end{array}|+c|\begin{array}{ll}d & e \\ g & h\end{array}|=a(ei-fh)–b(di-fg)+c(dh-eg).$

The Cross Product and Its Properties

The dot product is a multiplication of two vectors that results in a scalar. In this section, we introduce a product of two vectors that generates a third vector orthogonal to the first two. Consider how we might find such a vector. Let $\kvecu=\langle u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\rangle$ and $\kvecv=\langle v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\rangle$ be nonzero vectors. We want to find a vector $\kvecw=\langle w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\rangle$ orthogonal to both $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $—that is, we want to find $\kvecw $ such that $\kvecu\cdot \kvecw=0$ and $\kvecv\cdot \kvecw=0.$ Therefore, $w_{1},$ $w_{2},$ and $w_{3}$ must satisfy

$$\begin{array}{rll}u_{1}w_{1}+u_{2}w_{2}+u_{3}w_{3} & = & 0 \\ v_{1}w_{1}+v_{2}w_{2}+v_{3}w_{3} & = & 0.\end{array}$$

If we multiply the top equation by $v_{3}$ and the bottom equation by $u_{3}$ and subtract, we can eliminate the variable $w_{3},$ which gives

$$(u_{1}v_{3}-v_{1}u_{3})w_{1}+(u_{2}v_{3}-v_{2}u_{3})w_{2}=0.$$

If we select

$$\begin{array}{rll}w_{1} & = & u_{2}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{2} \\ w_{2} & = & -(u_{1}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{1}),\end{array}$$

we get a possible solution vector. Substituting these values back into the original equations gives

$$w_{3}=u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1}.$$

That is, vector

$$\kvecw=\langle u_{2}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{2},-(u_{1}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{1}),u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1}\rangle$$

is orthogonal to both $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv,$ which leads us to define the following operation, called the cross product.

Definition

Let $\kvecu=\langle u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\rangle \;\text{and}\;\kvecv=\langle v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\rangle .$ Then, the cross product $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv $ is vector

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv & =(u_{2}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{2})\kveci-(u_{1}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{1})\kvecj+(u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1})\kveck \\ & =\langle u_{2}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{2},-(u_{1}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{1}),u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1}\rangle .\end{array}\tag{12.9}$$

From the way we have developed $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv,$ it should be clear that the cross product is orthogonal to both $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv.$ However, it never hurts to check. To show that $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv $ is orthogonal to $\kvecu,$ we calculate the dot product of $\kvecu $ and $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv.$

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecu\cdot (\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv) & =\langle u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\rangle \cdot \langle u_{2}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{2},-u_{1}v_{3}+u_{3}v_{1},u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1}\rangle \\ & =u_{1}(u_{2}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{2})+u_{2}(-u_{1}v_{3}+u_{3}v_{1})+u_{3}(u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1}) \\ & =u_{1}u_{2}v_{3}-u_{1}u_{3}v_{2}-u_{1}u_{2}v_{3}+u_{2}u_{3}v_{1}+u_{1}u_{3}v_{2}-u_{2}u_{3}v_{1} \\ & =(u_{1}u_{2}v_{3}-u_{1}u_{2}v_{3})+(-u_{1}u_{3}v_{2}+u_{1}u_{3}v_{2})+(u_{2}u_{3}v_{1}-u_{2}u_{3}v_{1}) \\ & =0\end{array}$$

In a similar manner, we can show that the cross product is also orthogonal to $\kvecv.$

Example 12.31 · Finding a Cross Product

Let $\kvecp=\langle -1,2,5\rangle \;\text{and}\;\kvecq=\langle 4,0,-3\rangle$ (Figure 12.53). Find $\kvecp\;\times \;\kvecq.$

Solution

Substitute the components of the vectors into Equation 12.9:

$$\begin{array}{ll}\kvecp\;\times \;\kvecq & =\langle -1,2,5\rangle \;\times \;\langle 4,0,-3\rangle \\ & =\langle p_{2}q_{3}-p_{3}q_{2},-(p_{1}q_{3}-p_{3}q_{1}),p_{1}q_{2}-p_{2}q_{1}\rangle \\ & =\langle p_{2}q_{3}-p_{3}q_{2},p_{3}q_{1}-p_{1}q_{3},p_{1}q_{2}-p_{2}q_{1}\rangle \\ & =\langle 2(-3)-5(0),5(4)-(-1)(-3),(-1)0-2(4)\rangle \\ & =\langle -6,17,-8\rangle .\end{array}$$

Although it may not be obvious from Equation 12.9, the direction of $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv $ is given by the right-hand rule. If we hold the right hand out with the fingers pointing in the direction of $\kvecu,$ then curl the fingers toward vector $\kvecv,$ the thumb points in the direction of the cross product, as shown.

Notice what this means for the direction of $\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecu.$ If we apply the right-hand rule to $\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecu,$ we start with our fingers pointed in the direction of $\kvecv,$ then curl our fingers toward the vector $\kvecu.$ In this case, the thumb points in the opposite direction of $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv.$ (Try it!)

Example 12.32 · Anticommutativity of the Cross Product

Let $\kvecu=\langle 0,2,1\rangle$ and $\kvecv=\langle 3,-1,0\rangle .$ Calculate $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv $ and $\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecu $ and graph them.

Solution

We have

$$\begin{array}{rll}\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv & = & \langle (0+1),-(0-3),(0-6)\rangle =\langle 1,3,-6\rangle \\ \kvecv\;\times \;\kvecu & = & \langle (-1-0),-(3-0),(6-0)\rangle =\langle -1,-3,6\rangle .\end{array}$$

We see that, in this case, $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv=-(\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecu)$ (Figure 12.56). We prove this in general later in this section.

The cross products of the standard unit vectors $\kveci,\kvecj,$ and $\kveck $ can be useful for simplifying some calculations, so let’s consider these cross products. A straightforward application of the definition shows that

$$\kveci\;\times \;\kveci=\kvecj\;\times \;\kvecj=\kveck\;\times \;\kveck=\kveczero.$$

(The cross product of two vectors is a vector, so each of these products results in the zero vector, not the scalar $0.)$ It’s up to you to verify the calculations on your own.

Furthermore, because the cross product of two vectors is orthogonal to each of these vectors, we know that the cross product of $\kveci $ and $\kvecj $ is parallel to $\kveck.$ Similarly, the vector product of $\kveci $ and $\kveck $ is parallel to $\kvecj,$ and the vector product of $\kvecj $ and $\kveck $ is parallel to $\kveci.$ We can use the right-hand rule to determine the direction of each product. Then we have

$$\begin{array}{rllllrll}\kveci\;\times \;\kvecj & = & \kveck & & & \kvecj\;\times \;\kveci & = & -\kveck \\ \kvecj\;\times \;\kveck & = & \kveci & & & \kveck\;\times \;\kvecj & = & -\kveci \\ \kveck\;\times \;\kveci & = & \kvecj & & & \kveci\;\times \;\kveck & = & -\kvecj.\end{array}$$

These formulas come in handy later.

Example 12.33 · Cross Product of Standard Unit Vectors

Find $\kveci\;\times \;(\kvecj\;\times \;\kveck).$

Solution

We know that $\kvecj\;\times \;\kveck=\kveci.$ Therefore, $\kveci\;\times \;(\kvecj\;\times \;\kveck)=\kveci\;\times \;\kveci=\kveczero.$

As we have seen, the dot product is often called the scalar product because it results in a scalar. The cross product results in a vector, so it is sometimes called the vector product. These operations are both versions of vector multiplication, but they have very different properties and applications. Let’s explore some properties of the cross product. We prove only a few of them. Proofs of the other properties are left as exercises.

Theorem

Properties of the Cross Product

Let $\kvecu,\kvecv,$ and $\kvecw $ be vectors in space, and let $c$ be a scalar.

$$\begin{array}{lllrllll}\text{i.} & & & \kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv & = & -(\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecu) & & \text{Anticommutative property} \\ \text{ii.} & & & \kvecu\;\times \;(\kvecv+\kvecw) & = & \kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv+\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecw & & \text{Distributive property} \\ \text{iii.} & & & c(\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv) & = & (c\kvecu)\;\times \;\kvecv=\kvecu\;\times \;(c\kvecv) & & \text{Multiplication by a constant} \\ \text{iv.} & & & \kvecu\;\times \;\kveczero & = & \kveczero\;\times \;\kvecu=\kveczero & & \text{Cross product of the zero vector} \\ \text{v.} & & & \kvecv\;\times \;\kvecv & = & \kveczero & & \text{Cross product of a vector with itself} \\ \text{vi.} & & & \kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw) & = & (\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv)\cdot \kvecw & & \text{Scalar triple product}\end{array}$$

Proof

For property $\kveci.,$ we want to show $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv=-(\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecu).$ We have

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv & =\langle u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\rangle \;\times \;\langle v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\rangle \\ & =\langle u_{2}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{2},-u_{1}v_{3}+u_{3}v_{1},u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1}\rangle \\ & =-\langle u_{3}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{3},-u_{3}v_{1}+u_{1}v_{3},u_{2}v_{1}-u_{1}v_{2}\rangle \\ & =-\langle v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\rangle \;\times \;\langle u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\rangle \\ & =-(\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecu).\end{array}$$

Unlike most operations we’ve seen, the cross product is not commutative. This makes sense if we think about the right-hand rule.

For property $\text{iv}.,$ this follows directly from the definition of the cross product. We have

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecu\;\times \;\kveczero & =\langle u_{2}(0)-u_{3}(0),-(u_{2}(0)-u_{3}(0)),u_{1}(0)-u_{2}(0)\rangle \\ & =\langle 0,0,0\rangle =\kveczero.\end{array}$$

Then, by property i., $\kveczero\;\times \;\kvecu=\kveczero$ as well. Remember that the dot product of a vector and the zero vector is the scalar $0,$ whereas the cross product of a vector with the zero vector is the vector $\kveczero.$

Property $\text{vi}.$ looks like the associative property, but note the change in operations:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw) & =\kvecu\cdot \langle v_{2}w_{3}-v_{3}w_{2},-v_{1}w_{3}+v_{3}w_{1},v_{1}w_{2}-v_{2}w_{1}\rangle \\ & =u_{1}(v_{2}w_{3}-v_{3}w_{2})+u_{2}(-v_{1}w_{3}+v_{3}w_{1})+u_{3}(v_{1}w_{2}-v_{2}w_{1}) \\ & =u_{1}v_{2}w_{3}-u_{1}v_{3}w_{2}-u_{2}v_{1}w_{3}+u_{2}v_{3}w_{1}+u_{3}v_{1}w_{2}-u_{3}v_{2}w_{1} \\ & =(u_{2}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{2})w_{1}+(u_{3}v_{1}-u_{1}v_{3})w_{2}+(u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1})w_{3} \\ & =\langle u_{2}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{2},u_{3}v_{1}-u_{1}v_{3},u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1}\rangle \cdot \langle w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\rangle \\ & =(\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv)\cdot \kvecw.\end{array}$$

□

Example 12.34 · Using the Properties of the Cross Product

Use the cross product properties to calculate $(2\kveci\;\times \;3\kvecj)\;\times \;\kvecj.$

Solution

$$\begin{array}{rl}(2\kveci\;\times \;3\kvecj)\;\times \;\kvecj & =2(\kveci\;\times \;3\kvecj)\;\times \;\kvecj \\ & =2(3)(\kveci\;\times \;\kvecj)\;\times \;\kvecj \\ & =(6\kveck)\;\times \;\kvecj \\ & =6(\kveck\;\times \;\kvecj) \\ & =6(-\kveci)=-6\kveci.\end{array}$$

So far in this section, we have been concerned with the direction of the vector $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv,$ but we have not discussed its magnitude. It turns out there is a simple expression for the magnitude of $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv $ involving the magnitudes of $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv,$ and the sine of the angle between them.

Theorem

Magnitude of the Cross Product

Let $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $ be vectors, and let $\ktheta$ be the angle between them. Then, $\| \kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv\| =\| \kvecu\| \cdot \| \kvecv\| \cdot \sin \ktheta .$

Proof

Let $\kvecu=\langle u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\rangle$ and $\kvecv=\langle v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\rangle$ be vectors, and let $\ktheta$ denote the angle between them. Then

$$\begin{array}{rl}{\| \kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv\|}^{2} & ={(u_{2}v_{3}-u_{3}v_{2})}^{2}+{(u_{3}v_{1}-u_{1}v_{3})}^{2}+{(u_{1}v_{2}-u_{2}v_{1})}^{2} \\ & =u_2^{2}v_3^{2}-2u_{2}u_{3}v_{2}v_{3}+u_3^{2}v_2^{2}+u_3^{2}v_1^{2}-2u_{1}u_{3}v_{1}v_{3}+u_1^{2}v_3^{2}+u_1^{2}v_2^{2}-2u_{1}u_{2}v_{1}v_{2}+u_2^{2}v_1^{2} \\ & =u_1^{2}v_1^{2}+u_1^{2}v_2^{2}+u_1^{2}v_3^{2}+u_2^{2}v_1^{2}+u_2^{2}v_2^{2}+u_2^{2}v_3^{2}+u_3^{2}v_1^{2}+u_3^{2}v_2^{2}+u_3^{2}v_3^{2} \\ & \hspace{2em}-(u_1^{2}v_1^{2}+u_2^{2}v_2^{2}+u_3^{2}v_3^{2}+2u_{1}u_{2}v_{1}v_{2}+2u_{1}u_{3}v_{1}v_{3}+2u_{2}u_{3}v_{2}v_{3}) \\ & =(u_1^{2}+u_2^{2}+u_3^{2})(v_1^{2}+v_2^{2}+v_3^{2})-{(u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}+u_{3}v_{3})}^{2} \\ & ={\| \kvecu\|}^{2}{\| \kvecv\|}^{2}-{(\kvecu\cdot \kvecv)}^{2} \\ & ={\| \kvecu\|}^{2}{\| \kvecv\|}^{2}-{\| \kvecu\|}^{2}{\| \kvecv\|}^{2}{\cos }^{2}\ktheta \\ & ={\| \kvecu\|}^{2}{\| \kvecv\|}^{2}(1-{\cos }^{2}\ktheta ) \\ & ={\| \kvecu\|}^{2}{\| \kvecv\|}^{2}({\sin }^{2}\ktheta ).\end{array}$$

Taking square roots and noting that $\sqrt{{\sin }^{2}\ktheta}=\sin \ktheta$ for $0\le \ktheta \le 180^{\circ},$ we have the desired result:

$$\| \kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv\| =\| \kvecu\| \| \kvecv\| \sin \ktheta .$$

□

This definition of the cross product allows us to visualize or interpret the product geometrically. It is clear, for example, that the cross product is defined only for vectors in three dimensions, not for vectors in two dimensions. In two dimensions, it is impossible to generate a vector simultaneously orthogonal to two nonparallel vectors.

Example 12.35 · Calculating the Cross Product

Use Properties of the Cross Product to find the magnitude of the cross product of $\kvecu=\langle 0,4,0\rangle$ and $\kvecv=\langle 0,0,-3\rangle .$

Solution

We have

$$\begin{array}{rl}\| \kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv\| & =\| \kvecu\| \cdot \| \kvecv\| \cdot \sin \ktheta \\ & =\sqrt{0^{2}+4^{2}+0^{2}}\cdot \sqrt{0^{2}+0^{2}+{(-3)}^{2}}\cdot \sin \frac{\pi}{2} \\ & =4(3)(1)=12.\end{array}$$

Determinants and the Cross Product

Using Equation 12.9 to find the cross product of two vectors is straightforward, and it presents the cross product in the useful component form. The formula, however, is complicated and difficult to remember. Fortunately, we have an alternative. We can calculate the cross product of two vectors using determinant notation.

A $2\;\times \;2$ determinant is defined by

$$|\begin{array}{ll}a_{1} & a_{2} \\ b_{1} & b_{2}\end{array}|=a_{1}b_{2}-b_{1}a_{2}.$$

For example,

$$|\begin{array}{lr}3 & -2 \\ 5 & 1\end{array}|=3(1)-5(-2)=3+10=13.$$

A $3\;\times \;3$ determinant is defined in terms of $2\;\times \;2$ determinants as follows:

$$|\begin{array}{lll}a_{1} & a_{2} & a_{3} \\ b_{1} & b_{2} & b_{3} \\ c_{1} & c_{2} & c_{3}\end{array}|=a_{1}|\begin{array}{ll}b_{2} & b_{3} \\ c_{2} & c_{3}\end{array}|-a_{2}|\begin{array}{ll}b_{1} & b_{3} \\ c_{1} & c_{3}\end{array}|+a_{3}|\begin{array}{ll}b_{1} & b_{2} \\ c_{1} & c_{2}\end{array}|.\tag{12.10}$$

Equation 12.10 is referred to as the expansion of the determinant along the first row. Notice that the multipliers of each of the $2\;\times \;2$ determinants on the right side of this expression are the entries in the first row of the $3\;\times \;3$ determinant. Furthermore, each of the $2\;\times \;2$ determinants contains the entries from the $3\;\times \;3$ determinant that would remain if you crossed out the row and column containing the multiplier. Thus, for the first term on the right, $a_{1}$ is the multiplier, and the $2\;\times \;2$ determinant contains the entries that remain if you cross out the first row and first column of the $3\;\times \;3$ determinant. Similarly, for the second term, the multiplier is $a_{2},$ and the $2\;\times \;2$ determinant contains the entries that remain if you cross out the first row and second column of the $3\;\times \;3$ determinant. Notice, however, that the coefficient of the second term is negative. The third term can be calculated in similar fashion.

Example 12.36 · Using Expansion Along the First Row to Compute a $3\;\times \;3$ Determinant

Evaluate the determinant $|\begin{array}{rrr}2 & 5 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \\ -2 & 3 & 4\end{array}|.$

Solution

We have

$$\begin{array}{rl}|\begin{array}{rrr}2 & 5 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 & 3 \\ -2 & 3 & 4\end{array}| & =2|\begin{array}{ll}1 & 3 \\ 3 & 4\end{array}|-5|\begin{array}{ll}-1 & 3 \\ -2 & 4\end{array}|-1|\begin{array}{ll}-1 & 1 \\ -2 & 3\end{array}| \\ & =2(4-9)-5(-4+6)-1(-3+2) \\ & =2(-5)-5(2)-1(-1)=-10-10+1 \\ & =-19.\end{array}$$

Technically, determinants are defined only in terms of arrays of real numbers. However, the determinant notation provides a useful mnemonic device for the cross product formula.

Rule

Cross Product Calculated by a Determinant

Let $\kvecu=\langle u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\rangle$ and $\kvecv=\langle v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\rangle$ be vectors. Then the cross product $\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv $ is given by

$$\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv=|\begin{array}{lll}\kveci & \kvecj & \kveck \\ u_{1} & u_{2} & u_{3} \\ v_{1} & v_{2} & v_{3}\end{array}|=|\begin{array}{ll}u_{2} & u_{3} \\ v_{2} & v_{3}\end{array}|\kveci-|\begin{array}{ll}u_{1} & u_{3} \\ v_{1} & v_{3}\end{array}|\kvecj+|\begin{array}{ll}u_{1} & u_{2} \\ v_{1} & v_{2}\end{array}|\kveck.$$

Example 12.37 · Using Determinant Notation to find $\kvecp\;\times \;\kvecq $

Let $\kvecp=\langle -1,2,5\rangle$ and $\kvecq=\langle 4,0,-3\rangle .$ Find $\kvecp\;\times \;\kvecq.$

Solution

We set up our determinant by putting the standard unit vectors across the first row, the components of $\kvecu $ in the second row, and the components of $\kvecv $ in the third row. Then, we have

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecp\;\times \;\kvecq & =|\begin{array}{rrr}\kveci & \kvecj & \kveck \\ -1 & 2 & 5 \\ 4 & 0 & -3\end{array}|=|\begin{array}{rr}2 & 5 \\ 0 & -3\end{array}|\kveci-|\begin{array}{rr}-1 & 5 \\ 4 & -3\end{array}|\kvecj+|\begin{array}{rr}-1 & 2 \\ 4 & 0\end{array}|\kveck \\ & =(-6-0)\kveci-(3-20)\kvecj+(0-8)\kveck \\ & =-6\kveci+17\kvecj-8\kveck.\end{array}$$

Notice that this answer confirms the calculation of the cross product in Example 12.31.

Using the Cross Product

The cross product is very useful for several types of calculations, including finding a vector orthogonal to two given vectors, computing areas of triangles and parallelograms, and even determining the volume of the three-dimensional geometric shape made of parallelograms known as a parallelepiped. The following examples illustrate these calculations.

Example 12.38 · Finding a Unit Vector Orthogonal to Two Given Vectors

Let $\kveca=\langle 5,2,-1\rangle$ and $\kvecb=\langle 0,-1,4\rangle .$ Find a unit vector orthogonal to both $\kveca $ and $\kvecb.$

Solution

The cross product $\kveca\;\times \;\kvecb $ is orthogonal to both vectors $\kveca $ and $\kvecb.$ We can calculate it with a determinant:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kveca\;\times \;\kvecb & =|\begin{array}{rrr}\kveci & \kvecj & \kveck \\ 5 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 4\end{array}|=|\begin{array}{rr}2 & -1 \\ -1 & 4\end{array}|\kveci-|\begin{array}{rr}5 & -1 \\ 0 & 4\end{array}|\kvecj+|\begin{array}{rr}5 & 2 \\ 0 & -1\end{array}|\kveck \\ & =(8-1)\kveci-(20-0)\kvecj+(-5-0)\kveck \\ & =7\kveci-20\kvecj-5\kveck.\end{array}$$

Normalize this vector to find a unit vector in the same direction:

$$\| \kveca\;\times \;\kvecb\| =\sqrt{{(7)}^{2}+{(-20)}^{2}+{(-5)}^{2}}=\sqrt{474}.$$

Thus, $\langle \frac{7}{\sqrt{474}},\frac{-20}{\sqrt{474}},\frac{-5}{\sqrt{474}}\rangle$ is a unit vector orthogonal to $\kveca $ and $\kvecb.$

To use the cross product for calculating areas, we state and prove the following theorem.

Theorem

Area of a Parallelogram

If we locate vectors $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $ such that they form adjacent sides of a parallelogram, then the area of the parallelogram is given by $\| \kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv\|$ (Figure 12.57).

Proof

We show that the magnitude of the cross product is equal to the base times height of the parallelogram.

$$\begin{array}{rl}\text{Area of a parallelogram} & =\text{base}\;\times \;\text{height} \\ & =\| \kvecu\| (\| \kvecv\| \sin \ktheta ) \\ & =\| \kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv\| \end{array}$$

□

Example 12.39 · Finding the Area of a Triangle

Let $P=(1,0,0),Q=(0,1,0),\;\text{and}\;R=(0,0,1)$ be the vertices of a triangle (Figure 12.58). Find its area.

Solution

We have $\kPQ =\langle 0-1,1-0,0-0\rangle =\langle -1,1,0\rangle$ and $\vec{PR}=\langle 0-1,0-0,1-0\rangle =\langle -1,0,1\rangle .$ The area of the parallelogram with adjacent sides $\kPQ $ and $\vec{PR}$ is given by $\| \kPQ \;\times \;\vec{PR}\| \text{:}$

$$\begin{array}{rll}\kPQ \;\times \;\vec{PR} & = & |\begin{array}{rrr}\kveci & \kvecj & \kveck \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1\end{array}|=(1-0)\kveci-(-1-0)\kvecj+(0-(-1))\kveck=\kveci+\kvecj+\kveck \\ \| \kPQ \;\times \;\vec{PR}\| & = & \| \langle 1,1,1\rangle \| =\sqrt{1^{2}+1^{2}+1^{2}}=\sqrt{3}.\end{array}$$

The area of $\Delta PQR$ is half the area of the parallelogram, or $\sqrt{3}/2.$

The Triple Scalar Product

Because the cross product of two vectors is a vector, it is possible to combine the dot product and the cross product. The dot product of a vector with the cross product of two other vectors is called the triple scalar product because the result is a scalar.

Definition

The triple scalar product of vectors $\kvecu,$ $\kvecv,$ and $\kvecw $ is $\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw).$

Theorem

Calculating a Triple Scalar Product

The triple scalar product of vectors $\kvecu=u_{1}\kveci+u_{2}\kvecj+u_{3}\kveck,$ $\kvecv=v_{1}\kveci+v_{2}\kvecj+v_{3}\kveck,$ and $\kvecw=w_{1}\kveci+w_{2}\kvecj+w_{3}\kveck $ is the determinant of the $3\;\times \;3$ matrix formed by the components of the vectors:

$$\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw)=|\begin{array}{lll}u_{1} & u_{2} & u_{3} \\ v_{1} & v_{2} & v_{3} \\ w_{1} & w_{2} & w_{3}\end{array}|.$$

Proof

The calculation is straightforward.

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw) & =\langle u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\rangle \cdot \langle v_{2}w_{3}-v_{3}w_{2},-v_{1}w_{3}+v_{3}w_{1},v_{1}w_{2}-v_{2}w_{1}\rangle \\ & =u_{1}(v_{2}w_{3}-v_{3}w_{2})+u_{2}(-v_{1}w_{3}+v_{3}w_{1})+u_{3}(v_{1}w_{2}-v_{2}w_{1}) \\ & =u_{1}(v_{2}w_{3}-v_{3}w_{2})-u_{2}(v_{1}w_{3}-v_{3}w_{1})+u_{3}(v_{1}w_{2}-v_{2}w_{1}) \\ & =|\begin{array}{lll}u_{1} & u_{2} & u_{3} \\ v_{1} & v_{2} & v_{3} \\ w_{1} & w_{2} & w_{3}\end{array}|\end{array}$$

□

Example 12.40 · Calculating the Triple Scalar Product

Let $\kvecu=\langle 1,3,5\rangle ,\kvecv=\langle 2,-1,0\rangle \;\text{and}\;\kvecw=\langle -3,0,-1\rangle .$ Calculate the triple scalar product $\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw).$

Solution

Apply Calculating a Triple Scalar Product directly:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw) & =|\begin{array}{rrr}1 & 3 & 5 \\ 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -3 & 0 & -1\end{array}| \\ & =1|\begin{array}{rr}-1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1\end{array}|-3|\begin{array}{rr}2 & 0 \\ -3 & -1\end{array}|+5|\begin{array}{rr}2 & -1 \\ -3 & 0\end{array}| \\ & =(1-0)-3(-2-0)+5(0-3) \\ & =1+6-15=-8.\end{array}$$

When we create a matrix from three vectors, we must be careful about the order in which we list the vectors. If we list them in a matrix in one order and then rearrange the rows, the absolute value of the determinant remains unchanged. However, each time two rows switch places, the determinant changes sign:

$$|\begin{array}{lll}a_{1} & a_{2} & a_{3} \\ b_{1} & b_{2} & b_{3} \\ c_{1} & c_{2} & c_{3}\end{array}|=d\hspace{2em}|\begin{array}{lll}b_{1} & b_{2} & b_{3} \\ a_{1} & a_{2} & a_{3} \\ c_{1} & c_{2} & c_{3}\end{array}|=-d\hspace{2em}|\begin{array}{lll}b_{1} & b_{2} & b_{3} \\ c_{1} & c_{2} & c_{3} \\ a_{1} & a_{2} & a_{3}\end{array}|=d\hspace{2em}|\begin{array}{lll}c_{1} & c_{2} & c_{3} \\ b_{1} & b_{2} & b_{3} \\ a_{1} & a_{2} & a_{3}\end{array}|=-d.$$

Verifying this fact is straightforward, but rather messy. Let’s take a look at this with an example:

$$\begin{array}{rl}|\begin{array}{rlr}1 & 2 & 1 \\ -2 & 0 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 & -1\end{array}| & =|\begin{array}{lr}0 & 3 \\ 1 & -1\end{array}|-2|\begin{array}{rr}-2 & 3 \\ 4 & -1\end{array}|+|\begin{array}{rl}-2 & 0 \\ 4 & 1\end{array}| \\ & =(0-3)-2(2-12)+(-2-0)=-3+20-2=15.\end{array}$$

Switching the top two rows we have

$$|\begin{array}{rlr}-2 & 0 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 & -1\end{array}|=-2|\begin{array}{lr}2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1\end{array}|+3|\begin{array}{ll}1 & 2 \\ 4 & 1\end{array}|=-2(-2-1)+3(1-8)=6-21=-15.$$

Rearranging vectors in the triple products is equivalent to reordering the rows in the matrix of the determinant. Let $\kvecu=u_{1}\kveci+u_{2}\kvecj+u_{3}\kveck,$ $\kvecv=v_{1}\kveci+v_{2}\kvecj+v_{3}\kveck,$ and $\kvecw=w_{1}\kveci+w_{2}\kvecj+w_{3}\kveck.$ Applying Calculating a Triple Scalar Product, we have

$$\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw)=|\begin{array}{lll}u_{1} & u_{2} & u_{3} \\ v_{1} & v_{2} & v_{3} \\ w_{1} & w_{2} & w_{3}\end{array}|\;\text{and}\;\kvecu\cdot (\kvecw\;\times \;\kvecv)=|\begin{array}{lll}u_{1} & u_{2} & u_{3} \\ w_{1} & w_{2} & w_{3} \\ v_{1} & v_{2} & v_{3}\end{array}|.$$

We can obtain the determinant for calculating $\kvecu\cdot (\kvecw\;\times \;\kvecv)$ by switching the bottom two rows of $\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw).$ Therefore, $\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw)=-\kvecu\cdot (\kvecw\;\times \;\kvecv).$

Following this reasoning and exploring the different ways we can interchange variables in the triple scalar product lead to the following identities:

$$\begin{array}{rll}\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw) & = & -\kvecu\cdot (\kvecw\;\times \;\kvecv) \\ \kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw) & = & \kvecv\cdot (\kvecw\;\times \;\kvecu)=\kvecw\cdot (\kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv).\end{array}$$

Let $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $ be two vectors in standard position. If $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $ are not scalar multiples of each other, then these vectors form adjacent sides of a parallelogram. We saw in Area of a Parallelogram that the area of this parallelogram is $\| \kvecu\;\times \;\kvecv\| .$ Now suppose we add a third vector $\kvecw $ that does not lie in the same plane as $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $ but still shares the same initial point. Then these vectors form three edges of a parallelepiped, a three-dimensional prism with six faces that are each parallelograms, as shown in Figure 12.59. The volume of this prism is the product of the figure’s height and the area of its base. The triple scalar product of $\kvecu,\kvecv,$ and $\kvecw $ provides a simple method for calculating the volume of the parallelepiped defined by these vectors.

Theorem

Volume of a Parallelepiped

The volume of a parallelepiped with adjacent edges given by the vectors $\kvecu,\kvecv,\;\text{and}\;\kvecw $ is the absolute value of the triple scalar product:

$$V=|\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw)|.$$

See Figure 12.59.

Note that, as the name indicates, the triple scalar product produces a scalar. The volume formula just presented uses the absolute value of a scalar quantity.

Proof

The area of the base of the parallelepiped is given by $\| \kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw\| .$ The height of the figure is given by $\| {\text{proj}}_{\text{v×w}}\kvecu\| .$ The volume of the parallelepiped is the product of the height and the area of the base, so we have

$$\begin{array}{rl}V & =\| {\text{proj}}_{\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw}\kvecu\| \| \kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw\| \\ & =|\frac{\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw)}{\| \kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw\|}|\| \kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw\| \\ & =|\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw)|.\end{array}$$

□

Example 12.41 · Calculating the Volume of a Parallelepiped

Let $\kvecu=\langle -1,-2,1\rangle ,\kvecv=\langle 4,3,2\rangle ,\;\text{and}\;\kvecw=\langle 0,-5,-2\rangle .$ Find the volume of the parallelepiped with adjacent edges $\kvecu,\kvecv,\;\text{and}\;\kvecw $ (Figure 12.60).

Solution

We have

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw) & =|\begin{array}{rrr}-1 & -2 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 2 \\ 0 & -5 & -2\end{array}|=(-1)|\begin{array}{rr}3 & 2 \\ -5 & -2\end{array}|+2|\begin{array}{rr}4 & 2 \\ 0 & -2\end{array}|+|\begin{array}{rr}4 & 3 \\ 0 & -5\end{array}| \\ & =(-1)(-6+10)+2(-8-0)+(-20-0) \\ & =-4-16-20 \\ & =-40.\end{array}$$

Thus, the volume of the parallelepiped is $|-40|=40$ units3.

Applications of the Cross Product

The cross product appears in many practical applications in mathematics, physics, and engineering. Let’s examine some of these applications here, including the idea of torque, with which we began this section. Other applications show up in later chapters, particularly in our study of vector fields such as gravitational and electromagnetic fields (Introduction to Vector Calculus).

Example 12.42 · Using the Triple Scalar Product

Use the triple scalar product to show that vectors $\kvecu=\langle 2,0,5\rangle ,\kvecv=\langle 2,2,4\rangle ,\;\text{and}\;\kvecw=\langle 1,-1,3\rangle$ are coplanar—that is, show that these vectors lie in the same plane.

Solution

Start by calculating the triple scalar product to find the volume of the parallelepiped defined by $\kvecu,\kvecv,\;\text{and}\;\kvecw\text{:}$

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecu\cdot (\kvecv\;\times \;\kvecw) & =|\begin{array}{rrr}2 & 0 & 5 \\ 2 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & -1 & 3\end{array}| \\ & =[2(2)(3)+(0)(4)(1)+5(2)(-1)]-[5(2)(1)+(2)(4)(-1)+(0)(2)(3)] \\ & =2-2 \\ & =0.\end{array}$$

The volume of the parallelepiped is $0$ units3, so one of the dimensions must be zero. Therefore, the three vectors all lie in the same plane.

Example 12.43 · Finding an Orthogonal Vector

Only a single plane can pass through any set of three noncolinear points. Find a vector orthogonal to the plane containing points $P=(9,-3,-2),Q=(1,3,0),$ and $R=(-2,5,0).$

Solution

The plane must contain vectors $\kPQ $ and $\vec{QR}\text{:}$

$$\begin{array}{l}\kPQ =\langle 1-9,3-(-3),0-(-2)\rangle =\langle -8,6,2\rangle \\ \vec{QR}=\langle -2-1,5-3,0-0\rangle =\langle -3,2,0\rangle .\end{array}$$

The cross product $\kPQ \;\times \;\vec{QR}$ produces a vector orthogonal to both $\kPQ $ and $\vec{QR}.$ Therefore, the cross product is orthogonal to the plane that contains these two vectors:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kPQ \;\times \;\vec{QR} & =|\begin{array}{rrr}\kveci & \kvecj & \kveck \\ -8 & 6 & 2 \\ -3 & 2 & 0\end{array}| \\ & =0\kveci-6\kvecj-16\kveck-(-18\kveck+4\kveci+0\kvecj) \\ & =-4\kveci-6\kvecj+2\kveck.\end{array}$$

We have seen how to use the triple scalar product and how to find a vector orthogonal to a plane. Now we apply the cross product to real-world situations.

Sometimes a force causes an object to rotate. For example, turning a screwdriver or a wrench creates this kind of rotational effect, called torque.

Definition

Torque, $\ktau$ (the Greek letter tau), measures the tendency of a force to produce rotation about an axis of rotation. Let $\kvecr $ be a vector with an initial point located on the axis of rotation and with a terminal point located at the point where the force is applied, and let vector $\kvecF $ represent the force. Then torque is equal to the cross product of $\kvecr $ and $\kvecF\text{:}$

$$\ktau=\kvecr\;\times \;\kvecF.$$

See Figure 12.61.

Think about using a wrench to tighten a bolt. The torque $\ktau$ applied to the bolt depends on how hard we push the wrench (force) and how far up the handle we apply the force (distance). The torque increases with a greater force on the wrench at a greater distance from the bolt. Common units of torque are the newton-meter or foot-pound. Although torque is dimensionally equivalent to work (it has the same units), the two concepts are distinct. Torque is used specifically in the context of rotation, whereas work typically involves motion along a line.

Example 12.44 · Evaluating Torque

A bolt is tightened by applying a force of $6$ N to a 0.15-m wrench (Figure 12.62). The angle between the wrench and the force vector is $40^{\circ}.$ Find the magnitude of the torque about the center of the bolt. Round the answer to two decimal places.

Solution

Substitute the given information into the equation defining torque:

$$\| \ktau\| =\| \kvecr\;\times \;\kvecF\| =\| \kvecr\| \| \kvecF\| \sin \ktheta =(0.15\;\text{m})(6\;\text{N})\sin 40^{\circ}\approx 0.58\;\text{N}\cdot \text{m}.$$
