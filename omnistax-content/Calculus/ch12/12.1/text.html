When describing the movement of an airplane in flight, it is important to communicate two pieces of information: the direction in which the plane is traveling and the plane’s speed. When measuring a force, such as the thrust of the plane’s engines, it is important to describe not only the strength of that force, but also the direction in which it is applied. Some quantities, such as velocity or force, are defined in terms of both size (also called magnitude) and direction. A quantity that has magnitude and direction is called a vector. In this text, we denote vectors by boldface letters, such as $\kvecv$.

Definition

A vector is a quantity that has both magnitude and direction.

Vector Representation

A vector in a plane is represented by a directed line segment (an arrow). The endpoints of the segment are called the initial point and the terminal point of the vector. An arrow from the initial point to the terminal point indicates the direction of the vector. The length of the line segment represents its magnitude. We use the notation $\| \kvecv\|$ to denote the magnitude of the vector $\kvecv.$ A vector with an initial point and terminal point that are the same is called the zero vector, denoted $\kveczero.$ The zero vector is the only vector without a direction, and by convention can be considered to have any direction convenient to the problem at hand.

Vectors with the same magnitude and direction are called equivalent vectors. We treat equivalent vectors as equal, even if they have different initial points. Thus, if $\kvecv $ and $\kvecw $ are equivalent, we write

$$\kvecv=\kvecw.$$
Definition

Vectors are said to be equivalent vectors if they have the same magnitude and direction.

The arrows in Figure 12.2(b) are equivalent. Each arrow has the same length and direction. A closely related concept is the idea of parallel vectors. Two vectors are said to be parallel if they have the same or opposite directions. We explore this idea in more detail later in the chapter. A vector is defined by its magnitude and direction, regardless of where its initial point is located.

The use of boldface, lowercase letters to name vectors is a common representation in print, but there are alternative notations. When writing the name of a vector by hand, for example, it is easier to sketch an arrow over the variable than to simulate boldface type: $\vec{v}.$ When a vector has initial point $P$ and terminal point $Q,$ the notation $\kPQ $ is useful because it indicates the direction and location of the vector.

Combining Vectors

Vectors have many real-life applications, including situations involving force or velocity. For example, consider the forces acting on a boat crossing a river. The boat’s motor generates a force in one direction, and the current of the river generates a force in another direction. Both forces are vectors. We must take both the magnitude and direction of each force into account if we want to know where the boat will go.

A second example that involves vectors is a quarterback throwing a football. The quarterback does not throw the ball parallel to the ground; instead, he aims up into the air. The velocity of his throw can be represented by a vector. If we know how hard he throws the ball (magnitude—in this case, speed), and the angle (direction), we can tell how far the ball will travel down the field.

A real number is often called a scalar in mathematics and physics. Unlike vectors, scalars are generally considered to have a magnitude only, but no direction. Multiplying a vector by a scalar changes the vector’s magnitude. This is called scalar multiplication. Note that changing the magnitude of a vector does not indicate a change in its direction. For example, wind blowing from north to south might increase or decrease in speed while maintaining its direction from north to south.

Definition

The product $k\kvecv $ of a vector $\kvecv$ and a scalar k is a vector with a magnitude that is $|k|$ times the magnitude of $\kvecv,$ and with a direction that is the same as the direction of $\kvecv $ if $k\gt 0,$ and opposite the direction of $\kvecv $ if $k\lt 0.$ This is called scalar multiplication. If $k=0$ or $\kvecv=\kveczero ,$ then $k\kvecv=\kveczero.$

As you might expect, if $k=-1,$ we denote the product $k\kvecv $ as

$$k\kvecv=(-1)\kvecv=-\kvecv.$$

Note that $-\kvecv $ has the same magnitude as $\kvecv,$ but has the opposite direction (Figure 12.4).

Another operation we can perform on vectors is to add them together in vector addition, but because each vector may have its own direction, the process is different from adding two numbers. The most common graphical method for adding two vectors is to place the initial point of the second vector at the terminal point of the first, as in Figure 12.5(a). To see why this makes sense, suppose, for example, that both vectors represent displacement. If an object moves first from the initial point to the terminal point of vector $\kvecv,$ then from the initial point to the terminal point of vector $\kvecw,$ the overall displacement is the same as if the object had made just one movement from the initial point to the terminal point of the vector $\kvecv+\kvecw.$ For obvious reasons, this approach is called the triangle method. Notice that if we had switched the order, so that $\kvecw $ was our first vector and $\kvecv$ was our second vector, we would have ended up in the same place. (Again, see Figure 12.5(a).) Thus, $\kvecv+\kvecw=\kvecw+\kvecv.$

A second method for adding vectors is called the parallelogram method. With this method, we place the two vectors so they have the same initial point, and then we draw a parallelogram with the vectors as two adjacent sides, as in Figure 12.5(b). The length of the diagonal of the parallelogram is the sum. Comparing Figure 12.5(b) and Figure 12.5(a), we can see that we get the same answer using either method. The vector $\kvecv+\kvecw $ is called the vector sum.

Definition

The sum of two vectors $\kvecv $ and $\kvecw $ can be constructed graphically by placing the initial point of $\kvecw $ at the terminal point of $\kvecv.$ Then, the vector sum, $\kvecv+\kvecw,$ is the vector with an initial point that coincides with the initial point of $\kvecv $ and has a terminal point that coincides with the terminal point of $\kvecw.$ This operation is known as vector addition.

It is also appropriate here to discuss vector subtraction. We define $\kvecv-\kvecw $ as $\kvecv+(-\kvecw)=\kvecv+(-1)\kvecw.$ The vector $\kvecv-\kvecw $ is called the vector difference. Graphically, the vector $\kvecv-\kvecw $ is depicted by drawing a vector from the terminal point of $\kvecw $ to the terminal point of $\kvecv $ (Figure 12.6).

In Figure 12.5(a), the initial point of $\kvecv+\kvecw $ is the initial point of $\kvecv.$ The terminal point of $\kvecv+\kvecw $ is the terminal point of $\kvecw.$ These three vectors form the sides of a triangle. It follows that the length of any one side is less than the sum of the lengths of the remaining sides. So we have

$$\| \kvecv+\kvecw\| \le \| \kvecv\| +\| \kvecw\| .$$

This is known more generally as the triangle inequality. There is one case, however, when the resultant vector $\kvecu+\kvecv $ has the same magnitude as the sum of the magnitudes of $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv.$ This happens only when $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $ have the same direction.

Vector Components

Working with vectors in a plane is easier when we are working in a coordinate system. When the initial points and terminal points of vectors are given in Cartesian coordinates, computations become straightforward.

We have seen how to plot a vector when we are given an initial point and a terminal point. However, because a vector can be placed anywhere in a plane, it may be easier to perform calculations with a vector when its initial point coincides with the origin. We call a vector with its initial point at the origin a standard-position vector. Because the initial point of any vector in standard position is known to be $(0,0),$ we can describe the vector by looking at the coordinates of its terminal point. Thus, if vector $\kvecv$ has its initial point at the origin and its terminal point at $(x,y),$ we write the vector in component form as

$$\kvecv=\langle x,y\rangle .$$

When a vector is written in component form like this, the scalars x and y are called the components of $\kvecv.$

Definition

The vector with initial point $(0,0)$ and terminal point $(x,y)$ can be written in component form as

$$\kvecv=\langle x,y\rangle .$$

The scalars $x$ and $y$ are called the components of $\kvecv.$

Recall that vectors are named with lowercase letters in bold type or by drawing an arrow over their name. We have also learned that we can name a vector by its component form, with the coordinates of its terminal point in angle brackets. However, when writing the component form of a vector, it is important to distinguish between $\langle x,y\rangle$ and $(x,y).$ The first ordered pair uses angle brackets to describe a vector, whereas the second uses parentheses to describe a point in a plane. The initial point of $\langle x,y\rangle$ is $(0,0);$ the terminal point of $\langle x,y\rangle$ is $(x,y).$

When we have a vector not already in standard position, we can determine its component form in one of two ways. We can use a geometric approach, in which we sketch the vector in the coordinate plane, and then sketch an equivalent standard-position vector. Alternatively, we can find it algebraically, using the coordinates of the initial point and the terminal point. To find it algebraically, we subtract the x-coordinate of the initial point from the x-coordinate of the terminal point to get the x component, and we subtract the y-coordinate of the initial point from the y-coordinate of the terminal point to get the y component.

Rule

Component Form of a Vector

Let $\kvecv$ be a vector with initial point $(x_{i},y_{i})$ and terminal point $(x_{t},y_{t}).$ Then we can express $\kvecv$ in component form as $\kvecv=\langle x_{t}-x_{i},y_{t}-y_{i}\rangle .$

Example 12.4 · Expressing Vectors in Component Form

Express vector $\kvecv $ with initial point $(-3,4)$ and terminal point $(1,2)$ in component form.

Solution

a. Geometric

  1. Sketch the vector in the coordinate plane (Figure 12.12).
  2. The terminal point is 4 units to the right and 2 units down from the initial point.
  3. Find the point that is 4 units to the right and 2 units down from the origin.
  4. In standard position, this vector has initial point $(0,0)$ and terminal point $(4,-2)\text{:}$
$$\kvecv=\langle 4,-2\rangle .$$

b. Algebraic

In the first solution, we used a sketch of the vector to see that the terminal point lies 4 units to the right. We can accomplish this algebraically by finding the difference of the x-coordinates:

$$x_{t}-x_{i}=1-(-3)=4.$$

Similarly, the difference of the y-coordinates shows the vertical length of the vector.

$$y_{t}-y_{i}=2-4=-2.$$

So, in component form,

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecv & =\langle x_{t}-x_{i},y_{t}-y_{i}\rangle \\ & =\langle 1-(-3),2-4\rangle \\ & =\langle 4,-2\rangle .\end{array}$$

To find the magnitude of a vector, we calculate the distance between its initial point and its terminal point. The magnitude of vector $\kvecv=\langle x,y\rangle$ is denoted $\| \kvecv\| ,$ or $|\kvecv |,$ and can be computed using the formula

$$\| \kvecv\| =\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}.$$

Note that because this vector is written in component form, it is equivalent to a vector in standard position, with its initial point at the origin and terminal point $(x,y).$ Thus, it suffices to calculate the magnitude of the vector in standard position. We use the distance formula to calculate the distance between initial point $(0,0)$ and terminal point $(x,y).$

Based on this formula, it is clear that for any vector $\kvecv,$ $\| \kvecv\| \ge 0,$ and $\| \kvecv\| =0$ if and only if $\kvecv=\kveczero .$

Example 12.5 · Performing Operations in Component Form

Let $\kvecv $ be the vector with initial point $(2,5)$ and terminal point $(8,13),$ and let $\kvecw=\langle -2,4\rangle .$

a. Express $\kvecv $ in component form and find $\| \kvecv\| .$ Then, using algebra, find

b. $\kvecv+\kvecw,$

c. $3\kvecv,$ and

d. $\kvecv-2\kvecw.$

Solution

a. To place the initial point of $\kvecv $ at the origin, we must translate the vector $2$ units to the left and $5$ units down (Figure 12.15). Using the algebraic method, we can express $\kvecv $ as $\kvecv=\langle 8-2,13-5\rangle =\langle 6,8\rangle \text{:}$

$$\| \kvecv\| =\sqrt{6^{2}+8^{2}}=\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}=10.$$

b. To find $\kvecv+\kvecw,$ add the x-components and the y-components separately:

$$\kvecv+\kvecw=\langle 6,8\rangle +\langle -2,4\rangle =\langle 4,12\rangle .$$

c. To find $3\kvecv,$ multiply $\kvecv $ by the scalar $k=3\text{:}$

$$3\kvecv=3\cdot \langle 6,8\rangle =\langle 3\cdot 6,3\cdot 8\rangle =\langle 18,24\rangle .$$

d. To find $\kvecv-2\kvecw,$ find $-2\kvecw $ and add it to $\kvecv\text{:}$

$$\kvecv-2\kvecw=\langle 6,8\rangle -2\cdot \langle -2,4\rangle =\langle 6,8\rangle +\langle 4,-8\rangle =\langle 10,0\rangle .$$

Unit Vectors

A unit vector is a vector with magnitude $1.$ For any nonzero vector $\kvecv,$ we can use scalar multiplication to find a unit vector $\kvecu $ that has the same direction as $\kvecv.$ To do this, we multiply the vector by the reciprocal of its magnitude:

$$\kvecu=\frac{1}{\| \kvecv\|}\kvecv.$$

Recall that when we defined scalar multiplication, we noted that $\| k\kvecv\| =|k|\cdot \| \kvecv\| .$ For $\kvecu=\frac{1}{\| \kvecv\|}\kvecv,$ it follows that $\| \kvecu\| =\frac{1}{\| \kvecv\|}(\| \kvecv\| )=1.$ We say that $\kvecu $ is the unit vector in the direction of$\kvecv $ (Figure 12.17). The process of using scalar multiplication to find a unit vector with a given direction is called normalization.

Example 12.7 · Finding a Unit Vector

Let $\kvecv=\langle 1,2\rangle .$

a. Find a unit vector with the same direction as $\kvecv.$

b. Find a vector $\kvecw $ with the same direction as $\kvecv $ such that $\| \kvecw\| =7.$

Solution

a. First, find the magnitude of $\kvecv,$ then divide the components of $\kvecv $ by the magnitude:

$$\| \kvecv\| =\sqrt{1^{2}+2^{2}}=\sqrt{1+4}=\sqrt{5}$$ $$\kvecu=\frac{1}{\| \kvecv\|}\kvecv=\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\langle 1,2\rangle =\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}},\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\rangle .$$

b. The vector $\kvecu $ is in the same direction as $\kvecv $ and $\| \kvecu\| =1.$ Use scalar multiplication to increase the length of $\kvecu $ without changing direction:

$$\kvecw=7\kvecu=7\langle \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}},\frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\rangle =\langle \frac{7}{\sqrt{5}},\frac{14}{\sqrt{5}}\rangle .$$

Applications of Vectors

Because vectors have both direction and magnitude, they are valuable tools for solving problems involving such applications as motion and force. Recall the boat example and the quarterback example we described earlier. Here we look at two other examples in detail.

Example 12.9 · Finding Resultant Force

Jane’s car is stuck in the mud. Lisa and Jed come along in a truck to help pull her out. They attach one end of a tow strap to the front of the car and the other end to the truck’s trailer hitch, and the truck starts to pull. Meanwhile, Jane and Jed get behind the car and push. The truck generates a horizontal force of $300$ lb on the car. Jane and Jed are pushing at a slight upward angle and generate a force of $150$ lb on the car. These forces can be represented by vectors, as shown in Figure 12.21. The angle between these vectors is $15^{\circ}.$ Find the resultant force (the vector sum) and give its magnitude to the nearest tenth of a pound and its direction angle from the positive x-axis.

Solution

To find the effect of combining the two forces, add their representative vectors. First, express each vector in component form or in terms of the standard unit vectors. For this purpose, it is easiest if we align one of the vectors with the positive x-axis. The horizontal vector, then, has initial point $(0,0)$ and terminal point $(300,0).$ It can be expressed as $\langle 300,0\rangle$ or $300\kveci.$

The second vector has magnitude $150$ and makes an angle of $15^{\circ}$ with the first, so we can express it as $\langle 150\;\cos (15^{\circ}),150\;\sin (15^{\circ})\rangle ,$ or $150\;\cos (15^{\circ})\kveci+150\;\sin (15^{\circ})\kvecj.$ Then, the sum of the vectors, or resultant vector, is $\kvecr=\langle 300,0\rangle +\langle 150\;\cos (15^{\circ}),150\;\sin (15^{\circ})\rangle ,$ and we have

$$\begin{array}{rl}\| \kvecr\| & =\sqrt{{(300+150\;\cos (15^{\circ}))}^{2}+{(150\;\sin (15^{\circ}))}^{2}} \\ & \approx 446.6.\end{array}$$

The angle $\ktheta$ made by $\kvecr $ and the positive x-axis has $\tan \ktheta =\frac{150\;\sin 15^{\circ}}{(300+150\;\cos 15^{\circ})}\approx 0.09,$ so $\ktheta \approx tan^{-1}(0.09)\approx 5^{\circ},$ which means the resultant force $\kvecr $ has an angle of $5^{\circ}$ above the horizontal axis.

Example 12.10 · Finding Resultant Velocity

An airplane flies due west at an airspeed of $425$ mph. The wind is blowing from the northeast at $40$ mph. What is the ground speed of the airplane? What is the bearing of the airplane?

Solution

Let’s start by sketching the situation described (Figure 12.22).

Set up a sketch so that the initial points of the vectors lie at the origin. Then, the plane’s velocity vector is $\kvecp=-425\kveci.$ The vector describing the wind makes an angle of $225^{\circ}$ with the positive x-axis:

$$\kvecw=\langle 40\;\cos (225^{\circ}),40\;\sin (225^{\circ})\rangle =\langle -\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}},-\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}}\rangle =-\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}}\kveci-\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}}\kvecj.$$

When the airspeed and the wind act together on the plane, we can add their vectors to find the resultant force:

$$\kvecp+\kvecw=-425\kveci+(-\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}}\kveci-\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}}\kvecj)=(-425-\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}})\kveci-\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}}\kvecj.$$

The magnitude of the resultant vector shows the effect of the wind on the ground speed of the airplane:

$$\| \kvecp+\kvecw\| =\sqrt{{(-425-\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}})}^{2}+{(-\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}})}^{2}}\approx 454.17\;\text{mph}$$

As a result of the wind, the plane is traveling at approximately $454$ mph relative to the ground.

To determine the bearing of the airplane, we want to find the direction of the vector $\kvecp+\kvecw\text{:}$

$$\begin{array}{rll}\tan \ktheta & = & \frac{-\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}}}{(-425-\frac{40}{\sqrt{2}})}\approx 0.06 \\ \ktheta & \approx & 3.57^{\circ}.\end{array}$$

The overall direction of the plane is $3.57^{\circ}$ south of west.
