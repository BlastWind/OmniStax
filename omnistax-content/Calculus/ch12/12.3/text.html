If we apply a force to an object so that the object moves, we say that work is done by the force. In Introduction to Applications of Integration on integration applications, we looked at a constant force and we assumed the force was applied in the direction of motion of the object. Under those conditions, work can be expressed as the product of the force acting on an object and the distance the object moves. In this chapter, however, we have seen that both force and the motion of an object can be represented by vectors.

In this section, we develop an operation called the dot product, which allows us to calculate work in the case when the force vector and the motion vector have different directions. The dot product essentially tells us how much of the force vector is applied in the direction of the motion vector. The dot product can also help us measure the angle formed by a pair of vectors and the position of a vector relative to the coordinate axes. It even provides a simple test to determine whether two vectors meet at a right angle.

The Dot Product and Its Properties

We have already learned how to add and subtract vectors. In this chapter, we investigate two types of vector multiplication. The first type of vector multiplication is called the dot product, based on the notation we use for it, and it is defined as follows:

Definition

The dot product of vectors $\kvecu=\langle u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\rangle$ and $\kvecv=\langle v_{1},v_{2},v_{3}\rangle$ is given by the sum of the products of the components

$$\kvecu\cdot \kvecv=u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}+u_{3}v_{3}.\tag{12.3}$$

Note that if $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $ are two-dimensional vectors, we calculate the dot product in a similar fashion. Thus, if $\kvecu=\langle u_{1},u_{2}\rangle$ and $\kvecv=\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle ,$ then

$$\kvecu\cdot \kvecv=u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}.$$

When two vectors are combined under addition or subtraction, the result is a vector. When two vectors are combined using the dot product, the result is a scalar. For this reason, the dot product is often called the scalar product. It may also be called the inner product.

Example 12.21 · Calculating Dot Products

a. Find the dot product of $\kvecu=\langle 3,5,2\rangle$ and $\kvecv=\langle -1,3,0\rangle .$

b. Find the scalar product of $\kvecp=10\kveci-4\kvecj+7\kveck $ and $\kvecq=-2\kveci+\kvecj+6\kveck.$

Solution

a. Substitute the vector components into the formula for the dot product:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecu\cdot \kvecv & =u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}+u_{3}v_{3} \\ & =3(-1)+5(3)+2(0)=-3+15+0=12.\end{array}$$

b. The calculation is the same if the vectors are written using standard unit vectors. We still have three components for each vector to substitute into the formula for the dot product:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecp\cdot \kvecq & =u_{1}v_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}+u_{3}v_{3} \\ & =10(-2)+(-4)(1)+(7)(6)=-20-4+42=18.\end{array}$$

Like vector addition and subtraction, the dot product has several algebraic properties. We prove three of these properties and leave the rest as exercises.

Theorem

Properties of the Dot Product

Let $\kvecu,$ $\kvecv,$ and $\kvecw $ be vectors, and let c be a scalar.

$$\begin{array}{lllrlllll}\text{i.} & & & \kvecu\cdot \kvecv & = & \kvecv\cdot \kvecu & & & \text{Commutative property} \\ \text{ii.} & & & \kvecu\cdot (\kvecv+\kvecw) & = & \kvecu\cdot \kvecv+\kvecu\cdot \kvecw & & & \text{Distributive property} \\ \text{iii.} & & & c(\kvecu\cdot \kvecv) & = & (c\kvecu)\cdot \kvecv=\kvecu\cdot (c\kvecv) & & & \text{Associative property} \\ \text{iv.} & & & \kvecv\cdot \kvecv & = & {\| \kvecv\|}^{2} & & & \text{Property of magnitude}\end{array}$$

Proof

The associative property looks like the associative property for real-number multiplication, but pay close attention to the difference between scalar and vector objects. The proof that $c(\kvecu\cdot \kvecv)=\kvecu\cdot (c\kvecv)$ is similar.

The fourth property shows the relationship between the magnitude of a vector and its dot product with itself.

Note that the definition of the dot product yields $\kveczero\cdot \kvecv=0.$ By property iv., if $\kvecv\cdot \kvecv=0,$ then $\kvecv=\kveczero.$

Example 12.22 · Using Properties of the Dot Product

Let $\kveca=\langle 1,2,-3\rangle ,$ $\kvecb=\langle 0,2,4\rangle ,$ and $\kvecc=\langle 5,-1,3\rangle .$ Find each of the following products.

a. $(\kveca\cdot \kvecb)\kvecc $

b. $\kveca\cdot (2\kvecc)$

c. ${\| \kvecb\|}^{2}$

Solution

a. Note that this expression asks for the scalar multiple of $\kvecc$ by $\kveca\cdot \kvecb\text{:}$

$$\begin{array}{rl}(\kveca\cdot \kvecb)\kvecc & =(\langle 1,2,-3\rangle \cdot \langle 0,2,4\rangle )\langle 5,-1,3\rangle \\ & =(1(0)+2(2)+(-3)(4))\langle 5,-1,3\rangle \\ & =-8\langle 5,-1,3\rangle \\ & =\langle -40,8,-24\rangle .\end{array}$$

b. This expression is a dot product of vector $\kveca$ and scalar multiple 2$\kvecc$:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kveca\cdot (2\kvecc) & =2(\kveca\cdot \kvecc) \\ & =2(\langle 1,2,-3\rangle \cdot \langle 5,-1,3\rangle ) \\ & =2(1(5)+2(-1)+(-3)(3)) \\ & =2(-6)=-12.\end{array}$$

c. Simplifying this expression is a straightforward application of the dot product:

$${\| \kvecb\|}^{2}=\kvecb\cdot \kvecb=\langle 0,2,4\rangle \cdot \langle 0,2,4\rangle =0^{2}+2^{2}+4^{2}=0+4+16=20.$$

Using the Dot Product to Find the Angle between Two Vectors

When two nonzero vectors are placed in standard position, whether in two dimensions or three dimensions, they form an angle between them (Figure 12.44). The dot product provides a way to find the measure of this angle. This property is a result of the fact that we can express the dot product in terms of the cosine of the angle formed by two vectors.

Theorem

Evaluating a Dot Product

The dot product of two vectors is the product of the magnitude of each vector and the cosine of the angle between them:

$$\kvecu\cdot \kvecv=\| \kvecu\| \| \kvecv\| \cos \ktheta .\tag{12.4}$$

Proof

We can use this form of the dot product to find the measure of the angle between two nonzero vectors. The following equation rearranges Equation 12.3 to solve for the cosine of the angle:

$$\cos \ktheta =\frac{\kvecu\cdot \kvecv}{\| \kvecu\| \| \kvecv\|}.\tag{12.5}$$

Using this equation, we can find the cosine of the angle between two nonzero vectors. Since we are considering the smallest angle between the vectors, we assume $0^{\circ}\le \ktheta \le 180^{\circ}$ (or $0\le \ktheta \le \pi$ if we are working in radians). The inverse cosine is unique over this range, so we are then able to determine the measure of the angle $\ktheta .$

Example 12.23 · Finding the Angle between Two Vectors

Find the measure of the angle between each pair of vectors.

a. $\kveci$ + $\kvecj$ + $\kveck$ and 2$\kveci$ – $\kvecj$ – 3$\kveck$

b. $\langle 2,5,6\rangle$ and $\langle -2,-4,4\rangle$

Solution

a. To find the cosine of the angle formed by the two vectors, substitute the components of the vectors into Equation 12.5:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\cos \ktheta & =\frac{(\kveci+\kvecj+\kveck)\cdot (2\kveci-\kvecj-3\kveck)}{\| \kveci+\kvecj+\kveck\| \cdot \| 2\kveci-\kvecj-3\kveck\|} \\ & =\frac{1(2)+(1)(-1)+(1)(-3)}{\sqrt{1^{2}+1^{2}+1^{2}}\;\sqrt{2^{2}+{(-1)}^{2}+{(-3)}^{2}}} \\ & =\frac{-2}{\sqrt{3}\;\sqrt{14}}=\frac{-2}{\sqrt{42}}.\end{array}$$

Therefore, $\ktheta =\operatorname{arccos}\;\frac{-2}{\sqrt{42}}$.

b. Start by finding the value of the cosine of the angle between the vectors:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\cos \ktheta & =\frac{\langle 2,5,6\rangle \cdot \langle -2,-4,4\rangle}{\| \langle 2,5,6\rangle \| \cdot \| \langle -2,-4,4\rangle \|} \\ & =\frac{2(-2)+(5)(-4)+(6)(4)}{\sqrt{2^{2}+5^{2}+6^{2}}\;\sqrt{{(-2)}^{2}+{(-4)}^{2}+4^{2}}} \\ & =\frac{0}{\sqrt{65}\;\sqrt{36}}=0.\end{array}$$

Now, $\cos \ktheta =0$ and $0\le \ktheta \le \pi ,$ so $\ktheta =\pi /2.$

The angle between two vectors can be acute $(0\lt \cos \ktheta \lt 1),$ obtuse $(-1\lt \cos \ktheta \lt 0),$ or straight $(\cos \ktheta =-1).$ If $\cos \ktheta =1,$ then both vectors have the same direction. If $\cos \ktheta =0,$ then the vectors, when placed in standard position, form a right angle (Figure 12.46). We can formalize this result into a theorem regarding orthogonal (perpendicular) vectors.

Theorem

Orthogonal Vectors

The nonzero vectors $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $ are orthogonal vectors if and only if $\kvecu\cdot \kvecv=0.$

Proof

The terms orthogonal, perpendicular, and normal each indicate that mathematical objects are intersecting at right angles. The use of each term is determined mainly by its context. We say that vectors are orthogonal and lines are perpendicular. The term normal is used most often when measuring the angle made with a plane or other surface.

Example 12.24 · Identifying Orthogonal Vectors

Determine whether $\kvecp=\langle 1,0,5\rangle$ and $\kvecq=\langle 10,3,-2\rangle$ are orthogonal vectors.

Solution

Using the definition, we need only check the dot product of the vectors:

$$\kvecp\cdot \kvecq=1(10)+(0)(3)+(5)(-2)=10+0-10=0.$$

Because $\kvecp\cdot \kvecq=0,$ the vectors are orthogonal (Figure 12.47).

Example 12.25 · Measuring the Angle Formed by Two Vectors

Let $\kvecv=\langle 2,3,3\rangle .$ Find the measures of the angles formed by the following vectors.

a. $\kvecv $ and $\kveci$

b. $\kvecv $ and $\kvecj$

c. $\kvecv $ and $\kveck$

Solution

a. Let α be the angle formed by $\kvecv $ and i:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\cos \alpha & =\frac{\kvecv\cdot \kveci}{\| \kvecv\| \cdot \| \kveci\|} \\ & =\frac{\langle 2,3,3\rangle \cdot \langle 1,0,0\rangle}{\sqrt{2^{2}+3^{2}+3^{2}}\;\sqrt{1}} \\ & =\frac{2}{\sqrt{22}}.\end{array}$$ $$\alpha =\operatorname{arccos}\;\frac{2}{\sqrt{22}}\approx 1.130\;\text{rad}.$$

b. Let β represent the angle formed by $\kvecv $ and $\kvecj$:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\cos β & =\frac{\kvecv\cdot \kvecj}{\| \kvecv\| \cdot \| \kvecj\|} \\ & =\frac{\langle 2,3,3\rangle \cdot \langle 0,1,0\rangle}{\sqrt{2^{2}+3^{2}+3^{2}}\;\sqrt{1}} \\ & =\frac{3}{\sqrt{22}}.\end{array}$$ $$β=\operatorname{arccos}\;\frac{3}{\sqrt{22}}\approx 0.877\;\text{rad.}$$

c. Let γ represent the angle formed by $\kvecv $ and $\kveck$:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\cos γ & =\frac{\kvecv\cdot \kveck}{\| \kvecv\| \cdot \| \kveck\|} \\ & =\frac{\langle 2,3,3\rangle \cdot \langle 0,0,1\rangle}{\sqrt{2^{2}+3^{2}+3^{2}}\;\sqrt{1}} \\ & =\frac{3}{\sqrt{22}}.\end{array}$$ $$γ=\operatorname{arccos}\;\frac{3}{\sqrt{22}}\approx 0.877\;\text{rad.}$$

The angle a vector makes with each of the coordinate axes, called a direction angle, is very important in practical computations, especially in a field such as engineering. For example, in astronautical engineering, the angle at which a rocket is launched must be determined very precisely. A very small error in the angle can lead to the rocket going hundreds of miles off course. Direction angles are often calculated by using the dot product and the cosines of the angles, called the direction cosines. Therefore, we define both these angles and their cosines.

Definition

The angles formed by a nonzero vector and the coordinate axes are called the direction angles for the vector (Figure 12.48). The cosines for these angles are called the direction cosines.

In Example 12.25, the direction cosines of $\kvecv=\langle 2,3,3\rangle$ are $\cos \alpha =\frac{2}{\sqrt{22}},$ $\cos β=\frac{3}{\sqrt{22}},$ and $\cos γ=\frac{3}{\sqrt{22}}.$ The direction angles of $\kvecv $ are $\alpha =1.130\;\text{rad},$ $β=0.877\;\text{rad},$ and $γ=0.877\;\text{rad}.$

So far, we have focused mainly on vectors related to force, movement, and position in three-dimensional physical space. However, vectors are often used in more abstract ways. For example, suppose a fruit vendor sells apples, bananas, and oranges. On a given day, he sells 30 apples, 12 bananas, and 18 oranges. He might use a quantity vector, $\kvecq=\langle 30,12,18\rangle ,$ to represent the quantity of fruit he sold that day. Similarly, he might want to use a price vector, $\kvecp=\langle 0.50,0.25,1\rangle ,$ to indicate that he sells his apples for 50¢ each, bananas for 25¢ each, and oranges for $1 apiece. In this example, although we could still graph these vectors, we do not interpret them as literal representations of position in the physical world. We are simply using vectors to keep track of particular pieces of information about apples, bananas, and oranges.

This idea might seem a little strange, but if we simply regard vectors as a way to order and store data, we find they can be quite a powerful tool. Going back to the fruit vendor, let’s think about the dot product, $\kvecq\cdot \kvecp.$ We compute it by multiplying the number of apples sold (30) by the price per apple (50¢), the number of bananas sold by the price per banana, and the number of oranges sold by the price per orange. We then add all these values together. So, in this example, the dot product tells us how much money the fruit vendor had in sales on that particular day.

When we use vectors in this more general way, there is no reason to limit the number of components to three. What if the fruit vendor decides to start selling grapefruit? In that case, he would want to use four-dimensional quantity and price vectors to represent the number of apples, bananas, oranges, and grapefruit sold, and their unit prices. As you might expect, to calculate the dot product of four-dimensional vectors, we simply add the products of the components as before, but the sum has four terms instead of three.

Example 12.26 · Using Vectors in an Economic Context

AAA Party Supply Store sells invitations, party favors, decorations, and food service items such as paper plates and napkins. When AAA buys its inventory, it pays 25¢ per package for invitations and party favors. Decorations cost AAA 50¢ each, and food service items cost 20¢ per package. AAA sells invitations for $2.50 per package and party favors for $1.50 per package. Decorations sell for $4.50 each and food service items for $1.25 per package.

During the month of May, AAA Party Supply Store sells 1258 invitations, 342 party favors, 2426 decorations, and 1354 food service items. Use vectors and dot products to calculate how much money AAA made in sales during the month of May. How much did the store make in profit?

Solution

The cost, price, and quantity vectors are

$$\begin{array}{l}\kvecc=\langle 0.25,0.25,0.50,0.20\rangle \\ \kvecp=\langle 2.50,1.50,4.50,1.25\rangle \\ \kvecq=\langle 1258,342,2426,1354\rangle .\end{array}$$

AAA sales for the month of May can be calculated using the dot product $\kvecp\cdot \kvecq.$ We have

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecp\cdot \kvecq & =\langle 2.50,1.50,4.50,1.25\rangle \cdot \langle 1258,342,2426,1354\rangle \\ & =3145+513+10917+1692.5 \\ & =16267.5.\end{array}$$

So, AAA took in $16,267.50 during the month of May.

To calculate the profit, we must first calculate how much AAA paid for the items sold. We use the dot product $\kvecc\cdot \kvecq $ to get

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecc\cdot \kvecq & =\langle 0.25,0.25,0.50,0.20\rangle \cdot \langle 1258,342,2426,1354\rangle \\ & =314.5+85.5+1213+270.8 \\ & =1883.8.\end{array}$$

So, AAA paid $1,883.80 for the items they sold. Their profit, then, is given by

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecp\cdot \kvecq-\kvecc\cdot \kvecq & =16267.5-1883.8 \\ & =14383.7.\end{array}$$

Therefore, AAA Party Supply Store made $14,383.70 in May.

Projections

As we have seen, addition combines two vectors to create a resultant vector. But what if we are given a vector and we need to find its component parts? We use vector projections to perform the opposite process; they can break down a vector into its components. The magnitude of a vector projection is a scalar projection. For example, if a child is pulling the handle of a wagon at a 55° angle, we can use projections to determine how much of the force on the handle is actually moving the wagon forward (Figure 12.49). We return to this example and learn how to solve it after we see how to calculate projections.

Definition

The vector projection of $\kvecv $ onto $\kvecu $ is the vector labeled $\text{proj}_{\kvecu}\kvecv$ in Figure 12.50. It has the same initial point as $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $ and the same direction as $\kvecu $, and represents the component of $\kvecv $ that acts in the direction of $\kvecu $. If $\ktheta$ represents the angle between $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $, then, by properties of triangles, we know the length of ${\text{proj}}_{\kvecu}\kvecv $ is $\| {\text{proj}}_{\kvecu}\kvecv\| =\| \kvecv\| \cdot |\cos |\;\ktheta .$ Note that when the angle $\ktheta$ between $\kvecu $ and $\kvecv $ is an obtuse angle, the projection will be in the opposite direction of $\kvecu $.

The length of this vector is also known as the scalar projection of $\kvecv $ onto $\kvecu $ and is denoted by

$${\text{comp}}_{\kvecu}\kvecv=\frac{\kvecu\cdot \kvecv}{\| \kvecu\|}.\tag{12.7}$$

Example 12.27 · Finding Projections

Find the projection of $\kvecv $ onto u.

a. $\kvecv=\langle 3,5,1\rangle$ and $\kvecu=\langle -1,4,3\rangle$

b. $\kvecv=3\kveci-2\kvecj $ and $\kvecu=\kveci+6\kvecj $

Solution

a. Substitute the components of $\kvecv $ and $\kvecu $ into the formula for the projection:

$$\begin{array}{rl}{\text{proj}}_{\kvecu}\kvecv & =\frac{\kvecu\cdot \kvecv}{{\| \kvecu\|}^{2}}\kvecu \\ & =\frac{\langle -1,4,3\rangle \cdot \langle 3,5,1\rangle}{{\| \langle -1,4,3\rangle \|}^{2}}\langle -1,4,3\rangle \\ & =\frac{-3+20+3}{{(-1)}^{2}+4^{2}+3^{2}}\langle -1,4,3\rangle \\ & =\frac{20}{26}\langle -1,4,3\rangle \\ & =\langle -\frac{10}{13},\frac{40}{13},\frac{30}{13}\rangle .\end{array}$$

b. To find the two-dimensional projection, simply adapt the formula to the two-dimensional case:

$$\begin{array}{rl}{\text{proj}}_{\kvecu}\kvecv & =\frac{\kvecu\cdot \kvecv}{{\| \kvecu\|}^{2}}\kvecu \\ & =\frac{(\kveci+6\kvecj)\cdot (3\kveci-2\kvecj)}{{\| \kveci+6\kvecj\|}^{2}}(\kveci+6\kvecj) \\ & =\frac{1(3)+6(-2)}{1^{2}+6^{2}}(\kveci+6\kvecj) \\ & =-\frac{9}{37}(\kveci+6\kvecj) \\ & =-\frac{9}{37}\kveci-\frac{54}{37}\kvecj.\end{array}$$

Sometimes it is useful to decompose vectors—that is, to break a vector apart into a sum. This process is called the resolution of a vector into components. Projections allow us to identify two orthogonal vectors having a desired sum. For example, let $\kvecv=\langle 6,-4\rangle$ and let $\kvecu=\langle 3,1\rangle .$ We want to decompose the vector $\kvecv $ into orthogonal components such that one of the component vectors has the same direction as $\kvecu $.

We first find the component that has the same direction as $\kvecu $ by projecting $\kvecv $ onto $\kvecu $. Let $\kvecp={\text{proj}}_{\kvecu}\kvecv.$ Then, we have

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecp & =\frac{\kvecu\cdot \kvecv}{{\| \kvecu\|}^{2}}\kvecu \\ & =\frac{18-4}{9+1}\kvecu \\ & =\frac{7}{5}\kvecu=\frac{7}{5}\langle 3,1\rangle =\langle \frac{21}{5},\frac{7}{5}\rangle .\end{array}$$

Now consider the vector $\kvecq=\kvecv-\kvecp.$ We have

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecq & =\kvecv-\kvecp \\ & =\langle 6,-4\rangle -\langle \frac{21}{5},\frac{7}{5}\rangle \\ & =\langle \frac{9}{5},-\frac{27}{5}\rangle .\end{array}$$

Clearly, by the way we defined $\kvecq $, we have $\kvecv=\kvecq+\kvecp,$ and

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecq\cdot \kvecp & =\langle \frac{9}{5},-\frac{27}{5}\rangle \cdot \langle \frac{21}{5},\frac{7}{5}\rangle \\ & =\frac{9(21)}{25}+\frac{-27(7)}{25} \\ & =\frac{189}{25}-\frac{189}{25}=0.\end{array}$$

Therefore, $\kvecq $ and $\kvecp$ are orthogonal.

Example 12.28 · Resolving Vectors into Components

Express $\kvecv=\langle 8,-3,-3\rangle$ as a sum of orthogonal vectors such that one of the vectors has the same direction as $\kvecu=\langle 2,3,2\rangle .$

Solution

Let $\kvecp$ represent the projection of $\kvecv $ onto $\kvecu $:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kvecp & ={\text{proj}}_{\kvecu}\kvecv \\ & =\frac{\kvecu\cdot \kvecv}{{\| \kvecu\|}^{2}}\kvecu \\ & =\frac{\langle 2,3,2\rangle \cdot \langle 8,-3,-3\rangle}{{\| \langle 2,3,2\rangle \|}^{2}}\langle 2,3,2\rangle \\ & =\frac{16-9-6}{2^{2}+3^{2}+2^{2}}\langle 2,3,2\rangle \\ & =\frac{1}{17}\langle 2,3,2\rangle \\ & =\langle \frac{2}{17},\frac{3}{17},\frac{2}{17}\rangle .\end{array}$$

Then,

$$\kvecq=\kvecv-\kvecp=\langle 8,-3,-3\rangle -\langle \frac{2}{17},\frac{3}{17},\frac{2}{17}\rangle =\langle \frac{134}{17},-\frac{54}{17},-\frac{53}{17}\rangle .$$

To check our work, we can use the dot product to verify that $\kvecp$ and $\kvecq $ are orthogonal vectors:

$$\kvecp\cdot \kvecq=\langle \frac{2}{17},\frac{3}{17},\frac{2}{17}\rangle \cdot \langle \frac{134}{17},-\frac{54}{17},-\frac{53}{17}\rangle =\frac{268}{289}-\frac{162}{289}-\frac{106}{289}=0.$$

Then,

$$\kvecv=\kvecp+\kvecq=\langle \frac{2}{17},\frac{3}{17},\frac{2}{17}\rangle +\langle \frac{134}{17},-\frac{54}{17},-\frac{53}{17}\rangle .$$

Example 12.29 · Scalar Projection of Velocity

A container ship leaves port traveling $15^{\circ}$ north of east. Its engine generates a speed of 20 knots along that path (see the following figure). In addition, the ocean current moves the ship northeast at a speed of 2 knots. Considering both the engine and the current, how fast is the ship moving in the direction $15^{\circ}$ north of east? Round the answer to two decimal places.

Solution

Let $\kvecv $ be the velocity vector generated by the engine, and let $\kvecw $ be the velocity vector of the current. We already know $\| \kvecv\| =20$ along the desired route. We just need to add in the scalar projection of $\kvecw $ onto $\kvecv $. We get

$$\begin{array}{rl}{\text{comp}}_{\kvecv}\kvecw & =\frac{\kvecv\cdot \kvecw}{\| \kvecv\|} \\ & =\frac{\| \kvecv\| \| \kvecw\| \cos (30^{\circ})}{\| \kvecv\|} \\ & =\| \kvecw\| \cos (30^{\circ}) \\ & =2\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}=\sqrt{3}\approx 1.73\;\text{knots}.\end{array}$$

The ship is moving at 21.73 knots in the direction $15^{\circ}$ north of east.

Work

Now that we understand dot products, we can see how to apply them to real-life situations. The most common application of the dot product of two vectors is in the calculation of work.

From physics, we know that work is done when an object is moved by a force. When the force is constant and applied in the same direction the object moves, then we define the work done as the product of the force and the distance the object travels: $W=Fd.$ We saw several examples of this type in earlier chapters. Now imagine the direction of the force is different from the direction of motion, as with the example of a child pulling a wagon. To find the work done, we need to multiply the component of the force that acts in the direction of the motion by the magnitude of the displacement. The dot product allows us to do just that. If we represent an applied force by a vector $\kvecF$ and the displacement of an object by a vector $\kvecs$, then the work done by the force is the dot product of $\kvecF$ and $\kvecs$.

Definition

When a constant force is applied to an object so the object moves in a straight line from point P to point Q, the work W done by the force $\kvecF$, acting at an angle θ from the line of motion, is given by

$$W=\kvecF\cdot \kPQ =\| \kvecF\| \| \kPQ \| \cos \ktheta .\tag{12.8}$$

Let’s revisit the problem of the child’s wagon introduced earlier. Suppose a child is pulling a wagon with a force having a magnitude of 8 lb on the handle at an angle of 55°. If the child pulls the wagon 50 ft, find the work done by the force (Figure 12.51).

We have

$$W=\| \kvecF\| \| \kPQ \| \cos \ktheta =8(50)(\cos (55^{\circ}))\approx 229\;\text{ft}\cdot \text{lb}.$$

In U.S. standard units, we measure the magnitude of force $\| \kvecF\|$ in pounds. The magnitude of the displacement vector $\| \kPQ \|$ tells us how far the object moved, and it is measured in feet. The customary unit of measure for work, then, is the foot-pound. One foot-pound is the amount of work required to move an object weighing 1 lb a distance of 1 ft straight up. In the metric system, the unit of measure for force is the newton (N), and the unit of measure of magnitude for work is a newton-meter (N·m), or a joule (J).

Example 12.30 · Calculating Work

A conveyor belt generates a force $\kvecF=5\kveci-3\kvecj+\kveck $ that moves a suitcase from point $(1,1,1)$ to point $(9,4,7)$ along a straight line. Find the work done by the conveyor belt. The distance is measured in meters and the force is measured in newtons.

Solution

The displacement vector $\kPQ $ has initial point $(1,1,1)$ and terminal point $(9,4,7)\text{:}$

$$\kPQ =\langle 9-1,4-1,7-1\rangle =\langle 8,3,6\rangle =8\kveci+3\kvecj+6\kveck.$$

Work is the dot product of force and displacement:

$$\begin{array}{rl}W & =\kvecF\cdot \kPQ \\ & =(5\kveci-3\kvecj+\kveck)\cdot (8\kveci+3\kvecj+6\kveck) \\ & =5(8)+(-3)(3)+1(6) \\ & =37\text{N}\cdot \text{m} \\ & =37\;\text{J}.\end{array}$$
