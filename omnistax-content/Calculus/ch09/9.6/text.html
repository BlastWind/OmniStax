In this section, we prove the last two series convergence tests: the ratio test and the root test. These tests are particularly nice because they do not require us to find a comparable series. The ratio test will be especially useful in the discussion of power series in the next chapter.

Throughout this chapter, we have seen that no single convergence test works for all series. Therefore, at the end of this section we discuss a strategy for choosing which convergence test to use for a given series.

Ratio Test

Consider a series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kaseq_{n}.$ From our earlier discussion and examples, we know that $\lim_{n\to \infty}\kaseq_{n}=0$ is not a sufficient condition for the series to converge. Not only do we need $\kaseq_{n}\to 0,$ but we need $\kaseq_{n}\to 0$ quickly enough. For example, consider the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n$ and the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n^{2}.$ We know that $1/n\to 0$ and $1/n^{2}\to 0.$ However, only the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n^{2}$ converges. The series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n$ diverges because the terms in the sequence $\{1/n\}$ do not approach zero fast enough as $n\to \infty.$ Here we introduce the ratio test, which provides a way of measuring how fast the terms of a series approach zero.

Theorem

Ratio Test

Let $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}$ be a series with nonzero terms. Let

$$\krho =\lim_{n\to \infty}|\frac{\kaseq_{n+1}}{\kaseq_{n}}|.$$
  1. If $0\le\krho \lt 1,$ then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}$ converges absolutely.
  2. If $\krho \gt 1$ or $\krho =\infty,$ then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}$ diverges.
  3. If $\krho =1,$ the test does not provide any information.

Proof

Let $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kaseq_{n}$ be a series with nonzero terms.

We begin with the proof of part i. In this case, $\krho =\lim_{n\to \infty}|\frac{\kaseq_{n+1}}{\kaseq_{n}}|\lt 1.$ Since $0\le\krho \lt 1,$ there exists $R$ such that $0\le\krho \lt R\lt 1.$ Let $\varepsilon =R-\krho \gt 0.$ By the definition of limit of a sequence, there exists some integer $N$ such that

$$||\frac{\kaseq_{n+1}}{\kaseq_{n}}|-\krho |\lt \varepsilon \;\text{for all}\;n\ge N.$$

Therefore,

$$|\frac{\kaseq_{n+1}}{\kaseq_{n}}|\lt \krho +\varepsilon =R\;\text{for all}\;n\ge N$$

and, thus,

$$\begin{gathered}|\kaseq_{N+1}|\lt R|\kaseq_{N}| \\ |\kaseq_{N+2}|\lt R|\kaseq_{N+1}|\lt R^{2}|\kaseq_{N}| \\ |\kaseq_{N+3}|\lt R|\kaseq_{N+2}|\lt R^{2}|\kaseq_{N+1}|\lt R^{3}|\kaseq_{N}| \\ |\kaseq_{N+4}|\lt R|\kaseq_{N+3}|\lt R^{2}|\kaseq_{N+2}|\lt R^{3}|\kaseq_{N+1}|\lt R^{4}|\kaseq_{N}| \\ \vdots .\end{gathered}$$

Since $R\lt 1,$ the geometric series

$$R|\kaseq_{N}|+R^{2}|\kaseq_{N}|+R^{3}|\kaseq_{N}|+\cdots$$

converges. Given the inequalities above, we can apply the comparison test and conclude that the series

$$|\kaseq_{N+1}|+|\kaseq_{N+2}|+|\kaseq_{N+3}|+|\kaseq_{N+4}|+\cdots$$

converges. Therefore, since

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |\kaseq_{n}|=\sum_{n=1}^{N} |\kaseq_{n}|+\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty} |\kaseq_{n}|$$

where $\sum_{n=1}^{N} |\kaseq_{n}|$ is a finite sum and $\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty} |\kaseq_{n}|$ converges, we conclude that $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |\kaseq_{n}|$ converges.

For part ii.

Now suppose $\krho =|\frac{\kaseq_{n+1}}{\kaseq_{n}}|\gt 1$.

There exists some $N$ such that $|\frac{\kaseq_{n+1}}{\kaseq_{n}}|\gt 1$ for all $n\ge N$.

Then $|\kaseq_{n-}|\gt |\kaseq_{n}|$.

Now because $|\kaseq_{n-}|\gt |\kaseq_{n}|$ for all $n\ge N$, we know that $|\kaseq_{n}|\ne0$.

It follows that $|\kaseq_{n}|\ne0$.

By the divergence test, the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kaseq_{n}$ diverges.

$$|\kaseq_{N+1}|+|\kaseq_{N+2}|+|\kaseq_{N+3}|+\cdots$$

diverges, and therefore the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |\kaseq_{n}|$ diverges.

For part iii. we show that the test does not provide any information if $\krho =1$ by considering the $p-\text{series}$ $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^{p}.$ For any real number $p,$

$$\krho =\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{1/{(n+1)}^{p}}{1/n^{p}}=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{n^{p}}{{(n+1)}^{p}}=1.$$

However, we know that if $p\le1,$ the $p-\text{series}$ $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^{p}$ diverges, whereas $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^{p}$ converges if $p\gt 1.$

□

The ratio test is particularly useful for series whose terms contain factorials or exponentials, where the ratio of terms simplifies the expression. The ratio test is convenient because it does not require us to find a comparative series. The drawback is that the test sometimes does not provide any information regarding convergence.

Example 9.23 · Using the Ratio Test

For each of the following series, use the ratio test to determine whether the series converges or diverges.

  1. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{n}}{n\text{!}}$
  2. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{n}}{n\text{!}}$
  3. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{{(-1)}^{n}{(n\text{!})}^{2}}{(2n)\text{!}}$

Solution

  1. From the ratio test, we can see that $$\krho =\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{2^{n+1}/(n+1)\text{!}}{2^{n}/n\text{!}}=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{2^{n+1}}{(n+1)\text{!}}\cdot \frac{n\text{!}}{2^{n}}.$$

Since $(n+1)\text{!}=(n+1)\cdot n\text{!},$

$$\krho =\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{2}{n+1}=0.$$

Since $\krho \lt 1,$ the series converges.

  1. We can see that $$\begin{aligned}\krho &=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{{(n+1)}^{n+1}/(n+1)\text{!}}{n^{n}/n\text{!}} \\ &=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{{(n+1)}^{n+1}}{(n+1)\text{!}}\cdot \frac{n\text{!}}{n^{n}} \\ &=\lim_{n\to \infty}{(\frac{n+1}{n})}^{n}=\lim_{n\to \infty}{(1+\frac{1}{n})}^{n}=e.\end{aligned}$$

Since $\krho \gt 1,$ the series diverges.

  1. Since $$\begin{aligned}|\frac{{(-1)}^{n+1}{((n+1)\text{!})}^{2}/(2(n+1))\text{!}}{{(-1)}^{n}{(n\text{!})}^{2}/(2n)\text{!}}| &=\frac{(n+1)\text{!}(n+1)\text{!}}{(2n+2)\text{!}}\cdot \frac{(2n)\text{!}}{n\text{!}n\text{!}} \\ &=\frac{(n+1)(n+1)}{(2n+2)(2n+1)}\end{aligned}$$

we see that

$$\krho =\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{(n+1)(n+1)}{(2n+2)(2n+1)}=\frac{1}{4}.$$

Since $\krho \lt 1,$ the series converges.

Root Test

The approach of the root test is similar to that of the ratio test. Consider a series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}$ such that $\lim_{n\to \infty}\sqrt[n]{|\kaseq_{n}|}=\krho $ for some real number $\krho .$ Then for $N$ sufficiently large, $|\kaseq_{N}|\approx {\krho }^{N}.$ Therefore, we can approximate $\sum_{n=N}^{\infty} |\kaseq_{n}|$ by writing

$$|\kaseq_{N}|+|\kaseq_{N+1}|+|\kaseq_{N+2}|+\cdots\approx {\krho }^{N}+{\krho }^{N+1}+{\krho }^{N+2}+\cdots.$$

The expression on the right-hand side is a geometric series. As in the ratio test, the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}$ converges absolutely if $0\le\krho \lt 1$ and the series diverges if $\krho \ge1.$ If $\krho =1,$ the test does not provide any information. For example, for any p-series, $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n^{p},$ we see that

$$\krho =\lim_{n\to \infty}\sqrt[n]{|\frac{1}{n^{p}}|}=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{1}{n^{p/n}}.$$

To evaluate this limit, we use the natural logarithm function. Doing so, we see that

$$\ln\krho =\ln(\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{1}{n^{p/n}})=\lim_{n\to \infty}\ln{(\frac{1}{n})}^{p/n}=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{p}{n}\cdot \ln(\frac{1}{n})=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{p\;\ln(1/n)}{n}.$$

Using L’Hôpital’s rule, it follows that $\ln\krho =0,$ and therefore $\krho =1$ for all $p.$ However, we know that the p-series only converges if $p\gt 1$ and diverges if $p\lt 1.$

Theorem

Root Test

Consider the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}.$ Let

$$\krho =\lim_{n\to \infty}\sqrt[n]{|\kaseq_{n}|}.$$
  1. If $0\le\krho \lt 1,$ then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}$ converges absolutely.
  2. If $\krho \gt 1$ or $\krho =\infty,$ then $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}$ diverges.
  3. If $\krho =1,$ the test does not provide any information.

The root test is useful for series whose terms involve exponentials. In particular, for a series whose terms $\kaseq_{n}$ satisfy $|\kaseq_{n}|=\kbseq_{n}^{n},$ then $\sqrt[n]{|\kaseq_{n}|}=\kbseq_{n}$ and we need only evaluate $\lim_{n\to \infty}\kbseq_{n}.$

Example 9.24 · Using the Root Test

For each of the following series, use the root test to determine whether the series converges or diverges.

  1. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{{(n^{2}+3n)}^{n}}{{(4n^{2}+5)}^{n}}$
  2. $\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{n^{n}}{{(\ln(n))}^{n}}$

Solution

  1. To apply the root test, we compute $$\krho =\lim_{n\to \infty}\sqrt[n]{{(n^{2}+3n)}^{n}/{(4n^{2}+5)}^{n}}=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{n^{2}+3n}{4n^{2}+5}=\frac{1}{4}.$$

Since $\krho \lt 1,$ the series converges absolutely.

  1. We have $$\krho =\lim_{n\to \infty}\sqrt[n]{n^{n}/{(\ln n)}^{n}}=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{n}{\ln n}=\infty\;\text{by L’Hôpital’s rule}.$$

Since $\krho =\infty,$ the series diverges.

Choosing a Convergence Test

At this point, we have a long list of convergence tests. However, not all tests can be used for all series. When given a series, we must determine which test is the best to use. Here is a strategy for finding the best test to apply.

Problem-Solving Strategy

Choosing a Convergence Test for a Series

Consider a series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kaseq_{n}.$ In the steps below, we outline a strategy for determining whether the series converges.

  1. Is $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kaseq_{n}$ a familiar series? For example, is it the harmonic series (which diverges) or the alternating harmonic series (which converges)? Is it a $p-\text{series}$ or geometric series? If so, check the power $p$ or the ratio $r$ to determine if the series converges.
  2. Is it an alternating series? Are we interested in absolute convergence or just convergence? If we are just interested in whether the series converges, apply the alternating series test. If we are interested in absolute convergence, proceed to step $3,$ considering the series of absolute values $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |\kaseq_{n}|.$
  3. Is the series similar to a $p-\text{series}$ or geometric series? If so, try the comparison test or limit comparison test.
  4. Do the terms in the series contain a factorial or power? If the terms are powers such that $\kaseq_{n}=\kbseq_{n}^{n},$ try the root test first. Otherwise, try the ratio test first.
  5. Use the divergence test. If this test does not provide any information, try the integral test.

Example 9.25 · Using Convergence Tests

For each of the following series, determine which convergence test is the best to use and explain why. Then determine if the series converges or diverges. If the series is an alternating series, determine whether it converges absolutely, converges conditionally, or diverges.

  1. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{2}+2n}{n^{3}+3n^{2}+1}$
  2. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{{(-1)}^{n+1}(3n+1)}{n\text{!}}$
  3. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{n}}{n^{3}}$
  4. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{3^{n}}{{(n+1)}^{n}}$

Solution

  1. Step 1. The series is not a $p-\text{series}$ or geometric series.

Step 2. The series is not alternating.

Step 3. For large values of $n,$ we approximate the series by the expression

$$\frac{n^{2}+2n}{n^{3}+3n^{2}+1}\approx \frac{n^{2}}{n^{3}}=\frac{1}{n}.$$

Therefore, it seems reasonable to apply the comparison test or limit comparison test using the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n.$ Using the limit comparison test, we see that

$$\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{(n^{2}+2n)/(n^{3}+3n^{2}+1)}{1/n}=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{n^{3}+2n^{2}}{n^{3}+3n^{2}+1}=1.$$

Since the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n$ diverges, this series diverges as well.

  1. Step 1.The series is not a familiar series.

Step 2. The series is alternating. Since we are interested in absolute convergence, consider the series

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{3n}{(n+1)\text{!}}.$$

Step 3. The series is not similar to a p-series or geometric series.

Step 4. Since each term contains a factorial, apply the ratio test. We see that

$$\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{(3(n+1))/(n+1)\text{!}}{(3n+1)/n\text{!}}=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{3n+3}{(n+1)\text{!}}\cdot \frac{n\text{!}}{3n+1}=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{3n+3}{(n+1)(3n+1)}=0.$$

Therefore, this series converges, and we conclude that the original series converges absolutely, and thus converges.

  1. Step 1. The series is not a familiar series.

Step 2. It is not an alternating series.

Step 3. There is no obvious series with which to compare this series.

Step 4. There is no factorial. There is a power, but it is not an ideal situation for the root test.

Step 5. To apply the divergence test, we calculate that

$$\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{e^{n}}{n^{3}}=\infty.$$

Therefore, by the divergence test, the series diverges.

  1. Step 1. This series is not a familiar series.

Step 2. It is not an alternating series.

Step 3. There is no obvious series with which to compare this series.

Step 4. Since each term is a power of $n,$ we can apply the root test. Since

$$\lim_{n\to \infty}\sqrt[n]{{(\frac{3}{n+1})}^{n}}=\lim_{n\to \infty}\frac{3}{n+1}=0,$$

by the root test, we conclude that the series converges.
