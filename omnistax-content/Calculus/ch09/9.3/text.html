In the previous section, we determined the convergence or divergence of several series by explicitly calculating the limit of the sequence of partial sums $\{\kS_{k}\}.$ In practice, explicitly calculating this limit can be difficult or impossible. Luckily, several tests exist that allow us to determine convergence or divergence for many types of series. In this section, we discuss two of these tests: the divergence test and the integral test. We will examine several other tests in the rest of this chapter and then summarize how and when to use them.

Divergence Test

A series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}$ being convergent is equivalent to the convergence of the sequence of partial sums ($\kS_{k}$) as $k\to \infty.$

This test is known as the divergence test because it provides a way of proving that a series diverges.

Theorem

Divergence Test

If $\lim_{n\to \infty}\kaseq_{n}=c\ne0$ or $\lim_{n\to \infty}\kaseq_{n}$ does not exist, then the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kaseq_{n}$ diverges.

It is important to note that the converse of this theorem is not true. That is, if $\lim_{n\to \infty}\kaseq_{n}=0,$ we cannot make any conclusion about the convergence of $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kaseq_{n}.$ For example, $\lim_{n\to \infty}(1/n)=0,$ but the harmonic series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n$ diverges. In this section and the remaining sections of this chapter, we show many more examples of such series. Consequently, although we can use the divergence test to show that a series diverges, we cannot use it to prove that a series converges. Specifically, if $\kaseq_{n}\to 0,$ the divergence test is inconclusive.

Example 9.13 · Using the divergence test

For each of the following series, apply the divergence test. If the divergence test proves that the series diverges, state so. Otherwise, indicate that the divergence test is inconclusive.

  1. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n}{3n-1}$
  2. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{3}}$
  3. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{1/n^{2}}$

Solution

  1. Since $n/(3n-1)\to 1/3\ne0,$ by the divergence test, we can conclude that $$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n}{3n-1}$$

diverges.

  1. Since $1/n^{3}\to 0,$ the divergence test is inconclusive.
  2. Since $e^{1/n^{2}}\to 1\ne0,$ by the divergence test, the series $$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{1/n^{2}}$$

diverges.

Integral Test

In the previous section, we proved that the harmonic series diverges by looking at the sequence of partial sums $\{\kS_{k}\}$ and showing that $\kS_{2^{k}}\gt 1+k/2$ for all positive integers $k.$ In this section we use a different technique to prove the divergence of the harmonic series. This technique is important because it is used to prove the divergence or convergence of many other series. This test, called the integral test, compares an infinite sum to an improper integral. It is important to note that this test can only be applied when we are considering a series whose terms are all positive.

To illustrate how the integral test works, use the harmonic series as an example. In Figure 9.12, we depict the harmonic series by sketching a sequence of rectangles with areas $1,1/2,1/3,1/4\text{,\ldots}$ along with the function $\kf(x)=1/x.$

Now consider the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n^{2}.$ We show how an integral can be used to prove that this series converges. In Figure 9.13, we sketch a sequence of rectangles with areas $1,1/2^{2},1/3^{2}\text{,\ldots}$ along with the function $\kf(x)=1/x^{2}.$

We can extend this idea to prove convergence or divergence for many different series. Suppose $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}$ is a series with positive terms $\kaseq_{n}$ such that there exists a continuous, positive, decreasing function $\kf$ where $\kf(n)=\kaseq_{n}$ for all positive integers.

Theorem

Integral Test

Suppose $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kaseq_{n}$ is a series with positive terms $\kaseq_{n}.$ Suppose there exists a function $\kf$ and a positive integer $N$ such that the following three conditions are satisfied:

  1. $\kf$ is continuous,
  2. $\kf$ is decreasing, and
  3. $\kf(n)=\kaseq_{n}$ for all integers $n\ge N.$

Then

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}\text{and}\int_{N}^{\infty}\kf(x)dx$$

both converge or both diverge (see Figure 9.14).

Although convergence of $\int_{N}^{\infty}\kf(x)dx$ implies convergence of the related series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kaseq_{n},$ it does not imply that the value of the integral and the series are the same. They may be different, and often are. For example,

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} {(\frac{1}{e})}^{n}=\frac{1}{e}+{(\frac{1}{e})}^{2}+{(\frac{1}{e})}^{3}+\cdots$$

is a geometric series with initial term $a=1/e$ and ratio $r=1/e,$ which converges to

$$\frac{1/e}{1-(1/e)}=\frac{1/e}{(e-1)/e}=\frac{1}{e-1}.$$

However, the related integral $\int_{1}^{\infty}{(1/e)}^{x}dx$ satisfies

$$\int_{1}^{\infty}{(\frac{1}{e})}^{x}dx=\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-x}dx=\lim_{b\to \infty}\int_{1}^{b}e^{-x}dx=\lim_{b\to \infty}-e^{-x}{|}_{1}^{b}=\lim_{b\to \infty}[-e^{-b}+e^{-1}]=\frac{1}{e}.$$

Example 9.14 · Using the Integral Test

For each of the following series, use the integral test to determine whether the series converges or diverges. Assume that all conditions for the integral test are met.

  1. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n^{3}$
  2. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/\sqrt{2n-1}$

Solution

  1. Compare $$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^{3}}\;\text{and}\;\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{3}}dx.$$

We have

$$\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{3}}dx=\lim_{b\to \infty}\int_{1}^{b}\frac{1}{x^{3}}dx=\lim_{b\to \infty}[-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}{|}_{1}^{b}]=\lim_{b\to \infty}[-\frac{1}{2b^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}]=\frac{1}{2}.$$

Thus the integral $\int_{1}^{\infty}1/x^{3}dx$ converges, and therefore so does the series

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^{3}}.$$
  1. Compare $$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n-1}}\;\text{and}\;\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2x-1}}dx.$$

Since

$$\begin{aligned}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2x-1}}dx &=\lim_{b\to \infty}\int_{1}^{b}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2x-1}}dx=\lim_{b\to \infty}\sqrt{2x-1}{|}_{1}^{b} \\ &=\lim_{b\to \infty}[\sqrt{2b-1}-1]=\infty,\end{aligned}$$

the integral $\int_{1}^{\infty}1/\sqrt{2x-1}\;dx$ diverges, and therefore

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n-1}}$$

diverges.

The p-Series

The harmonic series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n$ and the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^{2}$ are both examples of a type of series called a p-series.

Definition

For any real number $p,$ the series

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{p}}$$

is called a p-series.

We know the p-series converges if $p=2$ and diverges if $p=1.$ What about other values of $p\text{?}$ In general, it is difficult, if not impossible, to compute the exact value of most $p$-series. However, we can use the tests presented thus far to prove whether a $p$-series converges or diverges.

Example 9.15 · Testing for Convergence of p-series

For each of the following series, determine whether it converges or diverges.

  1. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{4}}$
  2. $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2/3}}$

Solution

  1. This is a p-series with $p=4\gt 1,$ so the series converges.
  2. Since $p=2/3\lt 1,$ the series diverges.

Estimating the Value of a Series

Suppose we know that a series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kaseq_{n}$ converges and we want to estimate the sum of that series. Certainly we can approximate that sum using any finite sum $\sum_{n=1}^{N} \kaseq_{n}$ where $N$ is any positive integer. The question we address here is, for a convergent series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n},$ how good is the approximation $\sum_{n=1}^{N}\kaseq_{n}\text{?}$ More specifically, if we let

$$\kR_{N}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \kaseq_{n}-\sum_{n=1}^{N} \kaseq_{n}$$

be the remainder when the sum of an infinite series is approximated by the $N\text{th}$ partial sum, how large is $\kR_{N}\text{?}$ For some types of series, we are able to use the ideas from the integral test to estimate $\kR_{N}.$

Theorem

Remainder Estimate from the Integral Test

Suppose $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}$ is a convergent series with positive terms. Suppose there exists a function $\kf$ satisfying the following three conditions:

  1. $\kf$ is continuous,
  2. $\kf$ is decreasing, and
  3. $\kf(n)=\kaseq_{n}$ for all integers $n\ge1.$ Let $\kS_{N}$ be the $N$th partial sum of $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}.$ For all positive integers $N,$ $$\kS_{N}+\int_{N+1}^{\infty}\kf(x)dx\lt \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}\lt \kS_{N}+\int_{N}^{\infty}\kf(x)dx.$$

In other words, the remainder $\kR_{N}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}-\kS_{N}=\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\kaseq_{n}$ satisfies the following estimate:

$$\int_{N+1}^{\infty}\kf(x)dx\lt \kR_{N}\lt \int_{N}^{\infty}\kf(x)dx.\tag{9.10}$$

This is known as the remainder estimate.

We illustrate Remainder Estimate from the Integral Test in Figure 9.15. In particular, by representing the remainder $\kR_{N}=\kaseq_{N+1}+\kaseq_{N+2}+\kaseq_{N+3}+\cdots$ as the sum of areas of rectangles, we see that the area of those rectangles is bounded above by $\int_{N}^{\infty}\kf(x)dx$ and bounded below by $\int_{N+1}^{\infty}\kf(x)dx.$

Example 9.16 · Estimating the Value of a Series

Consider the series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^{3}.$

  1. Calculate $\kS_{10}=\sum_{n=1}^{10}1/n^{3}$ and estimate the error.
  2. Determine the least value of $N$ necessary such that $\kS_{N}$ will estimate $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^{3}$ to within $0.001.$

Solution

  1. Using a calculating utility, we have $$\kS_{10}=1+\frac{1}{2^{3}}+\frac{1}{3^{3}}+\frac{1}{4^{3}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{{10}^{3}}\approx 1.19753.$$

By the remainder estimate, we know

$$\kR_{N}\lt \int_{N}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{3}}dx.$$

We have

$$\int_{10}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^{3}}dx=\lim_{b\to \infty}\int_{10}^{b}\frac{1}{x^{3}}dx=\lim_{b\to \infty}[-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}{]}_{N}^{b}=\lim_{b\to \infty}[-\frac{1}{2b^{2}}+\frac{1}{2N^{2}}]=\frac{1}{2N^{2}}.$$

Therefore, the error is $\kR_{10}\lt 1/2{(10)}^{2}=0.005.$

  1. Find $N$ such that $\kR_{N}\lt 0.001.$ In part a. we showed that $\kR_{N}\lt 1/(2N^{2}).$ Therefore, the remainder $\kR_{N}\lt 0.001$ as long as $1/(2N^{2})\lt 0.001.$ That is, we need $2N^{2}\gt 1000.$ Solving this inequality for $N,$ we see that we need $N\gt 22.36.$ To ensure that the remainder is within the desired amount, we need to round up to the nearest integer. Therefore, the minimum necessary value is $N=23.$
