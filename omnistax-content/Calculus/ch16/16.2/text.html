We are familiar with single-variable integrals of the form $\int_a^{b}\kf(x)dx,$ where the domain of integration is an interval $[a,b].$ Such an interval can be thought of as a curve in the xy-plane, since the interval defines a line segment with endpoints $(a,0)$ and $(b,0)$—in other words, a line segment located on the $x$-axis. Suppose we want to integrate over any curve in the plane, not just over a line segment on the $x$-axis. Such a task requires a new kind of integral, called a line integral.

Line integrals have many applications to engineering and physics. They also allow us to make several useful generalizations of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. And, they are closely connected to the properties of vector fields, as we shall see.

Scalar Line Integrals

A line integral gives us the ability to integrate multivariable functions and vector fields over arbitrary curves in a plane or in space. There are two types of line integrals: scalar line integrals and vector line integrals. Scalar line integrals are integrals of a scalar function over a curve in a plane or in space. Vector line integrals are integrals of a vector field over a curve in a plane or in space. Let’s look at scalar line integrals first.

A scalar line integral is defined just as a single-variable integral is defined, except that for a scalar line integral, the integrand is a function of more than one variable and the domain of integration is a curve in a plane or in space, as opposed to a curve on the $x$-axis.

For a scalar line integral, we let $\kC$ be a smooth curve in a plane or in space and let $\kf$ be a function with a domain that includes $\kC$. We chop the curve into small pieces. For each piece, we choose point $\kP$ in that piece and evaluate $\kf$ at P. (We can do this because all the points in the curve are in the domain of $\kf.$) We multiply $\kf(\kP)$ by the arc length of the piece $\Delta s,$ add the product $\kf(\kP)\Delta s$ over all the pieces, and then let the arc length of the pieces shrink to zero by taking a limit. The result is the scalar line integral of the function over the curve.

For a formal description of a scalar line integral, let $C$ be a smooth curve in space given by the parameterization $\kvecr(t)=\langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle ,$ $a\le t\le b.$ Let $\kf(x,y,z)$ be a function with a domain that includes curve $C.$ To define the line integral of the function $\kf$ over $C,$ we begin as most definitions of an integral begin: we chop the curve into small pieces. Partition the parameter interval $[a,b]$ into $n$ subintervals $[t_{i-1},t_{i}]$ of equal width for $\text{l}\le i\le n,$ where $t_{0}=a$ and $t_{n}=b$ (Figure 16.12). Let $t_i^{*}$ be a value in the $i$th interval $[t_{i-\text{l}},t_{i}].$ Denote the endpoints of $\kvecr(t_{0}),\kvecr(t_{1})\text{,…},\kvecr(t_{n})$ by $P_{0}\text{,…},P_{n}.$ Points Pi divide curve $C$ into $n$ pieces $C_{1},C_{2}\text{,…},C_{n,}$ with lengths $\Delta s_{1},\Delta s_{2}\text{,…},\Delta s_{n},$ respectively. Let $P_i^{*}$ denote the endpoint of $\kvecr(t_i^{*})$ for $1\le i\le n.$ Now, we evaluate the function $\kf$ at point $P_i^{*}$ for $1\le i\le n.$ Note that $P_i^{*}$ is in piece $C_{i},$ and therefore $P_i^{*}$ is in the domain of $\kf.$ Multiply $\kf(P_i^{*})$ by the length $\Delta s_{i}$ of $C_{i},$ which gives the area of the “sheet” with base $C_{i},$ and height $\kf(P_i^{*}).$ This is analogous to using rectangles to approximate area in a single-variable integral. Now, we form the sum $\sum_{i=1}^{n} \kf(P_i^{*})\Delta s_{i} .$ Note the similarity of this sum versus a Riemann sum; in fact, this definition is a generalization of a Riemann sum to arbitrary curves in space. Just as with Riemann sums and integrals of form $\int_a^{b}g(x)dx ,$ we define an integral by letting the width of the pieces of the curve shrink to zero by taking a limit. The result is the scalar line integral of $\kf$ along $C.$

You may have noticed a difference between this definition of a scalar line integral and a single-variable integral. In this definition, the arc lengths $\Delta s_{1},\Delta s_{2}\text{,…},\Delta s_{n}$ aren’t necessarily the same; in the definition of a single-variable integral, the curve in the $x$-axis is partitioned into pieces of equal length. This difference does not have any effect in the limit. As we shrink the arc lengths to zero, their values become close enough that any small difference becomes irrelevant.

Definition

Let $\kf$ be a function with a domain that includes the smooth curve $C$ that is parameterized by $\kvecr(t)=\langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle ,$ $a\le t\le b.$ The scalar line integral of $\kf$ along $C$ is

$$\int_{C}\kf(x,y,z)ds=\lim_{n\to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \kf(P_i^{*})\Delta s_{i}\tag{16.5}$$

if this limit exists $(t_i^{*}$ and $\Delta s_{i}$ are defined as in the previous paragraphs). If $\kC$ is a planar curve, then $\kC$ can be represented by the parametric equations $x=x(t),y=y(t),$ and $a\le t\le b.$ If $\kC$ is smooth and $\kf(x,y)$ is a function of two variables, then the scalar line integral of $\kf$ along $\kC$ is defined similarly as

$$\int_{C}\kf(x,y)ds=\lim_{n\to \infty} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \kf(P_i^{*})\Delta s_{i} ,$$

if this limit exists.

If $\kf$ is a continuous function on a smooth curve $\kC$, then $\int_{C}fds$ always exists. Since $\int_{C}fds$ is defined as a limit of Riemann sums, the continuity of $\kf$ is enough to guarantee the existence of the limit, just as the integral $\int_a^{b}g(x)dx$ exists if g is continuous over $[a,b].$

Before looking at how to compute a line integral, we need to examine the geometry captured by these integrals. Suppose that $\kf(x,y)\ge 0$ for all points $(x,y)$ on a smooth planar curve $C.$ Imagine taking curve $C$ and projecting it “up” to the surface defined by $\kf(x,y),$ thereby creating a new curve $C^{\prime}$ that lies in the graph of $\kf(x,y)$ (Figure 16.13). Now we drop a “sheet” from $C^{\prime}$ down to the xy-plane. The area of this sheet is $\int_{C}\kf(x,y)ds .$ If $\kf(x,y)\le 0$ for some points in $C,$ then the value of $\int_{C}\kf(x,y)ds$ is the area above the xy-plane less the area below the xy-plane. (Note the similarity with integrals of the form $\int_a^{b}g(x)dx. )$

From this geometry, we can see that line integral $\int_{C}\kf(x,y)ds$ does not depend on the parameterization $\kvecr(t)$ of $\kC$. As long as the curve is traversed exactly once by the parameterization, the area of the sheet formed by the function and the curve is the same. This same kind of geometric argument can be extended to show that the line integral of a three-variable function over a curve in space does not depend on the parameterization of the curve.

Example 16.14 · Finding the Value of a Line Integral

Find the value of integral $\int_{C}2ds,$ where $C$ is the upper half of the unit circle.

Solution

The integrand is $\kf(x,y)=2.$ Figure 16.14 shows the graph of $\kf(x,y)=2,$ curve $\kC$, and the sheet formed by them. Notice that this sheet has the same area as a rectangle with width $\pi$ and length 2. Therefore, $\int_{C}2ds=2\pi .$

To see that $\int_{C}2ds=2\pi$ using the definition of line integral, we let $\kvecr(t)$ be a parameterization of $\kC$. Then, $\kf(\kvecr(t_{i}))=2$ for any number $t_{i}$ in the domain of $\kvecr$. Therefore,

$$\begin{array}{ll}\int_{C}fds & =\lim_{n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \kf(\kvecr(t_i^{*}))\Delta s_{i} \\ & =\lim_{n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} 2\Delta s_{i} \\ & =2\lim_{n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \Delta s_{i} \\ & =2(\text{length of C}) \\ & =2\pi .\end{array}$$

Note that in a scalar line integral, the integration is done with respect to arc length $s$, which can make a scalar line integral difficult to calculate. To make the calculations easier, we can translate $\int_{C}fds$ to an integral with a variable of integration that is $t$.

Let $\kvecr(t)=\langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle$ for $a\le t\le b$ be a parameterization of $C.$ Since we are assuming that $C$ is smooth, $\kvecrprime(t)=\langle x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t),z^{\prime}(t)\rangle$ is continuous for all $t$ in $[a,b].$ In particular, $x\text{′}(t),y\text{′}(t),$ and $z\text{′}(t)$ exist for all $t$ in $[a,b].$ According to the arc length formula, we have

$$\text{length}(C_{i})=\Delta s_{i}=\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\| \kvecrprime(t)\| dt.$$

If width $\Delta t_{i}=t_{i}-t_{i-1}$ is small, then function $\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\| \kvecrprime(t)\| dt\approx \| r^{\prime}(t_i^{*})\| \Delta t_{i},$ $\| \kvecrprime(t)\|$ is almost constant over the interval $[t_{i-1},t_{i}].$ Therefore,

$$\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\| \kvecrprime(t)\| dt \approx \| \kvecrprime(t_i^{*})\| \Delta t_{i},$$

and we have

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \kf(\kvecr(t_i^{*}))\Delta s_{i} =\sum_{i=1}^{n} \kf(\kvecr(t_i^{*}))\| \kvecrprime(t_i^{*})\| \Delta t_{i}.\tag{16.6}$$

See Figure 16.15.

Note that

$$\lim_{n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \kf(\kvecr(t_i^{*})) \| \kvecrprime(t_i^{*})\| \Delta t_{i}=\int_a^{b}\kf(\kvecr(t)) \| \kvecrprime(t)\| dt.$$

In other words, as the widths of intervals $[t_{i-1},t_{i}]$ shrink to zero, the sum $\sum_{i=1}^{n} \kf(\kvecr(t_i^{*})) \| \kvecrprime(t_i^{*})\| \Delta t_{i}$ converges to the integral $\int_a^{b}\kf(\kvecr(t)) \| \kvecrprime(t)\| dt.$ Therefore, we have the following theorem.

Theorem

Evaluating a Scalar Line Integral

Let $\kf$ be a continuous function with a domain that includes the smooth curve $C$ with parameterization $\kvecr(t),a\le t\le b.$ Then

$$\int_{C}fds=\int_a^{b}\kf(\kvecr(t)) \| \kvecrprime(t)\| dt .\tag{16.7}$$

Although we have labeled Equation 16.6 as an equation, it is more accurately considered an approximation because we can show that the left-hand side of Equation 16.6 approaches the right-hand side as $n\to \infty .$ In other words, letting the widths of the pieces shrink to zero makes the right-hand sum arbitrarily close to the left-hand sum. Since

$$\| \kvecrprime(t)\| =\sqrt{{(x'(t))}^{2}+{(y'(t))}^{2}+{(z'(t))}^{2},}$$

we obtain the following theorem, which we use to compute scalar line integrals.

Theorem

Scalar Line Integral Calculation

Let $\kf$ be a continuous function with a domain that includes the smooth curve $\kC$ with parameterization $\kvecr(t)=\langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle ,a\le t\le b.$ Then

$$\int_{C}\kf(x,y,z)ds=\int_a^{b}\kf(\kvecr(t))\sqrt{{(x^{\prime}(t))}^{2}+{(y^{\prime}(t))}^{2}+{(z^{\prime}(t))}^{2}} dt.\tag{16.8}$$

Similarly,

$$\int_{C}\kf(x,y)ds=\int_a^{b}\kf(\kvecr(t))\sqrt{{(x^{\prime}(t))}^{2}+{(y^{\prime}(t))}^{2}} dt$$

if $\kC$ is a planar curve and $\kf$ is a function of two variables.

Note that a consequence of this theorem is the equation $ds=\| \kvecrprime(t)\| dt.$ In other words, the change in arc length can be viewed as a change in the $t$ domain, scaled by the magnitude of vector $\kvecrprime(t).$

Example 16.15 · Evaluating a Line Integral

Find the value of integral $\int_{C}(x^{2}+y^{2}+z)ds,$ where $C$ is part of the helix parameterized by $\kvecr(t)=\langle \cos t,\sin t,t\rangle ,$ $0\le t\le 2\pi .$

Solution

To compute a scalar line integral, we start by converting the variable of integration from arc length $s$ to $t$. Then, we can use Equation 16.8 to compute the integral with respect to $t$. Note that $\kf(\kvecr(t))={\cos }^{2}t+{\sin }^{2}t+t=1+t$ and

$$\begin{array}{rl}\sqrt{{(x^{\prime}(t))}^{2}+{(y^{\prime}(t))}^{2}+{(z^{\prime}(t))}^{2}} & =\sqrt{{(-\sin (t))}^{2}+{\cos }^{2}(t)+1} \\ & =\sqrt{2}.\end{array}$$

Therefore,

$$\int_{C}(x^{2}+y^{2}+z)ds=\int_0^{2\pi}(1+t)\sqrt{2}dt.$$

Notice that Equation 16.8 translated the original difficult line integral into a manageable single-variable integral. Since

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_0^{2\pi}(1+t) \sqrt{2}dt & ={[\sqrt{2}t+\frac{\sqrt{2}t^{2}}{2}]}_0^{2\pi} \\ & =2\sqrt{2}\pi +2\sqrt{2}{\pi}^{2},\end{array}$$

we have

$$\int_{C}(x^{2}+y^{2}+z)ds=2\sqrt{2}\pi +2\sqrt{2}{\pi}^{2} .$$

Example 16.16 · Independence of Parameterization

Find the value of integral $\int_{C}(x^{2}+y^{2}+z)ds,$ where $C$ is part of the helix parameterized by $\kvecr(t)=\langle \cos (2t),\sin (2t),2t\rangle ,0\le t\le \pi .$ Notice that this function and curve are the same as in the previous example; the only difference is that the curve has been reparameterized so that time runs twice as fast.

Solution

As with the previous example, we use Equation 16.8 to compute the integral with respect to $t$. Note that $\kf(\kvecr(t))={\cos }^{2}(2t)+{\sin }^{2}(2t)+2t=2t+1$ and

$$\begin{array}{ll}\sqrt{{(x^{\prime}(t))}^{2}+{(y^{\prime}(t))}^{2}+{(z'(t))}^{2}} & =\sqrt{{(\text{-2\sin}(\text{2}t))}^{2}+{(2\cos (\text{2}t))}^{2}+2^{2}} \\ & \sqrt{4{\sin }^{2}(2t)+4{\cos }^{2}(2t)+4} \\ & =2\sqrt{2}\end{array}$$

so we have

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C}(x^{2}+y^{2}+z)ds & =2\sqrt{2}\int_0^{\pi}(1+2t)dt \\ & =2\sqrt{2}{[t+t^{2}]}_0^{\pi} \\ & =2\sqrt{2}(\pi +{\pi}^{2}).\end{array}$$

Notice that this agrees with the answer in the previous example. Changing the parameterization did not change the value of the line integral. Scalar line integrals are independent of parameterization, as long as the curve is traversed exactly once by the parameterization.

Now that we can evaluate line integrals, we can use them to calculate arc length. If $\kf(x,y,z)=1,$ then

$$\begin{array}{ll}\int_{C}\kf(x,y,z)ds & =\lim_{n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \kf(P_i^{*})\Delta s_{i} \\ & =\lim_{n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \Delta s_{i} \\ & =\lim_{n\to \infty}\text{length}(C) \\ & =\text{length}(C).\end{array}$$

Therefore, $\int_{C}1ds$ is the arc length of $C.$

Example 16.17 · Calculating Arc Length

A wire has a shape that can be modeled with the parameterization $\kvecr(t)=\langle \cos t,\sin t,\frac{2}{3}t^{3/2}\rangle ,0\le t\le 4\pi .$ Find the length of the wire.

Solution

The length of the wire is given by $\int_{C}1ds ,$ where $\kC$ is the curve with parameterization $\kvecr$. Therefore,

$$\begin{array}{rl}\text{The length of the wire} & =\int_{C}1ds \\ & =\int_0^{4\pi}\| \kvecrprime(t)\| dt \\ & =\int_0^{4\pi}\sqrt{{(-\sin t)}^{2}+{\cos }^{2}t+t}dt \\ & =\int_0^{4\pi}\sqrt{1+t}dt \\ & ={[\frac{2{(1+t)}^{3/2}}{3}]}_0^{4\pi} \\ & =\frac{2}{3}({(1+4\pi )}^{3/2}-1).\end{array}$$

Vector Line Integrals

The second type of line integrals are vector line integrals, in which we integrate along a curve through a vector field. For example, let

$$\kvecF(x,y,z)=\kP(x,y,z)\kveci+\kQ(x,y,z)\kvecj+\kR(x,y,z)\kveck$$

be a continuous vector field in ${\mathbb{R}}^{3}$ that represents a force on a particle, and let $\kC$ be a smooth curve in ${\mathbb{R}}^{3}$ contained in the domain of $\kvecF.$ How would we compute the work done by $\kvecF$ in moving a particle along $\kC$?

To answer this question, first note that a particle could travel in two directions along a curve: a forward direction and a backward direction. The work done by the vector field depends on the direction in which the particle is moving. Therefore, we must specify a direction along curve $\kC$; such a specified direction is called an orientation of a curve. The specified direction is the positive direction along $\kC$; the opposite direction is the negative direction along $\kC$. When $\kC$ has been given an orientation, $\kC$ is called an oriented curve (Figure 16.16). The work done on the particle depends on the direction along the curve in which the particle is moving.

A closed curve is one for which there exists a parameterization $\kvecr(t),$ $a\le t\le b,$ such that $\kvecr(a)=\kvecr(b),$ and the curve is traversed exactly once. In other words, the parameterization is one-to-one on the domain $(a,b).$

Let $\kvecr(t)$ be a parameterization of $\kC$ for $a\le t\le b$ such that the curve is traversed exactly once by the particle and the particle moves in the positive direction along $\kC$. Divide the parameter interval $[a,b]$ into $n$ subintervals $[t_{i-1},t_{i}],0\le i\le n,$ of equal width. Denote the endpoints of $\kvecr(t_{0}),\kvecr(t_{1})\text{,…},\kvecr(t_{n})$ by $P_{0}\text{,…},P_{n}.$ Points Pi divide $\kC$ into $n$ pieces. Denote the length of the piece from Pi−1 to Pi by $\Delta s_{i}.$ For each $i$, choose a value $t_i^{*}$ in the subinterval $[t_{i-1},t_{i}].$ Then, the endpoint of $\kvecr(t_i^{*})$ is a point in the piece of $\kC$ between $P_{i-1}$ and Pi (Figure 16.17). If $\Delta s_{i}$ is small, then as the particle moves from $P_{i-1}$ to $P_{i}$ along $\kC$, it moves approximately in the direction of $\kvecT(P_{i}),$ the unit tangent vector at the endpoint of $\kvecr(t_i^{*}).$ Let $P_i^{*}$ denote the endpoint of $\kvecr(t_i^{*}).$ Then, the work done by the force vector field in moving the particle from $P_{i-1}$ to Pi is $\kvecF(P_i^{*})\cdot (\Delta s_{i}\kvecT(P_i^{*})),$ so the total work done along $\kC$ is

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} \kvecF(P_i^{*})\cdot (\Delta s_{i}\kvecT(P_i^{*})) =\sum_{i=1}^{n} \kvecF(P_i^{*})\cdot \kvecT(P_i^{*}) \Delta s_{i}.$$

Letting the arc length of the pieces of $\kC$ get arbitrarily small by taking a limit as $n\to \infty$ gives us the work done by the field in moving the particle along $\kC$. Therefore, the work done by $\kvecF$ in moving the particle in the positive direction along $\kC$ is defined as

$$\kW=\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds,$$

which gives us the concept of a vector line integral.

Definition

The vector line integral of vector field $\kvecF$ along oriented smooth curve $\kC$ is

$$\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds= \lim_{n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \kvecF(P_i^{*}) \cdot \kvecT(P_i^{*})\Delta s_{i}$$

if that limit exists.

With scalar line integrals, neither the orientation nor the parameterization of the curve matters. As long as the curve is traversed exactly once by the parameterization, the value of the line integral is unchanged. With vector line integrals, the orientation of the curve does matter. If we think of the line integral as computing work, then this makes sense: if you hike up a mountain, then the gravitational force of Earth does negative work on you. If you walk down the mountain by the exact same path, then Earth’s gravitational force does positive work on you. In other words, reversing the path changes the work value from negative to positive in this case. Note that if $\kC$ is an oriented curve, then we let $-\kC$ represent the same curve but with opposite orientation.

As with scalar line integrals, it is easier to compute a vector line integral if we express it in terms of the parameterization function $\kvecr$ and the variable $t$. To translate the integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds$ in terms of $t$, note that unit tangent vector $\kvecT$ along $\kC$ is given by $\kvecT=\frac{\kvecrprime(t)}{\| \kvecrprime(t)\|}$ (assuming $\| \kvecrprime(t)\| \ne 0).$ Since $ds=\| \kvecrprime(t)\| dt,$ as we saw when discussing scalar line integrals, we have

$$\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds=\kvecF(\kvecr(t))\cdot \frac{\kvecrprime(t)}{\| \kvecrprime(t)\|}\| \kvecrprime(t)\| dt=\kvecF(\kvecr(t))\cdot \kvecrprime(t)dt.$$

Thus, we have the following formula for computing vector line integrals:

$$\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds =\int_a^{b}\kvecF(\kvecr(t)) \cdot \kvecrprime(t)dt.\tag{16.9}$$

Because of Equation 16.9, we often use the notation $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r}$ for the line integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds.$

If $\kvecr(t)=\langle x(t),y(t),z(t)\rangle ,$ then $d\mathbf{r}$ denotes vector differential $\langle x^{\prime}(t),y^{\prime}(t),z^{\prime}(t)\rangle dt.$

Example 16.18 · Evaluating a Vector Line Integral

Find the value of integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r},$ where $C$ is the semicircle parameterized by $\kvecr(t)=\langle \cos t,\sin t\rangle ,$ $0\le t\le \pi$ and $\kvecF=\langle -y,x\rangle .$

Solution

We can use Equation 16.9 to convert the variable of integration from r to $t$. We then have

$$\kvecF(\kvecr(t))=\langle -\sin t,\cos t\rangle \;\text{and}\;\kvecrprime(t)=\langle -\sin t,\cos t\rangle .$$

Therefore,

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C}\kvecF \cdot d\mathbf{r} & =\int_0^{\pi}\langle -\sin t,\cos t\rangle \cdot \langle -\sin t,\cos t\rangle dt \\ & =\int_0^{\pi}{\sin }^{2}t +{\cos }^{2}tdt \\ & =\int_0^{\pi}1dt =\pi .\end{array}$$

See Figure 16.18.

Example 16.19 · Reversing Orientation

Find the value of integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r},$ where $C$ is the semicircle parameterized by $\kvecr(t)=\langle \cos (t+\pi ),\sin t\rangle ,0\le t\le \pi$ and $\kvecF=\langle -y,x\rangle .$

Solution

Notice that this is the same problem as Example 16.18, except the orientation of the curve has been reversed. In this example, the parameterization starts at $\kvecr(0)=\langle –1,0\rangle$ and ends at $\kvecr(\pi )=\langle 1,0\rangle .$ By Equation 16.9,

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C}\kvecF \cdot d\mathbf{r} & =\int_0^{\pi}\langle -\sin t,\cos (t+\pi )\rangle \cdot \langle -\sin (t+\pi ),\cos t\rangle dt \\ & =\int_0^{\pi}\langle -\sin t,-\cos t\rangle \cdot \langle \sin t,\cos t\rangle dt \\ & =\int_0^{\pi}(-{\sin }^{2}t-{\cos }^{2}t)dt \\ & =\int_0^{\pi}-1dt \\ & =-\pi .\end{array}$$

Notice that this is the negative of the answer in Example 16.18. It makes sense that this answer is negative because the orientation of the curve goes against the “flow” of the vector field.

Let $\kC$ be an oriented curve and let $-\kC$ denote the same curve but with the orientation reversed. Then, the previous two examples illustrate the following fact:

$$\int_{–C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r}=-\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r} .$$

That is, reversing the orientation of a curve changes the sign of a line integral.

Another standard notation for integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r}$ is $\int_{C}Pdx+Qdy+Rdz.$ In this notation, $\kP$, $\kQ$, and $\kR$ are functions, and we think of *d*r as vector $\langle dx,dy,dz\rangle .$ To justify this convention, recall that $d\mathbf{r}={\kvecT}ds=\kvecrprime(t)dt=\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\frac{dy}{dt},\frac{dz}{dt}\rangle dt.$ Therefore,

$$\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\langle \kP,\kQ,\kR\rangle \cdot \langle dx,dy,dz\rangle =Pdx+Qdy+Rdz.$$

If $d\mathbf{r}=\langle dx,dy,dz\rangle ,$ then $\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt}=\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\frac{dy}{dt},\frac{dz}{dt}\rangle ,$ which implies that $d\mathbf{r}=\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\frac{dy}{dt},\frac{dz}{dt}\rangle dt.$ Therefore

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r} & =\int_{C}Pdx+Qdy+Rdz \\ & =\int (\kP(\kvecr(t))\frac{dx}{dt}+\kQ(\kvecr(t))\frac{dy}{dt}+\kR(\kvecr(t))\frac{dz}{dt})dt.\end{array}\tag{16.10}$$

Example 16.20 · Finding the Value of an Integral of the Form $\int_{C}Pdx+Qdy+Rdz$

Find the value of integral $\int_{C}zdx+xdy+ydz,$ where $\kC$ is the curve parameterized by $\kvecr(t)=\langle t^{2},\sqrt{t},t\rangle ,1\le t\le 4.$

Solution

As with our previous examples, to compute this line integral we should perform a change of variables to write everything in terms of $t$. In this case, Equation 16.10 allows us to make this change:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C}z dx+xdy+ydz & =\int_1^{4}(t(2t)+t^{2}(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t}})+\sqrt{t})dt \\ & =\int_1^{4}(2t^{2}+\frac{t^{3/2}}{2}+\sqrt{t})dt \\ & ={[\frac{2t^{3}}{3}+\frac{t^{5/2}}{5}+\frac{2t^{3/2}}{3}]}_{t=1}^{t=4} \\ & =\frac{793}{15}.\end{array}$$

We have learned how to integrate smooth oriented curves. Now, suppose that $\kC$ is an oriented curve that is not smooth, but can be written as the union of finitely many smooth curves. In this case, we say that $\kC$ is a piecewise smooth curve. To be precise, curve $\kC$ is piecewise smooth if $\kC$ can be written as a union of $n$ smooth curves $C_{1},C_{2}\text{,…},C_{n}$ such that the endpoint of $C_{i}$ is the starting point of $C_{i+1}$ (Figure 16.19). When curves $C_{i}$ satisfy the condition that the endpoint of $C_{i}$ is the starting point of $C_{i+1},$ we write their union as $C_{1}+C_{2}+\cdots +C_{n}.$

The next theorem summarizes several key properties of vector line integrals.

Theorem

Properties of Vector Line Integrals

Let $\kvecF$ and $\kvecG$ be continuous vector fields with domains that include the oriented smooth curve $\kC$. Then

  1. $\int_{C}(\kvecF+\kvecG)\cdot d\mathbf{r} =\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r} +\int_{C}\kvecG\cdot d\mathbf{r}$
  2. $\int_{C}k{\kvecF}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =k\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r},$ where $k$ is a constant
  3. $\int_{-C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r}= -\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r}$
  4. Suppose instead that $\kC$ is a piecewise smooth curve in the domains of $\kvecF$ and $\kvecG$, where $C=C_{1}+C_{2}+\cdots +C_{n}$ and $C_{1},C_{2}\text{,…},C_{n}$ are smooth curves such that the endpoint of $C_{i}$ is the starting point of $C_{i+1}.$ Then
$$\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r}=\int_{C_{1}}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r}+\int_{C_{2}}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r}+\cdots +\int_{C_{n}}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r}.$$

Notice the similarities between these items and the properties of single-variable integrals. Properties i. and ii. say that line integrals are linear, which is true of single-variable integrals as well. Property iii. says that reversing the orientation of a curve changes the sign of the integral. If we think of the integral as computing the work done on a particle traveling along $\kC$, then this makes sense. If the particle moves backward rather than forward, then the value of the work done has the opposite sign. This is analogous to the equation $\int_a^{b}\kf(x)dx=-\int_b^{a}\kf(x)dx.$ Finally, if $[a_{1},a_{2}],[a_{2},a_{3}]\text{,…},[a_{n-1},a_{n}]$ are intervals, then

$$\int_{a_{1}}^{a_{n}}\kf(x)dx= \int_{a_{1}}^{a_{2}}\kf(x)dx +\int_{a_{2}}^{a_{3}}\kf(x)dx+\cdots +\int_{a_{n-1}}^{a_{n}}\kf(x)dx ,$$

which is analogous to property iv.

Example 16.21 · Using Properties to Compute a Vector Line Integral

Find the value of integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot \kvecT ds,$ where $\kC$ is the rectangle (oriented counterclockwise) in a plane with vertices $(0,0),(2,0),(2,1),\;\text{and}\;(0,1),$ and where $\kvecF=\langle x-2y,y-x\rangle$ (Figure 16.20).

Solution

Note that curve $\kC$ is the union of its four sides, and each side is smooth. Therefore $\kC$ is piecewise smooth. Let $C_{1}$ represent the side from $(0,0)$ to $(2,0),$ let $C_{2}$ represent the side from $(2,0)$ to $(2,1),$ let $C_{3}$ represent the side from $(2,1)$ to $(0,1),$ and let $C_{4}$ represent the side from $(0,1)$ to $(0,0)$ (Figure 16.20). Then,

$$\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}d\mathbf{r} =\int_{C_{1}}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}d\mathbf{r} +\int_{C_{2}}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}d\mathbf{r} +\int_{C_{3}}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}d\mathbf{r} +\int_{C_{4}}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}d\mathbf{r} .$$

We want to compute each of the four integrals on the right-hand side using Equation 16.8. Before doing this, we need a parameterization of each side of the rectangle. Here are four parameterizations (note that they traverse $\kC$ counterclockwise):

$$\begin{array}{l}C_{1}:\langle t,0\rangle ,0\le t\le 2 \\ C_{2}:\langle 2,t\rangle ,0\le t\le 1 \\ C_{3}:\langle 2-t,1\rangle ,0\le t\le 2 \\ C_{4}:\langle 0,1-t\rangle ,0\le t\le 1.\end{array}$$

Therefore,

$$\begin{array}{ll}\int_{C_{1}}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}d\mathbf{r} & =\int_0^{2}\kvecF(\kvecr(t))\cdot r^{\prime}(t)dt \\ & =\int_0^{2}\langle t-2(0),0-t\rangle \cdot \langle 1,0\rangle dt=\int_0^{2}tdt \\ & ={[\frac{t^{2}}{2}]}_0^{2}=2.\end{array}$$

Notice that the value of this integral is positive, which should not be surprising. As we move along curve C1 from left to right, our movement flows in the general direction of the vector field itself. At any point along C1, the tangent vector to the curve and the corresponding vector in the field form an angle that is less than 90°. Therefore, the tangent vector and the force vector have a positive dot product all along C1, and the line integral will have positive value.

The calculations for the three other line integrals are done similarly:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C_{2}}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r} & =\int_0^{1}\langle 2-2t,t-2\rangle \cdot \langle 0,1\rangle dt \\ & =\int_0^{1}(t-2)dt \\ & ={[\frac{t^{2}}{2}-2t]}_0^{1}=-\frac{3}{2},\end{array}$$ $$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C_{3}}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds & =\int_0^{2}\langle (2-t)-2,1-(2-t)\rangle \cdot \langle -1,0\rangle dt \\ & =\int_0^{2}tdt =2,\end{array}$$

and

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C_{4}}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r} & =\int_0^{1}\langle -2(1-t),1-t\rangle \cdot \langle 0,-1\rangle dt \\ & =\int_0^{1}(t-1)dt \\ & ={[\frac{t^{2}}{2}-t]}_0^{1}=-\frac{1}{2}.\end{array}$$

Thus, we have $\int_{C}\kvecF \cdot d\mathbf{r}=2.$

Applications of Line Integrals

Scalar line integrals have many applications. They can be used to calculate the length or mass of a wire, the surface area of a sheet of a given height, or the electric potential of a charged wire given a linear charge density. Vector line integrals are extremely useful in physics. They can be used to calculate the work done on a particle as it moves through a force field, or the flow rate of a fluid across a curve. Here, we calculate the mass of a wire using a scalar line integral and the work done by a force using a vector line integral.

Suppose that a piece of wire is modeled by curve $\kC$ in space. The mass per unit length (the linear density) of the wire is a continuous function $\krho (x,y,z).$ We can calculate the total mass of the wire using the scalar line integral $\int_{C}\krho (x,y,z)ds.$ The reason is that mass is density multiplied by length, and therefore the density of a small piece of the wire can be approximated by $\krho (x*,y*,z*)\Delta s$ for some point $(x*,y*,z*)$ in the piece. Letting the length of the pieces shrink to zero with a limit yields the line integral $\int_{C}\krho (x,y,z)ds.$

Example 16.22 · Calculating the Mass of a Wire

Calculate the mass of a spring in the shape of a curve parameterized by $\langle t,2\;\cos t,2\;\sin t\rangle ,$ $0\le t\le \frac{\pi}{2},$ with a density function given by $\krho (x,y,z)=e^{x}+yz$ kg/m (Figure 16.21).

Solution

To calculate the mass of the spring, we must find the value of the scalar line integral $\int_{C}(e^{x}+yz)ds ,$ where $\kC$ is the given helix. To calculate this integral, we write it in terms of $t$ using Equation 16.8:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C}e^{x} +yzds & =\int_0^{\pi /2}((e^{t}+4\;\cos t\;\sin t)\sqrt{1+{(-2\;\cos t)}^{2}+{(2\;\sin t)}^{2}})dt \\ & =\int_0^{\pi /2}((e^{t}+4\;\cos t\;\sin t)\sqrt{5})dt \\ & =\sqrt{5}{[e^{t}+2\;{\sin }^{2}t]}_{t=0}^{t=\pi /2} \\ & =\sqrt{5}(e^{\pi /2}+1).\end{array}$$

Therefore, the mass is $\sqrt{5}(e^{\pi /2}+1)$ kg.

When we first defined vector line integrals, we used the concept of work to motivate the definition. Therefore, it is not surprising that calculating the work done by a vector field representing a force is a standard use of vector line integrals. Recall that if an object moves along curve $\kC$ in force field $\kvecF$, then the work required to move the object is given by $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r} .$

Example 16.23 · Calculating Work

How much work is required to move an object in vector force field $\kvecF=\langle yz,xy,xz\rangle$ along path $\kvecr(t)=\langle t^{2},t,t^{4}\rangle ,$ $0\le t\le 1?$ See Figure 16.22.

Solution

Let $\kC$ denote the given path. We need to find the value of $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot d\mathbf{r} .$ To do this, we use Equation 16.9:

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C}\kvecF \cdot d\mathbf{r} & =\int_0^{1}(\langle t^{5},t^{3},t^{6}\rangle \cdot \langle 2t,1,4t^{3}\rangle )dt \\ & =\int_0^{1}(2t^{6}+t^{3}+4t^{9})dt \\ & ={[\frac{2t^{7}}{7}+\frac{t^{4}}{4}+\frac{2t^{10}}{5}]}_{t=0}^{t=1}=\frac{131}{140}.\end{array}$$

Flux and Circulation

We close this section by discussing two key concepts related to line integrals: flux across a plane curve and circulation along a plane curve. Flux is used in applications to calculate fluid flow across a curve, and the concept of circulation is important for characterizing conservative gradient fields in terms of line integrals. Both these concepts are used heavily throughout the rest of this chapter. The idea of flux is especially important for Green’s theorem, and in higher dimensions for Stokes’ theorem and the divergence theorem.

Let $\kC$ be a plane curve and let $\kvecF$ be a vector field in the plane. Imagine $\kC$ is a membrane across which fluid flows, but $\kC$ does not impede the flow of the fluid. In other words, $\kC$ is an idealized membrane invisible to the fluid. Suppose $\kvecF$ represents the velocity field of the fluid. How could we quantify the rate at which the fluid is crossing $\kC$?

Recall that the line integral of $\kvecF$ along $\kC$ is $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds$—in other words, the line integral is the dot product of the vector field with the unit tangential vector with respect to arc length. If we replace the unit tangential vector with unit normal vector $\kvecN(t)$ and instead compute integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecN}ds ,$ we determine the flux across $\kC$. To be precise, the definition of integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecN}ds$ is the same as integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds ,$ except the $\kvecT$ in the Riemann sum is replaced with $\kvecN$. Therefore, the flux across $\kC$ is defined as

$$\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecN}ds= \lim_{n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \kvecF(P_i^{*}) \cdot \kvecN(P_i^{*})\Delta s_{i},$$

where $P_i^{*}$ and $\Delta s_{i}$ are defined as they were for integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds .$ Therefore, a flux integral is an integral that is perpendicular to a vector line integral, because $\kvecN$ and $\kvecT$ are perpendicular vectors.

If $\kvecF$ is a velocity field of a fluid and $\kC$ is a curve that represents a membrane, then the flux of $\kvecF$ across $\kC$ is the quantity of fluid flowing across $\kC$ per unit time, or the rate of flow.

More formally, let $\kC$ be a plane curve parameterized by $\kvecr(t)=\langle x(t),y(t)\rangle ,$ $a\le t\le b.$ Let $\kvecn(t)=\langle y^{\prime}(t),-x^{\prime}(t)\rangle$ be the vector that is normal to $\kC$ at the endpoint of $\kvecr(t)$ and points to the right as we traverse $\kC$ in the positive direction (Figure 16.23). Then, $\kvecN(t)=\frac{\kvecn(t)}{\| \kvecn(t)\|}$ is the unit normal vector to $\kC$ at the endpoint of $\kvecr(t)$ that points to the right as we traverse $\kC$.

Definition

The flux of $\kvecF$ across $\kC$ is line integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot \frac{\kvecn(t)}{\| \kvecn(t)\|} ds.$

We now give a formula for calculating the flux across a curve. This formula is analogous to the formula used to calculate a vector line integral (see Equation 16.9).

Theorem

Calculating Flux across a Curve

Let $\kvecF$ be a vector field and let $\kC$ be a smooth curve with parameterization $\kvecr(t)=\langle x(t),y(t)\rangle ,a\le t\le b.$ Let $\kvecn(t)=\langle y^{\prime}(t),-x^{\prime}(t)\rangle .$ The flux of $\kvecF$ across $\kC$ is

$$\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecN}ds=\int_a^{b}\kvecF(\kvecr(t))\cdot \kvecn(t)dt\tag{16.11}$$

Proof

The proof of Equation 16.11 is similar to the proof of Equation 16.8. Before deriving the formula, note that $\| \kvecn(t)\| =\| \langle y'(t),-x'(t)\rangle \| =\sqrt{{(y'(t))}^{2}+{(x'(t))}^{2}}=\| \kvecrprime(t)\| .$ Therefore,

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C}\kvecF \cdot {\kvecN}ds & =\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot \frac{\kvecn(t)}{\| \kvecn(t)\|}ds \\ & =\int_a^{b}\kvecF\cdot \frac{\kvecn(t)}{\| \kvecn(t)\|}\| \kvecrprime(t)\| dt \\ & =\int_a^{b}\kvecF(\kvecr(t))\cdot \kvecn(t)dt .\end{array}$$

□

Example 16.24 · Flux across a Curve

Calculate the flux of $\kvecF=\langle 2x,2y\rangle$ across a unit circle oriented counterclockwise (Figure 16.24).

Solution

To compute the flux, we first need a parameterization of the unit circle. We can use the standard parameterization $\kvecr(t)=\langle \cos t,\sin t\rangle ,$ $0\le t\le 2\pi .$ The normal vector to a unit circle is $\langle \cos t,\sin t\rangle .$ Therefore, the flux is

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot \kvecN ds & =\int_0^{2\pi}\langle 2\;\cos t,2\;\sin t\rangle \cdot \langle \cos t,\sin t\rangle \;dt \\ & =\int_0^{2\pi}(2\;{\cos }^{2}t+2\;{\sin }^{2}t) \;dt=2\int_0^{2\pi}({\cos }^{2}t+{\sin }^{2}t) \;dt \\ & =2\int_0^{2\pi}dt =4\pi .\end{array}$$

Let $\kvecF(x,y)=\langle \kP(x,y),\kQ(x,y)\rangle$ be a two-dimensional vector field. Recall that integral $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot \kvecT ds$ is sometimes written as $\int_{C}Pdx+Qdy .$ Analogously, flux $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecN}ds$ is sometimes written in the notation $\int_{C}-Qdx+Pdy,$ because the unit normal vector $\kvecN$ is perpendicular to the unit tangent $\kvecT$. Rotating the vector $d\mathbf{r}=\langle dx,dy\rangle$ by 90° results in vector $\langle dy,-dx\rangle .$ Therefore, the line integral in Example 16.21 can be written as $\int_{C}-2ydx+2xdy .$

Now that we have defined flux, we can turn our attention to circulation. The line integral of vector field $\kvecF$ along an oriented closed curve is called the circulation of $\kvecF$ along $\kC$. Circulation line integrals have their own notation: $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot \kvecT ds .$ The circle on the integral symbol denotes that $\kC$ is “circular” in that it has no endpoints. Example 16.18 shows a calculation of circulation.

To see where the term circulation comes from and what it measures, let $\kvecv$ represent the velocity field of a fluid and let $\kC$ be an oriented closed curve. At a particular point $\kP$, the closer the direction of $\kvecv$($\kP$) is to the direction of $\kvecT$($\kP$), the larger the value of the dot product $\kvecv(\kP)\cdot \kvecT(\kP).$ The maximum value of $\kvecv(\kP)\cdot \kvecT(\kP)$ occurs when the two vectors are pointing in the exact same direction; the minimum value of $\kvecv(\kP)\cdot \kvecT(\kP)$ occurs when the two vectors are pointing in opposite directions. Thus, the value of the circulation $\int_{C}\kvecv\cdot {\kvecT}ds$ measures the tendency of the fluid to move in the direction of $\kC$.

Example 16.25 · Calculating Circulation

Let $\kvecF=\langle -y,x\rangle$ be the vector field from Example 16.16 and let $\kC$ represent the unit circle oriented counterclockwise. Calculate the circulation of $\kvecF$ along $\kC$.

Solution

We use the standard parameterization of the unit circle: $\kvecr(t)=\langle \cos t,\sin t\rangle ,0\le t\le 2\pi .$ Then, $\kvecF(\kvecr(t))=\langle -\sin t,\cos t\rangle$ and $\kvecrprime(t)=\langle -\sin t,\cos t\rangle .$ Therefore, the circulation of $\kvecF$ along $\kC$ is

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C}\kvecF \cdot {\kvecT}ds & =\int_0^{2\pi}\langle -\sin t,\cos t\rangle \cdot \langle -\sin t,\cos t\rangle dt \\ & =\int_0^{2\pi}({\sin }^{2}t+{\cos }^{2}t) \;dt \\ & =\int_0^{2\pi}dt =2\pi .\end{array}$$

Notice that the circulation is positive. The reason for this is that the orientation of $\kC$ “flows” with the direction of $\kvecF$. At any point along the circle, the tangent vector and the vector from $\kvecF$ form an angle of less than 90°, and therefore the corresponding dot product is positive.

In Example 16.25, what if we had oriented the unit circle clockwise? We denote the unit circle oriented clockwise by $-C.$ Then

$$\int_{-C}\kvecF\cdot \kvecT ds=-\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot \kvecT ds=-2\pi .$$

Notice that the circulation is negative in this case. The reason for this is that the orientation of the curve flows against the direction of $\kvecF$.

Example 16.26 · Calculating Work

Calculate the work done on a particle that traverses circle $\kC$ of radius 2 centered at the origin, oriented counterclockwise, by field $\kvecF(x,y)=\langle -2,y\rangle .$ Assume the particle starts its movement at $(1,0).$

Solution

The work done by $\kvecF$ on the particle is the circulation of $\kvecF$ along $\kC$: $\int_{C}\kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds .$ We use the parameterization $\kvecr(t)=\langle 2\;\cos t,2\;\sin t\rangle ,0\le t\le 2\pi$ for $\kC$. Then, $\kvecrprime(t)=\langle -2\;\sin t,2\;\cos t\rangle$ and $\kvecF(\kvecr(t))=\langle -2,2\;\sin t\rangle .$ Therefore, the circulation of $\kvecF$ along $\kC$ is

$$\begin{array}{rl}\int_{C} \kvecF\cdot {\kvecT}ds & =\int_0^{2\pi}\langle -2,2\;\sin t\rangle \cdot \langle -2\;\sin t,2\;\cos t\rangle dt \\ & =\int_0^{2\pi}(4\;\sin t+4\;\sin t\;\cos t)dt \\ & ={[-4\;\cos t+4\;{\sin }^{2}t]}_0^{2\pi} \\ & =(-4\;\cos (2\pi )+2\;{\sin }^{2}(2\pi ))-(-4\;\cos (0)+4\;{\sin }^{2}(0)) \\ & =-4+4=0.\end{array}$$

The force field does zero work on the particle.

Notice that the circulation of $\kvecF$ along $\kC$ is zero. Furthermore, notice that since $\kvecF$ is the gradient of $\kf(x,y)=-2x+\frac{y^{2}}{2},$ $\kvecF$ is conservative. We prove in a later section that under certain broad conditions, the circulation of a conservative vector field along a closed curve is zero.
