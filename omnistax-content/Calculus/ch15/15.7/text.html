Recall from Substitution Rule the method of integration by substitution. When evaluating an integral such as $\int_2^{3}x{(x^{2}-4)}^{5}dx,$ we substitute $u=\kg(x)=x^{2}-4.$ Then $du=2x\;dx$ or $x\;dx=\frac{1}{2}du$ and the limits change to $u=\kg(2)=2^{2}-4=0$ and $u=\kg(3)=9-4=5.$ Thus the integral becomes $\int_0^{5}\frac{1}{2}u^{5}du$ and this integral is much simpler to evaluate. In other words, when solving integration problems, we make appropriate substitutions to obtain an integral that becomes much simpler than the original integral.

We also used this idea when we transformed double integrals in rectangular coordinates to polar coordinates and transformed triple integrals in rectangular coordinates to cylindrical or spherical coordinates to make the computations simpler. More generally,

$$\int_a^{b} \kf(x)dx= \int_c^{d} \kf(\kg(u)) g'(u)du,$$

Where $x=\kg(u),dx=g'(u)du,$ and $u=c$ and $u=d$ satisfy $c=g^{-1}(a)$ and $d=g^{-1}(b).$

A similar result occurs in double integrals when we substitute $x=\kh(r,\theta )=r\;\cos \theta ,$ $y=\kg(r,\theta )=r\;\sin \theta ,$ and $dA=dx\;dy=r\;dr\;d\theta .$ Then we get

$$\iint\limits_{\kR}\kf(x,y) dA=\iint\limits_{\kS}\kf(r\;\cos \theta ,r\;\sin \theta )r\;dr\;d\theta$$

where the domain $\kR$ is replaced by the domain $\kS$ in polar coordinates. Generally, the function that we use to change the variables to make the integration simpler is called a transformation or mapping.

Planar Transformations

A planar transformation $T$ is a function that transforms a region $\kG$ in one plane into a region $\kR$ in another plane by a change of variables. Both $\kG$ and $\kR$ are subsets of ${\mathbb{R}}^{2}.$ For example, Figure 15.71 shows a region $\kG$ in the $uv\text{-plane}$ transformed into a region $\kR$ in the $xy\text{-plane}$ by the change of variables $x=\kg(u,v)$ and $y=\kh(u,v),$ or sometimes we write $x=x(u,v)$ and $y=y(u,v).$ We shall typically assume that each of these functions has continuous first partial derivatives, which means $g_{u},g_{v},h_{u},$ and $h_{v}$ exist and are also continuous. The need for this requirement will become clear soon.

Definition

A transformation $T\text{:}\;\kG\to \kR,$ defined as $\kT(u,v)=(x,y),$ is said to be a one-to-one transformation if no two points map to the same image point.

To show that $T$ is a one-to-one transformation, we assume $\kT(u_{1},v_{1})=\kT(u_{2},v_{2})$ and show that as a consequence we obtain $(u_{1},v_{1})=(u_{2},v_{2}).$ If the transformation $T$ is one-to-one in the domain $\kG,$ then the inverse $\kT^{-1}$ exists with the domain $\kR$ such that $\kT^{-1}\circ T$ and $\kT\circ \kT^{-1}$ are identity functions.

Figure 15.71 shows the mapping $\kT(u,v)=(x,y)$ where $x$ and $y$ are related to $u$ and $v$ by the equations $x=\kg(u,v)$ and $y=\kh(u,v).$ The region $\kG$ is the domain of $T$ and the region $\kR$ is the range of $T,$ also known as the image of $\kG$ under the transformation $T.$

Example 15.65 · Determining How the Transformation Works

Suppose a transformation $T$ is defined as $\kT(r,\theta )=(x,y)$ where $x=r\;\cos \theta ,y=r\;\sin \theta .$ Find the image of the polar rectangle $\kG=\{(r,\theta )|0\lt r\le 1,0\le \theta \le \pi /2\}$ in the $r\theta \text{-plane}$ to a region $\kR$ in the $xy\text{-plane}\text{.}$ Show that $T$ is a one-to-one transformation in $\kG$ and find $\kT^{-1}(x,y).$

Solution

Since $r$ varies from 0 to 1 in the $r\theta \text{-plane},$ we have a circular disc of radius 0 to 1 in the $xy\text{-plane}\text{.}$ Because $\theta$ varies from 0 to $\pi \text{/2}$ in the $r\theta \text{-plane},$ we end up getting a quarter circle of radius $1$ in the first quadrant of the $xy\text{-plane}$ (Figure 15.72). Hence $\kR$ is a quarter circle bounded by $x^{2}+y^{2}=1$ in the first quadrant.

In order to show that $T$ is a one-to-one transformation, assume $\kT(r_{1},{\theta}_{1})=\kT(r_{2},{\theta}_{2})$ and show as a consequence that $(r_{1},{\theta}_{1})=(r_{2},{\theta}_{2}).$ In this case, we have

$$\begin{array}{lll}\kT(r_{1},{\theta}_{1}) & = & \kT(r_{2},{\theta}_{2}), \\ (x_{1},y_{1}) & = & (x_{2},y_{2}), \\ (r_{1}\cos {\theta}_{1},r_{1}\sin {\theta}_{1}) & = & (r_{2}\cos {\theta}_{2},r_{2}\sin {\theta}_{2}), \\ r_{1}\cos {\theta}_{1} & = & r_{2}\cos {\theta}_{2} \\ r_{1}\sin {\theta}_{1} & = & r_{2}\sin {\theta}_{2}\end{array}$$

Dividing, we obtain

$$\begin{array}{rll}\frac{r_{1}\cos {\theta}_{1}}{r_{1}\sin {\theta}_{1}} & = & \frac{r_{2}\cos {\theta}_{2}}{r_{2}\sin {\theta}_{2}} \\ \frac{\cos {\theta}_{1}}{\sin {\theta}_{1}} & = & \frac{\cos {\theta}_{2}}{\sin {\theta}_{2}} \\ \cot {\theta}_{1} & = & \cot {\theta}_{2} \\ {\theta}_{1} & = & {\theta}_{2}\end{array}$$

since the cotangent function is one-one function in the interval $0\le \theta \le \pi /2.$ Also, since $0\lt r\le 1,$ we have $r_{1}=r_{2},{\theta}_{1}={\theta}_{2}.$ Therefore, $(r_{1},{\theta}_{1})=(r_{2},{\theta}_{2})$ and $T$ is a one-to-one transformation from $\kG$ into $\kR.$

To find $\kT^{-1}(x,y)$ solve for $r,\theta$ in terms of $x,y.$ We already know that $r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}$ and $\tan \theta =\frac{y}{x}.$ Thus $\kT^{-1}(x,y)=(r,\theta )$ is defined as $r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}$ and $\theta ={\tan }^{-1}(\frac{y}{x}).$

Example 15.66 · Finding the Image under $T$

Let the transformation $T$ be defined by $\kT(u,v)=(x,y)$ where $x=u^{2}-v^{2}$ and $y=uv.$ Find the image of the triangle in the $uv\text{-plane}$ with vertices $(0,0),(0,1),$ and $(1,1).$

Solution

The triangle and its image are shown in Figure 15.73. To understand how the sides of the triangle transform, call the side that joins $(0,0)$ and $(0,1)$ side $A,$ the side that joins $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ side $B,$ and the side that joins $(1,1)$ and $(0,1)$ side $C.$

For the side $A\text{:}\;u=0,0\le v\le 1$ transforms to $x=-v^{2},y=0$ so this is the side $A'$ that joins $(-1,0)$ and $(0,0).$

For the side $B\text{:}\;u=v,0\le u\le 1$ transforms to $x=0,y=u^{2}$ so this is the side $B^{\prime}$ that joins $(0,0)$ and $(0,1).$

For the side $C\text{:}\;0\le u\le 1,v=1$ transforms to $x=u^{2}-1,y=u$ (hence $x=y^{2}-1)$ so this is the side $C^{\prime}$ that makes the upper half of the parabolic arc joining $(-1,0)$ and $(0,1).$

All the points in the entire region of the triangle in the $uv\text{-plane}$ are mapped inside the parabolic region in the $xy\text{-plane}\text{.}$

Jacobians

Recall that we mentioned near the beginning of this section that each of the component functions must have continuous first partial derivatives, which means that $g_{u},g_{v},h_{u},$ and $h_{v}$ exist and are also continuous. A transformation that has this property is called a $C^{1}$ transformation (here $C$ denotes continuous). Let $\kT(u,v)=(\kg(u,v),\kh(u,v)),$ where $x=\kg(u,v)$ and $y=\kh(u,v),$ be a one-to-one $C^{1}$ transformation. We want to see how it transforms a small rectangular region $\kS,$ $\Delta u$ units by $\Delta v$ units, in the $uv\text{-plane}$ (see the following figure).

Since $x=\kg(u,v)$ and $y=\kh(u,v),$ we have the position vector $\mathbf{r}(u,v)=\kg(u,v)\mathbf{i}+\kh(u,v)\mathbf{j}$ of the image of the point $(u,v).$ Suppose that $(u_{0},v_{0})$ is the coordinate of the point at the lower left corner that mapped to $(x_{0},y_{0})=\kT(u_{0},v_{0}).$ The line $v=v_{0}$ maps to the image curve with vector function $\text{r}(u,v_{0}),$ and the tangent vector at $(x_{0},y_{0})$ to the image curve is

$${\mathbf{r}}_{u}=g_{u}(u_{0},v_{0})\mathbf{i}+h_{u}(u_{0},v_{0})\mathbf{j}=\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\mathbf{i}+\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}\mathbf{j}.$$

Similarly, the line $u=u_{0}$ maps to the image curve with vector function $\mathbf{r}(u_{0},v),$ and the tangent vector at $(x_{0},y_{0})$ to the image curve is

$${\mathbf{r}}_{v}=g_{v}(u_{0},v_{0})\mathbf{i}+h_{v}(u_{0},v_{0})\mathbf{j}=\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\mathbf{i}+\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}\mathbf{j}.$$

Now, note that

$${\mathbf{r}}_{u}=\lim_{\Delta u\to 0}\frac{\mathbf{r}(u_{0}+\Delta u,v_{0})-\mathbf{r}(u_{0},v_{0})}{\Delta u}\;\text{so}\;\mathbf{r}(u_{0}+\Delta u,v_{0})-\mathbf{r}(u_{0},v_{0})\approx \Delta u{\mathbf{r}}_{u}.$$

Similarly,

$${\mathbf{r}}_{v}=\lim_{\Delta v\to 0}\frac{\mathbf{r}(u_{0},v_{0}+\Delta v)-\mathbf{r}(u_{0},v_{0})}{\Delta v}\;\text{so}\;\mathbf{r}(u_{0},v_{0}+\Delta v)-\mathbf{r}(u_{0},v_{0})\approx \Delta v{\mathbf{r}}_{v}.$$

This allows us to estimate the area $\Delta A$ of the image $\kR$ by finding the area of the parallelogram formed by the sides $\Delta v{\mathbf{r}}_{v}$ and $\Delta u{\mathbf{r}}_{u}.$ By using the cross product of these two vectors by adding the k component as $0,$ the area $\Delta A$ of the image $\kR$ (refer to The Cross Product) is approximately $\mathbf{||\textbf{\Delta }{ur}_{u}\;\times \;\textbf{\Delta }{vr}_{v}||}=\mathbf{||r_{u}\;\times \;r_{v}||}\Delta u\Delta v.$ In determinant form, the cross product is

$${\mathbf{r}}_{u}\;\times \;{\mathbf{r}}_{v}=|\begin{array}{clclc}\mathbf{i} & & \mathbf{j} & & \mathbf{k} \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & & 0 \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} & & 0\end{array}|=|\begin{array}{lll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v}\end{array}|\mathbf{k}=(\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\;\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}-\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\;\frac{\partial y}{\partial u})\mathbf{k}.$$

Since $\mathbf{||k||}=1,$ we have $\Delta A\approx \mathbf{||r_{u}\;\times \;r_{v}||}\Delta u\Delta v=(\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\;\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}-\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\;\frac{\partial y}{\partial u})\Delta u\Delta v.$

Definition

The Jacobian of the $C^{1}$ transformation $\kT(u,v)=(\kg(u,v),\kh(u,v))$ is denoted by $\kJ(u,v)$ and is defined by the $2\;\times \;2$ determinant

$$\kJ(u,v)=|\frac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (u,v)}|=|\begin{array}{lll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v}\end{array}|=(\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\;\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}-\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\;\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}).$$

Using the definition, we have

$$\Delta A\approx \kJ(u,v)\Delta u\Delta v=|\frac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (u,v)}|\Delta u\Delta v.$$

Note that the Jacobian is frequently denoted simply by

$$\kJ(u,v)=\frac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (u,v)}.$$

Note also that

$$|\begin{array}{lll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v}\end{array}|=(\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}\;\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}-\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}\;\frac{\partial y}{\partial u})=|\begin{array}{lll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v}\end{array}|.$$

Hence the notation $\kJ(u,v)=\frac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (u,v)}$ suggests that we can write the Jacobian determinant with partials of $x$ in the first row and partials of $y$ in the second row.

Example 15.67 · Finding the Jacobian

Find the Jacobian of the transformation given in Example 15.65.

Solution

The transformation in the example is $\kT(r,\theta )=(r\;\cos \theta ,r\;\sin \theta )$ where $x=r\;\cos \theta$ and $y=r\;\sin \theta .$ Thus the Jacobian is

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kJ(r,\theta ) & =\frac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (r,\theta )}=|\begin{array}{lll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial r} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial r} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta}\end{array}|=|\begin{array}{llr}\cos \theta & & -r\;\sin \theta \\ \sin \theta & & r\;\cos \theta \end{array}| \\ & =r\;{\cos }^{2}\theta +r\;{\sin }^{2}\theta =r({\cos }^{2}\theta +{\sin }^{2}\theta )=r.\end{array}$$

Example 15.68 · Finding the Jacobian

Find the Jacobian of the transformation given in Example 15.66.

Solution

The transformation in the example is $\kT(u,v)=(u^{2}-v^{2},uv)$ where $x=u^{2}-v^{2}$ and $y=uv.$ Thus the Jacobian is

$$\kJ(u,v)=\frac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (u,v)}=|\begin{array}{lll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v}\end{array}|=|\begin{array}{lll}2u & & v \\ -2v & & u\end{array}|=2u^{2}+2v^{2}.$$

Change of Variables for Double Integrals

We have already seen that, under the change of variables $\kT(u,v)=(x,y)$ where $x=\kg(u,v)$ and $y=\kh(u,v),$ a small region $\Delta A$ in the $xy\text{-plane}$ is related to the area formed by the product $\Delta u\Delta v$ in the $uv\text{-plane}$ by the approximation

$$\Delta A\approx \kJ(u,v)\Delta u,\Delta v.$$

Now let’s go back to the definition of double integral for a minute:

$$\iint\limits_{\kR}\kf(x,y)dA=\lim_{m,n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \kf(x_{ij},y_{ij}) \Delta A.$$

Referring to Figure 15.75, observe that we divided the region $\kS$ in the $uv\text{-plane}$ into small subrectangles $\kS_{ij}$ and we let the subrectangles $\kR_{ij}$ in the $xy\text{-plane}$ be the images of $\kS_{ij}$ under the transformation $\kT(u,v)=(x,y).$

Then the double integral becomes

$$\iint\limits_{\kR}\kf(x,y)dA=\lim_{m,n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \kf(x_{ij},y_{ij}) \Delta A=\lim_{m,n\to \infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \kf(\kg(u_{ij},v_{ij}),\kh(u_{ij},v_{ij})) |\kJ(u_{ij},v_{ij})|\Delta u\Delta v.$$

Notice this is exactly the double Riemann sum for the integral

$$\iint\limits_{\kS}\kf(\kg(u,v),\kh(u,v)) |\frac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (u,v)}|du\;dv.$$
Theorem

Change of Variables for Double Integrals

Let $\kT(u,v)=(x,y)$ where $x=\kg(u,v)$ and $y=\kh(u,v)$ be a one-to-one $C^{1}$ transformation, with a nonzero Jacobian on the interior of the region $\kS$ in the $uv\text{-plane;}$ it maps $\kS$ into the region $\kR$ in the $xy\text{-plane}\text{.}$ If $\kf$ is continuous on $\kR,$ then

$$\iint\limits_{\kR}\kf(x,y) dA=\iint\limits_{\kS}\kf(\kg(u,v),\kh(u,v)) |\frac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (u,v)}|du\;dv.$$

With this theorem for double integrals, we can change the variables from $(x,y)$ to $(u,v)$ in a double integral simply by replacing

$$dA=dx\;dy=|\frac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (u,v)}|du\;dv$$

when we use the substitutions $x=\kg(u,v)$ and $y=\kh(u,v)$ and then change the limits of integration accordingly. This change of variables often makes any computations much simpler.

Example 15.69 · Changing Variables from Rectangular to Polar Coordinates

Consider the integral

$$\int_0^{2} \;\int_0^{\sqrt{2x-x^{2}}} \sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\;dy\;dx .$$

Use the change of variables $x=r\;\cos \theta$ and $y=r\;\sin \theta ,$ and find the resulting integral.

Solution

First we need to find the region of integration. This region is bounded below by $y=0$ and above by $y=\sqrt{2x-x^{2}}$ (see the following figure).

Squaring and collecting terms, we find that the region is the upper half of the circle $x^{2}+y^{2}-2x=0,$ that is, $y^{2}+{(x-1)}^{2}=1.$ In polar coordinates, the circle is $r=2\;\cos \theta$ so the region of integration in polar coordinates is bounded by $0\le r\le 2\cos \theta$ and $0\le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}.$

The Jacobian is $\kJ(r,\theta )=r,$ as shown in Example 15.67. Since $r\ge 0,$ we have $|\kJ(r,\theta )|=r.$

The integrand $\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}$ changes to $r$ in polar coordinates, so the double iterated integral is

$$\int_0^{2} \;\int_0^{\sqrt{2x-x^{2}}} \sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}} dy\;dx=\int_0^{\pi /2} \;\int_0^{2\;\cos \theta} r|\kJ(r,\theta )| dr\;d\theta =\int_0^{\pi /2} \;\int_0^{2\;\cos \theta} r^{2} dr\;d\theta .$$

Notice in the next example that the region over which we are to integrate may suggest a suitable transformation for the integration. This is a common and important situation.

Example 15.70 · Changing Variables

Consider the integral $\iint\limits_{\kR}(x-y) dy\;dx,$ where $\kR$ is the parallelogram joining the points $(1,2),$ $(3,4),(4,3),$ and $(6,5)$ (Figure 15.77). Make appropriate changes of variables, and write the resulting integral.

Solution

First, we need to understand the region over which we are to integrate. The sides of the parallelogram are $x-y+1=0,x-y-1=0,$ $x-3y+5=0,\text{and}\;x-3y+9=0$ (Figure 15.78). Another way to look at them is $x-y=-1,x-y=1,$ $x-3y=-5,$ and $x-3y=-9.$

Clearly the parallelogram is bounded by the lines $y=x+1,y=x-1,y=\frac{1}{3}(x+5),$ and $y=\frac{1}{3}(x+9).$

Notice that if we were to make $u=x-y$ and $v=x-3y,$ then the limits on the integral would be $-1\le u\le 1$ and $-9\le v\le -5.$

To solve for $x$ and $y,$ we multiply the first equation by $3$ and subtract the second equation, $3u-v=(3x-3y)-(x-3y)=2x.$ Then we have $x=\frac{3u-v}{2}.$ Moreover, if we simply subtract the second equation from the first, we get $u-v=(x-y)-(x-3y)=2y$ and $y=\frac{u-v}{2}.$

Thus, we can choose the transformation

$$\kT(u,v)=(\frac{3u-v}{2},\frac{u-v}{2})$$

and compute the Jacobian $\kJ(u,v).$ We have

$$\kJ(u,v)=\frac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (u,v)}=|\begin{array}{lll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v}\end{array}|=|\begin{array}{lll}3/2 & & -1/2 \\ 1/2 & & -1/2\end{array}|=-\frac{3}{4}+\frac{1}{4}=-\frac{1}{2}.$$

Therefore, $|\kJ(u,v)|=\frac{1}{2}.$ Also, the original integrand becomes

$$x-y=\frac{1}{2}[3u-v-u+v]=\frac{1}{2}[3u-u]=\frac{1}{2}[2u]=u.$$

Therefore, by the use of the transformation $T,$ the integral changes to

$$\iint\limits_{\kR}(x-y)dy\;dx=\int_{-9}^{-5} \;\int_{-1}^{1} \kJ(u,v)u\;du\;dv=\int_{-9}^{-5} \;\int_{-1}^{1} (\frac{1}{2}) u\;du\;dv,$$

which is much simpler to compute. In fact, it is easily found to be zero. And this is just one example of why we transform integrals like this.

We are ready to give a problem-solving strategy for change of variables.

Problem-Solving Strategy

Change of Variables

  1. Sketch the region given by the problem in the $xy\text{-plane}$ and then write the equations of the curves that form the boundary.
  2. Depending on the region or the integrand, choose the transformations $x=\kg(u,v)$ and $y=\kh(u,v).$
  3. Determine the new limits of integration in the $uv\text{-plane}\text{.}$
  4. Find the Jacobian $\kJ(u,v).$
  5. In the integrand, replace the variables to obtain the new integrand.
  6. Replace $dy\;dx$ or $dx\;dy,$ whichever occurs, by $\kJ(u,v)du\;dv.$

In the next example, we find a substitution that makes the integrand much simpler to compute.

Example 15.71 · Evaluating an Integral

Using the change of variables $u=x-y$ and $v=x+y,$ evaluate the integral

$$\iint\limits_{\kR}(x-y)e^{x^{2}-y^{2}}dA ,$$

where $\kR$ is the region bounded by the lines $x+y=1$ and $x+y=3$ and the curves $x^{2}-y^{2}=-1$ and $x^{2}-y^{2}=1$ (see the first region in Figure 15.79).

Solution

As before, first find the region $\kR$ and picture the transformation so it becomes easier to obtain the limits of integration after the transformations are made (Figure 15.79).

Given $u=x-y$ and $v=x+y,$ we have $x=\frac{u+v}{2}$ and $y=\frac{v-u}{2}$ and hence the transformation to use is $\kT(u,v)=(\frac{u+v}{2},\frac{v-u}{2}).$ The lines $x+y=1$ and $x+y=3$ become $v=1$ and $v=3,$ respectively. The curves $x^{2}-y^{2}=1$ and $x^{2}-y^{2}=-1$ become $uv=1$ and $uv=-1,$ respectively.

Thus we can describe the region $\kS$ (see the second region Figure 15.79) as

$$\kS=\{(u,v)|1\le v\le 3,\frac{-1}{v}\le u\le \frac{1}{v}\}.$$

The Jacobian for this transformation is

$$\kJ(u,v)=\frac{\partial (x,y)}{\partial (u,v)}=|\begin{array}{lll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v}\end{array}|=|\begin{array}{lll}1/2 & & -1/2 \\ 1/2 & & 1/2\end{array}|=\frac{1}{2}.$$

Therefore, by using the transformation $T,$ the integral changes to

$$\iint\limits_{\kR}(x-y) e^{x^{2}-y^{2}}dA=\frac{1}{2}\int_1^{3} \;\int_{-1/v}^{1/v} ue^{uv}du\;dv .$$

Doing the evaluation, we have

$$\frac{1}{2}\int_1^{3} \;\int_{-1/v}^{1/v} ue^{uv}du\;dv =\frac{2}{3e}\approx 0.245.$$

Change of Variables for Triple Integrals

Changing variables in triple integrals works in exactly the same way. Cylindrical and spherical coordinate substitutions are special cases of this method, which we demonstrate here.

Suppose that $\kG$ is a region in $uvw\text{-space}$ and is mapped to $\kD$ in $xyz\text{-space}$ (Figure 15.80) by a one-to-one $C^{1}$ transformation $\kT(u,v,w)=(x,y,z)$ where $x=\kg(u,v,w),$ $y=\kh(u,v,w),$ and $z=k(u,v,w).$

Then any function $\kF(x,y,z)$ defined on $\kD$ can be thought of as another function $\kH(u,v,w)$ that is defined on $\kG\text{:}$

$$\kF(x,y,z)=\kF(\kg(u,v,w),\kh(u,v,w),k(u,v,w))=\kH(u,v,w).$$

Now we need to define the Jacobian for three variables.

Definition

The Jacobian determinant $\kJ(u,v,w)$ in three variables is defined as follows:

$$\kJ(u,v,w)=|\begin{array}{lllll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial w} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial w} & & \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}\end{array}|.$$

This is also the same as

$$\kJ(u,v,w)=|\begin{array}{lllll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial w} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial w} \\ \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}\end{array}|.$$

The Jacobian can also be simply denoted as $\frac{\partial (x,y,z)}{\partial (u,v,w)}.$

With the transformations and the Jacobian for three variables, we are ready to establish the theorem that describes change of variables for triple integrals.

Theorem

Change of Variables for Triple Integrals

Let $\kT(u,v,w)=(x,y,z)$ where $x=\kg(u,v,w),y=\kh(u,v,w),$ and $z=k(u,v,w),$ be a one-to-one $C^{1}$ transformation, with a nonzero Jacobian, that maps the region $\kG$ in the $uvw\text{-space}$ into the region $\kR$ in the $xyz\text{-space}\text{.}$ As in the two-dimensional case, if $F$ is continuous on $\kR,$ then

$$\begin{array}{rl}\iiint\limits_{\kR}\kF(x,y,z)dV & =\iiint\limits_{\kG}\kF(\kg(u,v,w),\kh(u,v,w),k(u,v,w))|\frac{\partial (x,y,z)}{\partial (u,v,w)}| du\;dv\;dw \\ & =\iiint\limits_{\kG}\kH(u,v,w) |\kJ(u,v,w)|du\;dv\;dw.\end{array}$$

Let us now see how changes in triple integrals for cylindrical and spherical coordinates are affected by this theorem. We expect to obtain the same formulas as in Triple Integrals in Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinates.

Example 15.72 · Obtaining Formulas in Triple Integrals for Cylindrical and Spherical Coordinates

Derive the formula in triple integrals for

a. cylindrical and

b. spherical coordinates.

Solution

a. For cylindrical coordinates, the transformation is $\kT(r,\theta ,z)=(x,y,z)$ from the Cartesian $r\theta z\text{-plane}$ to the Cartesian $xyz\text{-plane}$ (Figure 15.81). Here $x=r\;\cos \theta ,$ $y=r\;\sin \theta ,$ and $z=z.$ The Jacobian for the transformation is

$$\begin{array}{rl}\kJ(r,\theta ,z) & =\frac{\partial (x,y,z)}{\partial (r,\theta ,z)}=|\begin{array}{lllll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial r} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial z} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial r} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial z} \\ \frac{\partial z}{\partial r} & & \frac{\partial z}{\partial \theta} & & \frac{\partial z}{\partial z}\end{array}| \\ & =|\begin{array}{lllll}\cos \theta & & -r\;\sin \theta & & 0 \\ \sin \theta & & r\;\cos \theta & & 0 \\ 0 & & 0 & & 1\end{array}|=r\;{\cos }^{2}\theta +r\;{\sin }^{2}\theta =r({\cos }^{2}\theta +{\sin }^{2}\theta )=r.\end{array}$$

We know that $r\ge 0,$ so $|\kJ(r,\theta ,z)|=r.$ Then the triple integral is

$$\iiint\limits_{\kD}\kf(x,y,z)dV=\iiint\limits_{\kG}\kf(r\;\cos \theta ,r\;\sin \theta ,z) r\;dr\;d\theta \;dz.$$

b. For spherical coordinates, the transformation is $\kT(\rho ,\theta ,\varphi )=(x,y,z)$ from the Cartesian $p\theta \varphi \text{-plane}$ to the Cartesian $xyz\text{-plane}$ (Figure 15.82). Here $x=\rho \;\sin \varphi \;\cos \theta ,$ $y=\rho \;\sin \varphi \;\sin \theta ,$ and $z=\rho \;\cos \varphi .$ The Jacobian for the transformation is

$$\kJ(\rho ,\theta ,\varphi )=\frac{\partial (x,y,z)}{\partial (\rho ,\theta ,\varphi )}=|\begin{array}{lllll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial \rho } & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial \theta} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial \varphi } \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial \rho } & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial \theta} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial \varphi } \\ \frac{\partial z}{\partial \rho } & & \frac{\partial z}{\partial \theta} & & \frac{\partial z}{\partial \varphi }\end{array}|=|\begin{array}{lllll}\sin \varphi \;\cos \theta & & \rho \;\sin \varphi \;\sin \theta & & \rho \;\cos \varphi \;\cos \theta \\ \sin \varphi \;\sin \theta & & \rho \;\sin \varphi \;\cos \theta & & \rho \;\cos \varphi \;\sin \theta \\ \cos \varphi & & 0 & & -\rho \;\sin \varphi \end{array}|.$$

Expanding the determinant with respect to the third row:

$$\begin{array}{r} \\ \\ =\cos \varphi |\begin{array}{lll}-\rho \;\sin \varphi \;\sin \theta & & \rho \;\cos \varphi \;\cos \theta \\ \rho \;\sin \varphi \;\sin \theta & & \rho \;\cos \varphi \;\sin \theta \end{array}|-\rho \;\sin \varphi |\begin{array}{lll}\sin \varphi \;\cos \theta & & -\rho \;\sin \varphi \;\sin \theta \\ \sin \varphi \;\sin \theta & & \rho \;\sin \varphi \;\cos \theta \end{array}| \\ =\cos \varphi (-{\rho }^{2}\sin \varphi \;\cos \varphi \;{\sin }^{2}\theta -{\rho }^{2}\sin \varphi \;\cos \varphi \;{\cos }^{2}\theta ) \\ \;-\rho \;\sin \varphi (\rho \;{\sin }^{2}\varphi \;{\cos }^{2}\theta +\rho \;{\sin }^{2}\varphi \;{\sin }^{2}\theta ) \\ =-{\rho }^{2}\sin \varphi \;{\cos }^{2}\varphi ({\sin }^{2}\theta +{\cos }^{2}\theta )-{\rho }^{2}\sin \varphi \;{\sin }^{2}\varphi ({\sin }^{2}\theta +{\cos }^{2}\theta ) \\ =-{\rho }^{2}\sin \varphi \;{\cos }^{2}\varphi -{\rho }^{2}\sin \varphi \;{\sin }^{2}\varphi \\ =-{\rho }^{2}\sin \varphi ({\cos }^{2}\varphi +{\sin }^{2}\varphi )=-{\rho }^{2}\sin \varphi .\end{array}$$

Since $0\le \varphi \le \pi ,$ we must have $\sin \varphi \ge 0.$ Thus $|\kJ(\rho ,\theta ,\varphi )|=|-{\rho }^{2}\sin \varphi |={\rho }^{2}\sin \varphi .$

Then the triple integral becomes

$$\iiint\limits_{\kD}\kf(x,y,z) dV=\iiint\limits_{\kG}\kf(\rho \;\sin \varphi \;\cos \theta ,\rho \;\sin \varphi \;\sin \theta ,\rho \;\cos \varphi ) {\rho }^{2}\sin \varphi \;d\rho \;d\varphi \;d\theta .$$

Let’s try another example with a different substitution.

Example 15.73 · Evaluating a Triple Integral with a Change of Variables

Evaluate the triple integral

$$\int_0^{3} \;\int_0^{4} \;\int_{y/2}^{(y/2)+1} (x+\frac{z}{3}) dx\;dy\;dz$$

in $xyz\text{-space}$ by using the transformation

$$u=(2x-y)/2,v=y/2,\text{and}\;w=z/3.$$

Then integrate over an appropriate region in $uvw\text{-space}\text{.}$

Solution

As before, some kind of sketch of the region $\kG$ in $xyz\text{-space}$ over which we have to perform the integration can help identify the region $\kD$ in $uvw\text{-space}$ (Figure 15.83). Clearly $\kG$ in $xyz\text{-space}$ is bounded by the planes $x=y/2,x=(y/2)+1,y=0,$ $y=4,$ $z=0,\text{and}\;z=4.$ We also know that we have to use $u=(2x-y)/2,v=y/2,\text{and}\;w=z/3$ for the transformations. We need to solve for $x,y,\text{and}\;z.$ Here we find that $x=u+v,$ $y=2v,$ and $z=3w.$

Using elementary algebra, we can find the corresponding surfaces for the region $\kG$ and the limits of integration in $uvw\text{-space}\text{.}$ It is convenient to list these equations in a table.

Equations in $xyz$ for the region $\kD$Corresponding equations in $uvw$ for the region $\kG$Limits for the integration in $uvw$
$x=y/2$$u+v=2v/2=v$$u=0$
$x=y/2$$u+v=(2v/2)+1=v+1$$u=1$
$y=0$$2v=0$$v=0$
$y=4$$2v=4$$v=2$
$z=0$$3w=0$$w=0$
$z=3$$3w=3$$w=1$

Now we can calculate the Jacobian for the transformation:

$$\kJ(u,v,w)=|\begin{array}{lllll}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial x}{\partial w} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial y}{\partial w} \\ \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} & & \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} & & \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}\end{array}|=|\begin{array}{lllll}1 & & 1 & & 0 \\ 0 & & 2 & & 0 \\ 0 & & 0 & & 3\end{array}|=6.$$

The function to be integrated becomes

$$\kf(x,y,z)=x+\frac{z}{3}=u+v+\frac{3w}{3}=u+v+w.$$

We are now ready to put everything together and complete the problem.

$$\begin{array}{l} \\ \\ \\ \\ \;\int_0^{3} \;\int_0^{4} \;\int_{y/2}^{(y/2)+1} (x+\frac{z}{3}) dx\;dy\;dz \\ \\ =\int_0^{1} \;\int_0^{2} \;\int_0^{1} (u+v+w) |\kJ(u,v,w)|du\;dv\;dw=\int_0^{1} \;\int_0^{2} \;\int_0^{1} (u+v+w) |6|du\;dv\;dw \\ =6\int_0^{1} \;\int_0^{2} \;\int_0^{1} (u+v+w) du\;dv\;dw=6{\int_0^{1} \;\int_0^{2} [\frac{u^{2}}{2}+vu+wu] }_0^{1}dv\;dw \\ =6\int_0^{1} \;\int_0^{2} (\frac{1}{2}+v+w) dv\;dw=6{\int_0^{1} [\frac{1}{2}v+\frac{v^{2}}{2}+wv] }_0^{2}dw \\ =6\int_0^{1} (3+2w) dw=6{[3w+w^{2}]}_0^{1}=24.\end{array}$$
