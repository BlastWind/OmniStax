## What is new

The chapter is the second half of a symmetry. Chapter 22 showed that a current
makes a magnetic field; this chapter shows that a changing magnetic field makes
a current, and almost everything in it follows from one equation and one sign.

1. **Flux, and the fact that only change induces.** $\Phi = BA\cos\theta$ is the
   amount of field passing through an area, and a magnet held still inside a
   coil induces nothing at all. Three things can change the flux — the field,
   the area, the angle — and the chapter's twelve sections are, at bottom,
   twelve ways of changing one of them.
2. **Faraday's law, and Lenz's minus sign.** $\text{emf} = -N\Delta\Phi/\Delta t$.
   The minus sign is conservation of energy: the induced current makes a field
   that opposes the change that made it, and a reader who takes that seriously
   can work out every direction in the chapter without memorising anything
   further.
3. **Inductance, the henry, and the two circuits it makes.** $L = N\Delta\Phi/
   \Delta I$ measures how effectively a device turns its own changing current
   into flux; $\text{emf} = -L\Delta I/\Delta t$ is what it does with it, and
   $E_\text{ind} = \frac{1}{2}LI^2$ is the energy it holds. With a resistor it
   gives an RL circuit and the time constant $\tau = L/R$; with an AC source it
   gives a reactance.
4. **Reactance, impedance and resonance.** $X_L = 2\pi fL$ and $X_C = 1/2\pi fC$
   are oppositions to current measured in ohms, and they behave oppositely with
   frequency, so in series they subtract: $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}$. Where
   they cancel the circuit resonates, at $f_0 = 1/2\pi\sqrt{LC}$, which is
   Chapter 16's resonance in a new dress and is how a radio is tuned.

The chapter leans on Chapter 22 for the magnetic field, the force on a moving
charge and the Hall emf; on Chapters 20 and 21 for current, resistance, Ohm's
law, power, rms values, emf and the RC time constant; on Chapter 19 for
capacitance; on Chapter 16 for resonance, natural frequency and damping, which
23.12 names outright; and on Chapters 6 and 10 for angular velocity, since a
generator's coil turns.

## The type cases this chapter had to settle

Root rule 7 asks whether each new quantity is a type of its own, a variant of an
existing type, or untyped ink. Four cases needed an argument, and they are
recorded here so that no section decides them twice.

- **Magnetic flux is a type of its own.** $\Phi = BA\cos\theta$ is not a magnetic
  field: it is a field times an area, measured in $\text{T}\cdot\text{m}^2$, which
  the SI names the weber. It is also the quantity the whole chapter is about —
  every figure of 23.1, 23.2, 23.3 and 23.5 draws it, Faraday's law states its
  rate of change, and the reader's central difficulty is telling $\Phi$ from $B$
  when a magnet sits still inside a coil. Giving it the field's hue would make
  that difficulty worse. It is declared `magnetic-flux`.
- **Inductance is a type of its own.** The henry is $\Omega\cdot\text{s}$ and
  nothing the book has declared has that dimension. $L$ and $M$ are the
  chapter's two new devices' worth of physics, they carry sliders in 23.9, 23.10,
  23.11 and 23.12, and $\tau = L/R$, $X_L = 2\pi fL$ and $f_0 = 1/2\pi\sqrt{LC}$
  all read better when $L$ keeps one hue across four sections. It is declared
  `inductance`, and mutual inductance $M$ takes the same type, since $M$ and $L$
  are the same physical quantity measured between two devices and within one.
- **Reactance and impedance take the `resistance` type, not new ones of their
  own.** The book's own words settle this: inductive reactance is "an effective
  resistance of the inductor to AC", capacitive reactance is the same for a
  capacitor, and impedance is "the AC analogue to resistance in a DC circuit".
  All three are measured in ohms, all three stand in the same place in Ohm's law
  ($I = V/X_L$, $I = V/X_C$, $I_\text{rms} = V_\text{rms}/Z$), and 23.12's whole
  argument is that they combine with $R$ into one number:
  $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}$ and $\cos\phi = R/Z$. Root rule 7 keeps a
  variant of one type in its hue and tells the variants apart by decoration, and
  $X_L$, $X_C$ and $Z$ are told apart by their subscripts and their names. Three
  hues here would break the one equation the section exists to state, and would
  say that an ohm of reactance is a different kind of thing from an ohm of
  resistance, which is exactly what the reader must not conclude. This is not
  the coercion root rule 7 forbids: nothing is being squeezed into a neighbouring
  type to save a colour, since reactance is the same quantity as resistance in
  dimension and in role.
- **The phase angle $\phi$ and the number of turns $N$ stay untyped and in ink,**
  as every angle and every count in this book does, and so do the area $A$, the
  length $\ell$, the permeability $\mu_0$ and the power factor $\cos\phi$, which
  is a dimensionless ratio. The back emf, the peak emf and the primary and
  secondary voltages of a transformer are all voltages and take Chapter 19's
  hue; the energy stored in an inductor is an energy and takes Chapter 7's.

With `magnetic-flux` and `inductance` this chapter adds two types to the
book's declaration, which the colour scheme deals its hues along in order.

## Answer to root rule 23: BE INSPIRING

The reader arrives at this chapter having been told, for two chapters, that
fields are things that sit there. Chapter 18 drew an electric field round a
charge, Chapter 22 drew a magnetic field round a wire, and both were pictures of
a state. This chapter's one idea is that a field which changes does something a
field which sits still does not, and the first figure of 23.1 makes the point by
showing nothing happening: the switch is closed, the galvanometer swings, and
then, while the current in the top coil runs steadily on, the needle sits at
zero. A reader who never feels that will spend twelve sections hunting for the
$B$ in a problem that is asking about $\Delta\Phi/\Delta t$.

So the chapter's first opportunity is a figure that refuses to induce. One coil,
one magnet, one needle, and a hand that moves the magnet: the emf follows the
speed, reverses with the direction, reverses again with the pole, and falls to
nothing the moment the hand stops, with the flux drawn as a count of field lines
through the loop and the readout writing $-N\Delta\Phi/\Delta t$ with live
numbers. That is the book's Figure 23.4, which prints five stills of exactly
this and cannot show the one thing that matters, which is that the needle
depends on how fast the hand moves.

The chapter's second opportunity, and its greatest, is the generator. A
rectangular loop turning in a field between two poles, the sinusoid drawn
beneath it as the loop turns, and the angle $\theta = \omega t$ shared between
the scene and the graph. This single scene contains more of the book than any
other figure in it: it is the origin of the alternating current of Chapter 20,
the explanation of why household voltage is a sine wave, the motor of Chapter 22
run backwards, the back emf of 23.6, the changing flux a transformer needs in
23.7, and the wind turbine on the chapter's own opening photograph. Turn the
brushes into a split ring and the sinusoid rectifies, which is Figure 23.22 for
free. Slow the rotation and the peak emf falls, which is $\text{emf}_0 = NAB
\omega$ made visible. If one figure of this chapter is built well, it should be
this one, and it should move, because its idea has a clock in it.

The third opportunity is the one the book itself signposts and then cannot
deliver: the phase. 23.11 spends three pages saying that voltage leads current
in an inductor, follows it in a capacitor and is in phase with it in a resistor,
and 23.12 draws five stacked graphs to say that the three do not add. A reader
who can drag the frequency and watch the inductor's curve slide ahead of the
capacitor's, and watch their sum shrink as they come into opposition, has the
whole of 23.12 before the algebra starts. The impedance triangle, $R$ along one
side and $X_L - X_C$ up the other, is the same fact drawn as a shape, and the
resonance curve of Figure 23.48 is what happens to it when the vertical side
goes to zero. Three views of one idea, on one page, with one frequency slider
driving all three.

Beyond those, the chapter's stories are already visual and want only to be made
live. The eddy-current pendulum is an experiment the reader can run: a solid
bob, a slotted bob and a plastic bob, released from the same angle, damping at
three different rates, with the induced loops drawn in the plate as it crosses
the pole faces and nothing drawn at all while the plate is wholly inside the
field, which is the part of the argument print keeps having to assert. The RL
circuit is Chapter 21's RC circuit with a new time constant and should be drawn
the same way, so that the reader sees the family. And the LC circuit beside a
mass on a spring, which the book prints as four stills in Figure 23.50, is the
most quantitative analogy in the book and is wasted flat: energy sloshing from
the capacitor's field to the inductor's field while the mass swings, on one
clock, says in ten seconds what a paragraph says badly.

One thing the colour plan can do for this chapter that no figure can. The
chapter writes $\Phi$, $B$, $L$, $M$, $X_L$, $X_C$, $Z$, $R$, $I$, $V$ and
$\mathcal{E}$ within a dozen pages of each other, and a reader who cannot tell
which of those is a field, which is a flux and which is an opposition to current
is lost. If $\Phi$ wears one hue on every page from 23.1 to 23.9, and $R$, $X_L$,
$X_C$ and $Z$ all wear the resistance hue, then $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}$
reads as one sentence about ohms and $\text{emf} = -N\Delta\Phi/\Delta t$ reads
as one sentence about flux, and the chapter's hardest page becomes its
clearest.

## Depth: which figures argue a locked view, and which a full 3D scene

Root rule 28 asks each figure to be placed in one of three tiers. This chapter
is mostly flat, because half of it is circuits and graphs, but its two founding
scenes are arrangements in space and the book draws both in perspective.

**Full 3D scenes (root rule 28.3), two of them, each argued again in its plan.**

- **23.1, the flux through a tilted area (Figure 23.6, folding 23.4).** The
  definition $\Phi = BA\cos\theta$ *is* an angle between a field and a surface's
  normal, and flat drawing must either fix the tilt or lie about it. The reader
  turns the loop, watches the field lines that thread it thin out as it turns
  edge-on, and reads $\Phi$ fall to zero while $B$ and $A$ are untouched — which
  is the answer to the section's own conceptual question, how flux can be zero
  when the field is not. Orbit bounded to the hemisphere in front of the field
  so the loop is never seen from behind its own normal, with a snap-to-view
  button for the book's own viewpoint and one for looking straight down the
  field.
- **23.5, the generator (Figures 23.19, 23.20, 23.21 and 23.22).** A loop turning
  on a shaft between two poles, with the emf drawn against time beneath it. This
  is the chapter's one moving 3D scene: it registers a cycle and takes the app's
  transport, the rings-or-split-ring is a choice, and the book's four figures are
  four states of it. A flat drawing has to pick a moment, which is why the book
  needs four. Orbit bounded above the shaft's equator and within a right angle of
  the book's viewpoint, so the handedness of the induced current is never
  mirrored.

**Locked views (root rule 28.2), where the book prints perspective but nothing
turns.** 23.1's Faraday ring (23.3), which is two windings on an iron torus;
23.7's transformer core with its primary and secondary (23.27); 23.9's two
neighbouring coils (23.37) and its counter-wound dryer element (23.38); and
23.4's pendulum bob between two pole faces (23.12), where the bob swings in a
plane but the pole faces stand either side of it and the book draws them so.

**Flat (root rule 28.1), which is most of the chapter.** Every circuit schematic
(23.24, 23.29 to 23.36, 23.40, 23.42, 23.43 to 23.46); every graph (23.21's
sinusoid, 23.22's rectified pulses, 23.28's spikes, 23.42's growth and decay
curves, 23.43 to 23.45's phase pairs, 23.47's stack and 23.48's resonance
curves); the rod on its rails (23.10), which is a plane figure with the field
into the page and is the clearest case in the book of the dot-and-cross
convention doing its job; the eddy-current plate and its slots (23.13, 23.14);
the balance (23.15); the power distribution line (23.26); and the LC circuit
beside its mass on a spring (23.50), which is two plane systems drawn side by
side and gains nothing from depth.
