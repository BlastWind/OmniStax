## What is new

The chapter introduces one kind of physical quantity, the magnetic field $B$,
measured in teslas, and with it a force law unlike any the book has written so
far. Everything else in the chapter is built from three sentences.

1. **A magnetic field exerts a force on a moving charge, sideways.** $F = qvB
   \sin\theta$, with the direction perpendicular to the plane of $v$ and $B$.
   Every force before this chapter has pointed along a line the reader could
   draw on the page: gravity toward the centre, a spring back to equilibrium,
   the Coulomb force along the line between charges. This one points out of the
   page, and its magnitude vanishes when the charge moves along the field.
2. **A current is a stream of moving charges, so a wire feels the same force.**
   $F = IlB \sin\theta$, and a loop of wire in a field feels a torque, $\tau =
   NIAB \sin\theta$, which is the motor and the meter.
3. **A current makes a field.** $B = \mu_0 I / 2\pi r$ round a straight wire,
   $B = \mu_0 I / 2R$ at the centre of a loop, $B = \mu_0 n I$ inside a
   solenoid, and, with the first sentence, a force between two wires that
   defines the ampere.

Three rules of the hand carry the geometry: RHR-1 for the force on a charge or
a current (22.4, 22.7), RHR-2 for the field a current makes (22.9), and the
dot-and-cross notation for a vector out of or into the page (22.3). The chapter
also supplies the first two quantitative bridges between electricity and
magnetism the book has drawn: the Hall emf $\varepsilon = Blv$ (22.6), which is
a voltage made by a magnetic force, and the velocity selector $v = E/B$
(22.11), where the two fields cancel.

The chapter leans on Chapters 20 and 21 for current, resistance and emf, on
Chapter 18 for charge, on Chapter 19 for the electric field and the volt, on
Chapter 6 for centripetal force and circular motion, and on Chapter 9 for
torque.

## Answer to root rule 23: BE INSPIRING

The difficulty of this chapter is not arithmetic. It is that magnetism is the
first thing in the book that will not lie flat. Every force the reader has met
so far points along a line that can be drawn on the page — toward the centre,
back to equilibrium, along the line joining two charges — and the magnetic
force points out of the page, at right angles to both of the things that cause
it. The book knows this, which is why it draws a hand, a dot and a cross, and
why so many of its figures are printed in perspective. A reader who never
manages to see the third axis spends the chapter memorising three hand rules
and ends it believing that magnetism is a set of conventions rather than a
mechanism.

So the chapter's one great opportunity is depth the reader can turn. Three
scenes carry the whole subject and each of them is an arrangement in space:

- **The vector trio.** A velocity, a field and the force between them, drawn as
  three arrows the reader may orbit, with the angle $\theta$ on a slider and
  the force shrinking to nothing as the velocity swings onto the field. Seen
  from the book's own viewpoint it is the book's Figure 22.16; turned a quarter
  turn it becomes obvious that the force is perpendicular to a plane, not to a
  line, and that the reader has been looking at a projection all along.
- **The field a current makes.** A straight wire's circles, a loop's dipole and
  a solenoid's uniform interior are the same picture at three densities of
  winding, and they are one scene the reader can orbit and wind. Sliding a
  straight wire into a loop and a loop into a solenoid, with the field lines
  following, would tell the reader in twenty seconds what the section spends
  four pages on, and it would make the sentence "electromagnets and ferromagnets
  have the same basic characteristics" something seen rather than asserted.
- **The motor.** A loop on a shaft, the forces on its two vertical sides, the
  torque that turns it, the reversal at $\theta = 0$ and the brushes that
  prevent it. This one has a clock in it and should move: the reader sets the
  current and the field, watches the loop turn, switches the brushes off and
  watches it stall and rock about equilibrium instead. The book's Figure 22.34
  is four top views because print cannot animate; one turning loop replaces all
  four.

Beyond the three, the chapter's stories are already visual and want only to be
made live. A charge in a uniform field circles, and the radius $r = mv/qB$ is a
slider away from being obvious — set the mass and watch two isotopes separate,
which is the mass spectrometer and needs no second figure. Tip the velocity out
of the plane and the circle becomes a helix, and the helix walking up a
converging field is the magnetic mirror, the Van Allen belts and the aurora on
the introduction's own photograph: the chapter opens and closes on the same
picture, and one figure can carry both. Two parallel wires drawn with their
circular fields, with a switch that reverses one current, turn the definition
of the ampere from a sentence to be memorised into a thing the reader does.
And the Hall effect is a race that reaches a standstill: carriers pushed to one
side until the voltage they build pushes back exactly as hard, which is worth
watching settle.

The colour plan carries one further idea. Every equation in this chapter is a
product of a field, a current or a charge, a length or a speed, and a sine, and
if $B$ wears one hue on every page, then $F = qvB\sin\theta$, $F = IlB\sin\theta$,
$\tau = NIAB\sin\theta$ and $\varepsilon = Blv$ read as four sentences about the
same quantity rather than four formulas to be filed separately.

## Depth: which figures argue a locked view, and which a full 3D scene

Root rule 28 asks each figure to be placed in one of three tiers, and this
chapter has more members above the flat default than any chapter of the book so
far, because the book itself draws so much of it in perspective. The
recommendations, which each section's plan must argue again:

**Full 3D scenes (root rule 28.3), three of them.**

- **22.4, the vector trio (Figure 22.16, folding 22.17).** The lesson *is* that
  $F$ is perpendicular to the plane of $v$ and $B$, and a flat drawing must
  either lie about the third axis or hide it behind a dot. Orbit bounded to the
  hemisphere above the $v$–$B$ plane so the handedness is never mirrored, with
  snap-to-view buttons for the book's own viewpoint and for looking straight
  down the field. Auto-rotate off by default; the reader turns it.
- **22.9, the field a current makes (Figures 22.37, 22.38 and 22.39).** A
  straight wire's circular loops, a flat loop's dipole and a solenoid's uniform
  interior are three states of one arrangement in space, and the fold is the
  point: a choice of wire, loop or solenoid, a slider for the number of turns,
  and field lines drawn in three dimensions. Flat, the solenoid's interior
  field is a row of parallel arrows that says nothing about why the outside is
  nearly zero. Orbit bounded to above the horizon and to within a right angle
  of the axis, so the coil is never seen edge-on from inside.
- **22.8, the motor (Figures 22.33, 22.34 and 22.35).** The loop rotates out of
  the page, and the sign reversal of the torque at $\theta = 0$ is a fact about
  a plane turning through a field. This is the chapter's one moving 3D scene:
  it registers a cycle and takes the app's transport, with the brushes as a
  choice and the four top views of 22.34 available as snap-to-view buttons, so
  the book's figure is one state of it. Orbit bounded above the shaft's
  equator; the meter of 22.36 stays flat beside it.

**Locked views (root rule 28.2), where the book prints perspective but nothing
turns.** 22.6's Hall slab (Figures 22.26 and 22.27), which is a box with a
field through it and charges on two faces; 22.7's wire between the poles
(22.29) and the MHD duct (22.31); 22.10's two parallel wires with one field
circle drawn round each (22.40), which the book itself gives twice, once in
perspective and once from above, and which is therefore a locked view with a
button for the top view rather than an orbit; 22.2's electromagnet on its core
(22.11); and 22.25's toroid.

**Flat (root rule 28.1), which is most of the chapter.** Every graph; the
domains of 22.8; the split magnet of 22.6; the bar magnet and its compasses in
22.14, which the book draws in the plane and which is a plane figure; the
circular motion of 22.19 and the radius slider that carries the mass
spectrometer; the field-line maps of 22.15; the Van Allen belts and the
cosmic-ray paths of 22.22 and 22.23, which are a cross-section through the
Earth and are clearer as one; the CRT of 22.42; and the velocity selector of
22.41. The spiral of 22.20 and the magnetic mirror of 22.21 are a borderline
pair: a helix is an arrangement in space, but the book draws it in the plane
of the field lines and the lesson is the change in the parallel component,
which reads honestly flat. The 22.5 agent may argue a locked view for it.
