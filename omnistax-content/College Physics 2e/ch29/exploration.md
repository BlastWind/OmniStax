## Why this chapter

This is where the book leaves classical physics. Chapters 24 to 27 made light a
wave and gave it rays, interference and diffraction; this chapter takes the same
light, shines it on a metal plate, and finds that it arrives in lumps. It then
turns the argument round: if a wave can be a particle, a particle can be a wave,
and an electron fired at a crystal diffracts. The chapter ends on the price of
that symmetry, the uncertainty principle. Every idea in it is one relation with
Planck's constant in it ($E = hf$, $p = h/\lambda$, $\lambda = h/p$,
$\Delta x\Delta p \ge h/4\pi$), and the correspondence principle says in each
case why the reader has never noticed: $h$ is very small. Atomic physics
(Chapter 30) and nuclear physics (Chapters 31 and 32) are built on it.

## What is new, and the types the chapter asks for

**No new type.** Everything the chapter measures is already declared: photon
energy, binding energy, kinetic energy and an energy step are `energy`; the
frequency of the radiation and the threshold frequency are `frequency`; a
wavelength, a slit or plane spacing and the uncertainty in position are
`position`; photon momentum and its uncertainty are `momentum`; a lifetime is
`time`; the accelerating voltage of an x-ray tube is `voltage`; a source's
output in watts is `power`; the blackbody curves of 29.3 are `intensity`
against `position` at a `temperature`. Planck's constant is a constant like the
book's material constants and stays untyped and in ink; so do a count of
photons, the integer $n$, the order $n$ of Bragg's equation, a mass and an angle.

**Four symbol rows staged.** `h_planck` ($h$, untyped), because the typed `h` is
a height (position, `\kh`) and must never write Planck's constant;
`KE_e` ($\text{KE}_e$, energy, `\kKEe`); `E_prime` ($E'$, energy, `\kEprime`)
for the scattered photon of Figure 29.15; `f_max` ($f_{\text{max}}$, frequency,
`\kfmax`) for the end of the x-ray spectrum. `ΔE` (`\kdE`) and `BE` (`\kBE`)
were merged a moment earlier by Chapters 30 and 31 with the same LaTeX and type
and are reused. Everything else is reused as it stands: `E` `\kE`, `f` `\kf`,
`f_0` `\kfo`, `λ` `\klam`, `c` `\kc`, `p` `\kp`, `Δx` `\kdx`, `Δp` `\kdp`,
`Δt` `\kdt`, `Δv` `\kdv`, `v` `\kv`, `d` `\kd`, `q` `\kq`, `V_volt` `\kV`,
untyped `n`, `m`, `θ`. The Lorentz factor $\gamma$ of 29.4 and 29.8 is Chapter
28's and never `\kgamma`, which is a surface tension.

## BE INSPIRING (rule 23)

This is the chapter where the reader should feel the floor move, and three
figures can do it.

**The lamp that cannot do it.** The photoelectric effect is an argument, and the
reader should win it with their own hands. A lamp over a plate of calcium: turn
the intensity up on red light and nothing happens, however bright, photons
raining down in their true red and bouncing off; nudge the frequency past the
threshold and electrons leave at once, one per photon, the brightness now
changing how many and never how fast. The graph of $\text{KE}_e$ against $f$
sits beside it with the live point walking its line, and the slope is $h$. A
reader who has tried to force red light to work will not need to be told what a
photon is.

**A pattern that builds itself.** 29.7's opening figure should be a screen that
fills one electron at a time: each dot lands somewhere nobody could predict, and
after a few hundred the fringes of a wave are unmistakable. A choice between not
watching and watching which slit it went through turns the double-slit pattern
into a single-slit one in front of the reader. It is the most famous experiment
in quantum mechanics, and it is also a simple animation.

**The size of $h$.** The correspondence principle appears in every section, and
a shared idea can make it visible: in each figure where it applies, a mass or
wavelength slider that runs from an electron to a bowling ball, with the
wavelength or the uncertainty read out alongside the size of the thing, shows
the quantum world shrink out of sight as the object grows. Planck's constant is
the only reason the reader's world looks classical, and they should be able to
turn it into view.
