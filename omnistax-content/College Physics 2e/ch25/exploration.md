# Exploration: College Physics 2e, Chapter 25 Geometric Optics

Read by the prep agent on 2026-09-15, before the chapter's seven section pages
were built in parallel. The book's own rules are in the folder above; this file
records what the chapter's eight modules hold, what is new in them, and how the
chapter should look when it is built.

## Why this chapter

This is where the book opens optics. Chapter 24 established that light is an
electromagnetic wave and gave its speed; this chapter puts the wave aside, says
that light travels in straight lines called rays whenever it meets anything
larger than a micron, and then builds the whole of everyday optics out of two
laws and some trigonometry. Everything the reader owns by the end — a mirror, a
lens, a fiber, a rainbow, a pair of spectacles, a camera and an eye — comes from
the law of reflection and the law of refraction. The chapter is also the book's
first sustained treatment of the image, a thing that is somewhere without being
anywhere, and the sign conventions it teaches are used unchanged by the vision,
instruments and wave optics chapters that follow.

## What is new, and the types the chapter asks for

**No new type, and the least colour in the book since Chapter 1.** Geometric
optics is geometry. A ray diagram is made of straight lines, perpendiculars,
angles and surfaces, and this book keeps every angle, every count and every
dimensionless ratio untyped and in ink. That puts the index of refraction $n$ (a
ratio of two speeds), the angles $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$, $\theta_{\text{i}}$,
$\theta_{\text{r}}$ and $\theta_{\text{c}}$, and the magnification $m$ (a ratio
of two heights) in ink, and it leaves 25.1, 25.2 and most of 25.4 as pages whose
figures are wholly in ink, which is the second half of root rule 7's test and is
correct rather than unfinished. What the chapter does colour are the quantities
that carry a dimension and that its figures actually draw: the speed of light and
the speed of light in a material wear Chapter 2's `velocity`, and the focal
length, the object and image distances, the object and image heights, the radius
of curvature of a mirror and the wavelength wear Chapter 2's `position`. 25.3's
one moving figure binds `time`. `ch25/COLOR.md` sets this out page by page.

**The power of a lens is untyped, and this is a decision.** The book's $P$ here
is one over a focal length, measured in diopters, and the book itself warns that
it is not the power in watts of Chapter 7. It could have been given a type of its
own, and it is not, for three reasons: no figure of the chapter draws a diopter,
the quantity restates a focal length the same figure already colours, and a page
that binds `position` for $f$ and a second hue for $1/f$ would be saying that the
two are different physics when the whole point of $P = 1/f$ is that they are the
same fact read two ways. It takes the key `P_lens` with the LaTeX $P$ and no type,
so that Chapter 7's typed $P$ is untouched and nothing is coerced.

**Ten symbol rows, six of them suffixed because a typed key is already taken.**
`f_focal` ($f$, a position) because Chapter 16 owns $f$ as the frequency;
`R_curv` ($R$, a position) because Chapter 3 owns $R$ as a resultant displacement
and Chapter 6's `r_curv` is written $r$, not $R$; `h_img` and `h_obj` because
Chapter 7 owns `h_i` as an initial height; `d_obj` and `d_img`, which are free
keys but are spelled to match the heights; and `P_lens`, untyped, because
Chapter 7 owns the typed $P$. The four remaining rows are the untyped angles
`θ_i`, `θ_r` and `θ_c`, which the book writes and no chapter owns, and nothing
else.

**Why `n`, `n_1`, `n_2` and `m` are reused rather than suffixed.** An untyped
symbol row in this book carries nothing but its LaTeX: no macro, no hue, and so
no meaning that a second chapter could contradict. The meaning lives in the
chapter's own `variables` row, which is keyed by section and symbol. The book
already shares the untyped `n` between the moles of 13.3, the turns per unit
length of 22.9 and, in 24.2, the index of refraction itself, and it shares the
untyped `m` between the masses of 12.2 and 13.4; adding an `n_refr` whose LaTeX
is also $n$ would put two identical rows in the table and would make 24.2 and
25.3 write the same symbol two ways. So this chapter reuses `n`, `n_1`, `n_2`,
`m`, `θ`, `θ_1` and `θ_2` as they stand, and reuses the typed `c`, `v` and `λ`
as they stand too, and suffixes only where the existing row is typed and would
carry the wrong macro or the wrong hue. Chapter 12's `n_1` and `n_2` are branch
counts and Chapter 25's are indices of refraction; both are pure numbers in ink,
and the variables table says which is which.

**Everything else the chapter writes is already declared.** The speed of light
$c$ and the speed $v$ in a medium are Chapter 2's `velocity`, as 24.1 and 24.2
made them; the wavelength $\lambda$ is a `position`, as it has been since 16.9;
the round-trip time and the period of Michelson's mirror are `time`; the
intensity of the reflected infrared in 25.7's last problem is Chapter 17's
`intensity`, and the mass, specific heat and density of its solar collector
example are Chapter 14's and Chapter 11's, all untyped or already typed there.

## Which figures argue a locked view or a full 3D scene

Almost every figure of this chapter is planar, because almost every idea in it is
a relation between quantities laid out in one plane and is clearest drawn flat
with a fixed frame (root rule 28.1). A ray diagram is the flattest thing in the
book: the incident ray, the normal and the refracted ray define a plane, and the
lesson is the angles inside it. Three drawings the book prints in perspective are
locked views (root rule 28.2): the bundle of fibers carrying an image (25.15(a)),
the single fiber inside its cladding (25.16) and the pair of prisms inside
binoculars (25.18). Each is a solid seen from one viewpoint, and a locked view
gives the shading without paying for an orbit.

One figure argues a full 3D scene, and it is the best candidate in the book since
Chapter 6's Cavendish balance: **the arc of the rainbow, Figure 25.24(b)**. The
lesson there is not what happens inside one drop, which 25.23 draws perfectly
flat; it is why the bow is an arc, and the answer is a statement about space. The
sun's rays arrive parallel, every drop that sends red light to the eye lies on a
cone of one fixed half-angle about the line running from the sun through the
observer's head, and the bow the observer sees is where that cone meets the
curtain of rain. A flat drawing has to lie about this: it can show one drop at
the correct angle, or it can show an arc, but it cannot show that they are the
same fact. In 3D the reader turns the scene, watches the cone stay put while the
bow swings, sees the observer's shadow at its centre, and discovers that the bow
moves with the observer and that nobody sees the same rainbow twice. The orbit is
bounded to the sky above the observer's horizon, so the scene is never seen from
beneath the ground, and it carries the buttons root rule 26.2 asks for. 25.5's
plan makes the final call and states the bound; the prism's dispersion, the
diamond's facets and every lens and mirror of the chapter stay flat, since the
book draws them flat and a turn would add nothing.

## Motion

Optics has no clock in it. A ray diagram is a set of paths and not a history, so
the mental-translation test of root rule 24.3 is answered by sliders rather than
by animation nearly everywhere in this chapter, and a still figure that answers
its sliders is the right tier. One figure is different: **Michelson's rotating
mirror, Figure 25.11**, where the measurement *is* a time. The reader watches
light leave the eight-sided mirror, travel 35 km, come back, and arrive either
onto a face that has turned into place or onto one that has not, and the rotation
rate that makes the two coincide gives the speed of light. That figure registers a
cycle and gets the app's transport. 25.4's fiber may carry a pulse running down
its length if its plan argues that the piping of a signal, and not the geometry of
one bounce, is the idea; nothing else in the chapter moves, and no figure gets a
dummy loop to earn a transport.

## BE INSPIRING (rule 23)

Optics is the one part of a physics course where the reader can check the book
against the world without any apparatus at all, and the figures of this chapter
should keep sending them to do it. Three things should carry the chapter.

**One boundary, dragged.** The single most valuable object in the chapter is a
live boundary between two media: a ray arriving at an angle the reader sets, the
two materials chosen from Table 25.1 by name, the refracted ray bending toward or
away from the normal, and the reflected ray always there beside it. That one
figure, with nothing changed but its numbers, is 25.3's Snell's law, 25.4's
critical angle — push the incident angle up and watch the refracted ray swing
flat against the surface and then vanish — and, with the wavelength on a third
slider and the ray drawn in its own colour, 25.5's dispersion. The reader should
be able to discover the critical angle by dragging rather than by being told it
exists, and should see for themselves that it is only there when the light starts
in the slower medium. That is the chapter's single best gesture and it should be
built once, well, and reused.

**A rainbow the reader can stand inside.** The rainbow is the most beautiful thing
in this book and the chapter explains it completely. It deserves the whole
argument in one page: a drop with a ray through it, the colours leaving at
slightly different angles because the index rises as the wavelength falls, and
then the step nothing flat can make, the cone of directions that turns those
angles into an arc in the sky. Give the reader the sun behind them, the rain in
front, the shadow of their own head at the centre of the bow, and a handle to turn
the scene, and they will understand in ten seconds a thing most people carry a
wrong explanation of for life. The secondary bow, with its reversed colours, is
one more reflection inside the drop and should be a switch, not a second figure.

**An optical bench.** 25.6 and 25.7 are the same physics twice, and they should be
the same figure twice. One bench: an object with a height, a lens or a mirror whose
focal length and sign the reader sets, the three traceable rays drawn as they are
traced, the image appearing where they cross, and the thin lens equation written
underneath with the live numbers. Slide the object in from far away and watch the
image race out to infinity as the object passes the focal point and then flip to
the other side, upright and virtual: cases 1, 2 and 3 are not three facts to
memorise but one continuous motion of a single handle, and a reader who has pushed
that slider once will never again wonder why a magnifier has to be held close. Put
a screen on the bench that catches a real image and catches nothing at all from a
virtual one, and the distinction the whole chapter turns on becomes something the
reader has watched rather than something they have been told.
