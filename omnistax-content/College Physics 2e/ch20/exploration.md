# Exploration: College Physics 2e, Chapter 20 Electric Current, Resistance, and Ohm's Law

Read by the prep agent on 2026-09-14, before the chapter's seven section
pages were built in parallel. The book's own rules are in the folder above;
this file records what the chapter's eight modules hold, what is new in
them, and how the chapter should look when it is built.

## Why this chapter

Chapters 18 and 19 held charge still. This chapter lets it move. The whole
of the rest of the book's electricity, the circuits of Chapter 21 and the
magnetism of Chapter 22, is written in the two quantities this chapter
defines, the current in a wire and the resistance that limits it, and in
the one relation that ties them to the voltage of Chapter 19. The chapter
also carries the book's first quantitative treatment of what electricity
does to a person, and its last section is the physics of a nerve and of
the heart, which is where the reader meets the voltage of Chapter 19 as
something their own body makes.

## What is new, and the types the chapter asks for

Two kinds of quantity are new and are drawn, carried on sliders and
written into readouts on nearly every page of the chapter, so the chapter
declares both (root rule 7; the book's `RULES.md` § Types).

**`current`, the ampere.** The current is the chapter's subject. It is
drawn as the arrows along a wire in 20.2, 20.3 and 20.4, as the height of
the sine wave in 20.5 and as the number the whole of 20.6 turns on, it is
the slider of the simple circuit and of the shock calculation, and every
readout of the chapter that writes $I = \Delta Q/\Delta t$, $I = V/R$,
$I = nqAv_\text{d}$ or $P = I^2R$ states it. A current is not a charge and
not a flow rate: a flow rate is a volume per second and wears Chapter 12's
hue, while a current is a charge per second, so it is its own type.

**`resistance`, the ohm.** The resistance is the second half of Ohm's law
and the whole of 20.3. It is the slider of the simple circuit, the number
the cylinder's length and area set, the quantity that climbs with
temperature in 20.11, and the body resistance that decides whether 120 V
is a tickle or a fatal shock. Chapter 12's flow resistance $R$ is untyped
and stays untyped; this is a different quantity in a different chapter and
takes a type because its figures draw it.

**The resistivity stays untyped, in ink.** The book's rules put every
material constant in ink, naming a specific heat, a latent heat, a
conductivity, an emissivity and an expansion coefficient, and the
resistivity $\rho$ is exactly such a constant: it belongs to the material
and not to the object. It is a dropdown of materials in the figures of
20.3, not a hue, and the temperature coefficient $\alpha$ is untyped for
the same reason, as Chapter 13's $\alpha$ of thermal expansion already is.
The resistance the figure computes from them does wear its type hue, which
is the distinction the section is teaching.

**Everything else the chapter writes is already declared.** The voltage
and the potential difference are Chapter 19's `voltage`; the charge, the
electron's charge and the electric field are Chapter 18's `charge` and
`electric-field`; the power is Chapter 7's `power` and the energy a bill
is written in is Chapter 7's `energy`; the drift velocity is a `velocity`;
the frequency of AC power is Chapter 16's `frequency`; the temperature and
the temperature change of 20.3 are Chapter 13's `temperature`; the length
and diameter of a wire are `position`. The cross-sectional area, the free
charge density $n$, the number of electrons, the resistivity and its
coefficient, and the mass and specific heat that the Integrated Concepts
problems bring in stay untyped and in ink.

## BE INSPIRING (rule 23)

The hardest thing about this chapter is that nothing in it can be seen. A
wire carrying twenty amps looks exactly like a wire carrying none, and the
reader is asked to believe in a river of electrons moving at a tenth of a
millimetre a second inside it. Every figure of this chapter should be an
answer to that, and the chapter's whole story can be told as one wire seen
at four scales.

**The wire, seen close.** 20.1's central figure should be the inside of a
copper wire drawn at the scale where electrons are things: the free
electrons drawn with `F.el('e-')`, bouncing off the lattice in a fast
random walk, and a field switched on that tilts the walk so that the crowd
creeps one way while each electron still rattles. Two readouts run side by
side, the electron's own speed and the drift velocity, and the reader
watches a number four orders of magnitude smaller than the other emerge
from the same picture. The same engine, with the wire's radius and the
current on sliders, is the figure for $I = nqAv_\text{d}$: widen the wire
and the crowd slows, raise the current and it hurries. The peas in the
straw of the book's take-home box are the reason the lamp lights at once,
and a second panel drawn beside the crowd, a pulse running the length of
the wire while the electrons barely move, is worth more than any sentence
about $10^8$ m/s.

**The wire, as a circuit.** 20.2 should draw the book's schematic and let
the reader turn the two knobs that exist in the world, the battery's
voltage and the resistor's resistance, and watch the current answer. The
figure earns its place only if the current is visible as something other
than a number: the arrows should quicken, the resistor should warm, and
the readout should write $I = V/R$ in three colours so that the equation
and the picture are the same statement. The fluid analogy the book draws
in words, the pump and the narrow pipe, belongs beside it as a second
state of the same figure, since the reader has just spent Chapter 12 on
pipes.

**The wire, as a shape and a material.** 20.3 is the section where the
reader can be given a laboratory. One figure, a cylinder whose length,
diameter and material the reader sets, with the resistivity table as a
dropdown of real substances from silver to Teflon and the resistance on a
logarithmic scale beside it, teaches $R = \rho L/A$ and the twenty-two
orders of magnitude between a conductor and an insulator in one gesture.
Stretching the wire while its volume is held fixed, which two of the
chapter's problems ask for, should be one of its states, so the reader
discovers that resistance goes as the square of the stretch without being
told. The temperature figure is the tungsten filament: drag it from room
temperature to 2850 °C and watch its resistance climb by a factor of
fourteen, with mercury's superconducting cliff drawn on the same axes as
the thing that breaks the rule.

**The wire, carrying power.** 20.4's figure should be the transmission
line, not the light bulb: a hundred megawatts sent down a one-ohm line,
with the voltage on a slider, and the fraction of the power lost as heat
drawn as a shaded band that swells as the voltage falls. The reader moves
one slider from 480 V to 200 kV and watches a catastrophe become a quarter
of a percent, which is the reason the grid looks the way it does.

**The wire, alive.** 20.5's figure is the sine wave with the circuit drawn
beside it, the current reversing as the wave crosses zero, the power curve
sitting entirely above the axis, and the rms line drawn across it as the
constant DC that would do the same work; the 120 V in the wall and the
170 V peak become the same fact. 20.6 turns the same current on a person:
one figure with the voltage and the body's resistance on sliders, the
current it produces read against Table 20.3's bands, and the band the
reader is in named in words, so that the lesson that voltage alone is not
a hazard is something the reader works out rather than reads. 20.7 closes
the chapter inside a neuron, with the membrane's ions drawn in the element
palette, the reader stimulating the axon and watching the action potential
run down it, and the same pulse drawn as the ECG a doctor reads. The
chapter opens on a river turning turbines and ends on the current that
makes a heart beat, and the figures should make the reader feel that those
are the same physics.
