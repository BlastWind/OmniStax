## Why this chapter

Chapter 25 treated light as rays, and this chapter shows where that picture fails.
When light meets something about as small as its wavelength, it behaves like the
wave Chapter 24 said it was: it bends around edges, and waves from two openings add
and cancel. The chapter builds this from Huygens's principle, proves it with
Young's two slits, sharpens it with a grating, turns it back on a single slit and a
circular aperture to find the limit of every telescope and microscope, finds it
again in soap films and coatings, and closes on polarization, the proof that light
is transverse.

## What is new

- **No new type.** Lengths are `position`, the intensity of Malus's law is
  Chapter 17's `intensity`, the polarization arrow is Chapter 18's
  `electric-field`. Seven typed symbol rows are staged because the plain keys mean
  something else elsewhere: `λ_n`, `Δl`, `Δy` are new; `D_slit` and `D_ap` because
  `D` is a displacement (Chapter 3) and the two $D$s of this chapter mean a slit
  width and an aperture diameter; `t_film` because `t` is time; `I_0pol` because
  `I_0` is Chapter 17's reference intensity. Untyped `n_3` and `θ_b` are new;
  `NA` and `α_half` came with Chapter 26 and are reused; `d` (`\kd`) is reused for
  the slit and grating spacing and the distance to an objective.
- **Chapter 26's numerical aperture arrives first.** 26.4 (Microscopes) owns
  `numerical-aperture`; 27.6 recalls it and adds its link to resolving power, as
  `resolution-and-numerical-aperture`.
- **Two errata carried as printed.** 27.4 speaks of "the CD pictured in the
  opening photograph of this chapter", but the second edition's opening photograph
  is Blodgett at her trough; 27.1's Making Connections: Waves says matter waves are
  seen "in Special Relativity", which is not where the book treats them. 27.3's
  problem `fs-id1169737758071` and others cite the Problem-Solving Strategies of
  27.7 by name. Example 27.7's $\frac{\lambda n}{2}$
  in part (b) is a typesetting slip for $\lambda_n/2$, carried as printed and named
  in 27.7's notes. 27.6's last problem spells "Raleigh" for Rayleigh.

## Be inspiring (rule 23)

This is the chapter in which light shows it is a wave, and the figures should let
the reader watch it happen rather than be told.

- **One ripple tank for the whole chapter's first half.** Wavefronts arrive at a
  barrier; the reader chooses one opening or two, widens the opening against the
  wavelength and watches the ray-like beam of geometric optics dissolve into
  diffraction, then into Young's fringes, with the screen's pattern building beside
  it in the true colour of the chosen wavelength.
- **A screen point you drag.** In 27.3 the reader moves a point along the screen
  and sees the two paths, their difference $d\sin\theta$ in wavelengths, and the
  two waves arriving in or out of phase, so the bright and dark conditions are
  read off the picture.
- **From two slits to a thousand.** A slit count on 27.4's intensity graph that
  sharpens the maxima as it rises is the grating's whole argument in one control;
  white light turns the orders into rainbows with violet inside.
- **The resolution limit as two stars merging.** Two Airy patterns whose
  separation and aperture the reader controls, with a dashed special at the
  Rayleigh angle, make 1.22 $\lambda/D$ something seen, not memorized.
- **A soap film that thins.** A film thickness slider on a wedge or bubble, the
  reflected colour computed from the two rays and their phase changes, and the film
  going black at its thinnest, answers the introduction's question about the
  bubbles.
- **Polarizers turned in the hand.** The 3D chain of two, then three, filters, with
  the field arrow shrinking as $\cos\theta$ and the intensity as $\cos^2\theta$,
  is where the reader discovers that a third filter lets light back through.

## Errata carried as printed

although they are treated in a later chapter. 27.4's opening paragraph points at
the "CD pictured in the opening photograph of this chapter", although the chapter
opens on Katharine Burr Blodgett. Example 27.3 prints $y_{\text{V}} = 0.815$ m

takes the line spacing as $1.00\times 10^{-6}$ m, although 1000 lines in 10.00 mm
are $1.00\times 10^{-5}$ m apart. The key to 27.4's problem on a
30,000-line-per-centimeter grating writes (c) in centimeters where it means lines
per centimeter. Example 27.7(b) prints $\frac{\lambda n}{2}$ for $\lambda_n/2$.
27.6's laser printer problem spells the criterion "Raleigh's"; it is unkeyed and
left out. Each is named in its section's `notes`.
