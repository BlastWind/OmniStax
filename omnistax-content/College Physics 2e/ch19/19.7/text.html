What a capacitor's stored energy is used for

Most of us have seen dramatizations in which medical personnel use a defibrillator to pass an electric current through a patient’s heart to get it to beat normally. (Review Figure 19.23.) Often realistic in detail, the person applying the shock directs another person to “make it 400 joules this time.” The energy delivered by the defibrillator is stored in a capacitor and can be adjusted to fit the situation. SI units of joules are often employed. Less dramatic is the use of capacitors in microelectronics, such as certain handheld calculators, to supply energy when batteries are charged. (See Figure 19.23.) Capacitors are also used to supply energy for flash lamps on cameras.

The energy stored in a capacitor

Energy stored in a capacitor is electrical potential energy, and it is thus related to the charge $\kQch$ and voltage $\kV$ on the capacitor. We must be careful when applying the equation for electrical potential energy $\kdPE = \kq\kdV$ to a capacitor. Remember that $\kdPE$ is the potential energy of a charge $\kq$ going through a voltage $\kdV$. But the capacitor starts with zero voltage and gradually comes up to its full voltage as it is charged. The first charge placed on a capacitor experiences a change in voltage $\kdV = 0$, since the capacitor has zero voltage when uncharged. The final charge placed on a capacitor experiences $\kdV = \kV$, since the capacitor now has its full voltage $\kV$ on it. The average voltage on the capacitor during the charging process is $\kV/2$, and so the average voltage experienced by the full charge $\kq$ is $\kV/2$. Thus the energy stored in a capacitor, $\kEcap$, is

$$\kEcap = \frac{\kQch\kV}{2},$$

where $\kQch$ is the charge on a capacitor with a voltage $\kV$ applied. (Note that the energy is not $\kQch\kV$, but $\kQch\kV/2$.) Charge and voltage are related to the capacitance $\kCap$ of a capacitor by $\kQch = \kCap\kV$, and so the expression for $\kEcap$ can be algebraically manipulated into three equivalent expressions:

$$\kEcap = \frac{\kQch\kV}{2} = \frac{\kCap\kV^2}{2} = \frac{\kQch^2}{2\kCap},$$

where $\kQch$ is the charge and $\kV$ the voltage on a capacitor $\kCap$. The energy is in joules for a charge in coulombs, voltage in volts, and capacitance in farads.

Energy Stored in Capacitors

The energy stored in a capacitor can be expressed in three ways:

$$\kEcap = \frac{\kQch\kV}{2} = \frac{\kCap\kV^2}{2} = \frac{\kQch^2}{2\kCap},$$

where $\kQch$ is the charge, $\kV$ is the voltage, and $\kCap$ is the capacitance of the capacitor. The energy is in joules for a charge in coulombs, voltage in volts, and capacitance in farads.

The defibrillator

In a defibrillator, the delivery of a large charge in a short burst to a set of paddles across a person’s chest can be a lifesaver. The person’s heart attack might have arisen from the onset of fast, irregular beating of the heart—cardiac or ventricular fibrillation. The application of a large shock of electrical energy can terminate the arrhythmia and allow the body’s pacemaker to resume normal patterns. Today it is common for ambulances to carry a defibrillator, which also uses an electrocardiogram to analyze the patient’s heartbeat pattern. Automated external defibrillators (AED) are found in many public places (Figure 19.24). These are designed to be used by lay persons. The device automatically diagnoses the patient’s heart condition and then applies the shock with appropriate energy and waveform. CPR is recommended in many cases before use of an AED.

Example 19.11 · Capacitance in a Heart Defibrillator

A heart defibrillator delivers $4.00\times 10^{2}\ \text{J}$ of energy by discharging a capacitor initially at $1.00\times 10^{4}\ \text{V}$. What is its capacitance?

Strategy

We are given $\kEcap$ and $\kV$, and we are asked to find the capacitance $\kCap$. Of the three expressions in the equation for $\kEcap$, the most convenient relationship is

$$\kEcap = \frac{\kCap\kV^2}{2}.$$

Solution

Solving this expression for $\kCap$ and entering the given values yields

$$\begin{aligned}\kCap &= \frac{2\kEcap}{\kV^2} = \frac{2(4.00\times 10^{2}\ \text{J})}{(1.00\times 10^{4}\ \text{V})^2} = 8.00\times 10^{-6}\ \text{F}\\ &= 8.00\ \mu\text{F}.\end{aligned}$$

Discussion

This is a fairly large, but manageable, capacitance at $1.00\times 10^{4}\ \text{V}$.
