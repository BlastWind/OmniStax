(`source/osbooks-college-physics-bundle`), not the PDF. The book's
organisation (book → chapters → sections → untitled narrative headers, one
CNXML module per section, the apparatus inside the module) is as recorded in
`ch02/exploration.md`; nothing differs here.

## Why this chapter

Chapter 16 taught what a wave is; this chapter takes the one wave everyone
has been hearing all along and asks what its physics has to do with what
the ear reports. Sound is a disturbance of matter carried outward as
compressions and rarefactions, a longitudinal pressure wave, and the first
three sections give it the properties every wave has: a speed set by the
medium and its temperature, a frequency the ear hears as pitch, a
wavelength fixed by $v_\text{w} = f\lambda$, and an intensity, the power
crossing each square metre, which the ear meets over twelve powers of ten
and so is quoted on a logarithmic scale in decibels. The middle of the
chapter is the two things a moving source does to a wave: shift its
frequency for anyone it approaches or leaves, and, past the speed of sound,
pile every wavefront onto one cone that arrives as a sonic boom. Then the
wave meets itself: two sounds add as pressures, cancel or reinforce, and an
air column that is the right length keeps only the frequencies whose
reflections return in step, which is why a tube closed at one end sounds
its odd harmonics and a tube open at both sounds them all. The chapter
closes on the ear, which converts pressure into nerve signals and hears
loudness as a function of frequency as much as of intensity, and on
ultrasound, which turns the same echoes, impedances and Doppler shifts into
images and blood speeds.

## What is new, and the types the chapter asks for

- **Intensity is a type.** $I = P/A$ is defined in 17.3, every problem of
  that section and of 17.6 and 17.7 turns on it, a figure of 17.3 that
  lets the reader raise a pressure amplitude draws the intensity as the
  height of a bar and states $I$ in W/m² in its readout, and a figure of
  17.7 shows a fraction of it reflected at a boundary. Chapters 24 (electromagnetic waves) and 27
  (wave optics) will draw the same quantity for light, and 16.11 states it
  for waves in general. By rule 7 it is a type of the book's: declared
  `intensity`, labelled intensity, dimension W/m². The book already holds
  `I` as the moment of inertia (Chapter 10) with the macro `\kI`, so
  intensity is keyed `I_intens` with the macro `\kIntens`, and its
  variants are `I_0` (the threshold, `\kIo`), `I_1` and `I_2`.
- **The sound intensity level stays untyped.** $\beta = 10\log_{10}(I/I_0)$
  is a pure number, a ratio taken in logarithm and multiplied by ten, and
  the book says so in its own definition ("a unitless quantity telling you
  the level of the sound relative to a fixed standard"). Rule 7 keeps
  dimensionless coefficients in ink, and the decibel is not a dimension.
  A figure that slides $I$ over its twelve decades and writes $\beta$
  beside it colours $I$ and leaves $\beta$ in ink, which is itself the
  lesson: one is the physical quantity and the other is a scale for
  reading it. The book already holds `β` (Chapter 13's coefficient of
  volume expansion, untyped, LaTeX `\beta`), so the sound level is keyed
  `β_dB`, LaTeX `\beta`, no type and no macro, and the chapter's variables
  table gives it its meaning.
- **Acoustic impedance stays untyped.** $Z = \rho v$ is a property of a
  medium, tabulated beside density and the speed of sound in Table 17.5 the
  way the book tabulates conductivities and emissivities, and the book's
  rules keep every material constant in ink. A figure of 17.7 with a
  choice of medium on each side of a boundary states $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ in
  ink and colours the reflected fraction of the intensity. The intensity
  reflection coefficient $a$ is a ratio and is ink too; because `a` is the
  acceleration row with `\ka`, it is keyed `a_refl`.
- **The wavelength is a position.** $\lambda$ is a length, drawn as a
  bracket between two compressions in 17.2's tuning-fork figure and along
  the tubes of 17.5, and it takes the position hue as the amplitude $X$ of
  Chapter 16 does. The book has no `λ` row yet, because 16.9, which
  introduces it, is not built; this chapter stages `λ` (`\klam`) and
  `λ_prime` (`\lambda'`, `\klamprime`) as position rows, and 16.9 will use
  them as they stand when it is built.
- **The speed of sound is Chapter 3's `v_w`.** The book writes the speed of
  sound $v_\text{w}$ with the same LaTeX as 3.5's wind velocity, and both
  are velocities; the row `v_w` (`\kvw`) is used as it stands and the
  chapter's variables table gives it the meaning the speed of sound. The
  source and observer speeds of the Doppler effect are new velocity rows
  `v_s` (`\kvs`) and `v_obs` (`\kvobs`), and 17.7's blood velocity is
  `v_blood` (`\kvblood`, since `\kvb` is the average speed's).
- **Frequencies.** The observed and source frequencies of 17.4 are
  `f_obs` (`\kfobs`) and `f_src` (LaTeX `f_{\text{s}}`, `\kfsrc`, since
  `f_s` is Chapter 5's static friction); 17.5's harmonics are `f_n`,
  `f_1`, `f_2`, `f_3` and `f_prime`; 17.7's beat frequency is `f_B`. All
  are `frequency` rows.
- **The pressure amplitude.** $\Delta p$ is a pressure, half the difference
  between the greatest and least pressure in the wave, and 17.3's figure
  draws it as the height of the gauge-pressure graph. `Δp` is Chapter 8's
  impulse-momentum row, so it is keyed `Δp_press` (LaTeX `\Delta p`,
  `\kdpamp`, type `pressure`).
- **Rows used as they stand.** `f` (`\kf`), `T` for a period (`\kT`),
  `T_temp` for the temperature in the speed-of-sound formula (`\kTemp`),
  `v` (`\kv`) in $Z = \rho v$, `v_rms` (`\kvrms`), `k_boltz`, `m`,
  `P` (`\kP`) for power, `A` for area, `ρ_dens` (`\krho`), `P_press`
  (`\kPr`), `F` (`\kF`), `L_len` for the length of a tube, `n` for the
  harmonic number, `θ` for the cone's angle, `c` (`\kc`), `d`, `t`.
  Nothing existing is restaged or changed.

## BE INSPIRING (rule 23)

Sound is the wave the reader has been inside all their life and has never
once seen, and this chapter's figures can be the first time it is drawn
moving.

- **Air that the reader can see.** The book draws compressions as arcs. A
  figure that draws the air as a field of dots, each one oscillating a
  little about its rest position while the packing travels outward, shows
  the thing the text says three times and no still can: that the air does
  not go anywhere, and the wave does. Put the gauge-pressure graph
  underneath and let the reader watch the crests of the graph ride along
  with the dense places in the dots. Every later figure of the chapter is
  that one figure with something changed.
- **The tuning fork with two sliders.** Frequency up, wavefronts crowd
  together; temperature up, they spread, because $v_\text{w}$ rose and
  $f$ did not. The readout $v_\text{w} = f\lambda$ stays true on every
  slider position, and the reader learns which of the three is set by the
  source and which by the air.
- **A slider over twelve decades.** Intensity is the chapter's new type and
  the decibel is its new scale, and neither means anything until the
  reader has felt how far apart 0 and 120 dB are. One slider on $\beta$
  from 0 to 160, a bar for $I$ that would run off the page after 20 dB
  unless drawn on a log axis, the pressure graph's amplitude alongside,
  and the book's own list of examples (leaves, whisper, conversation,
  truck, concert, jet) sliding past as labels. The reader moves 3 dB and
  sees the bar double; moves 10 dB and sees it grow tenfold.
- **The Doppler scene as one drawing that runs.** A source emitting rings
  at $f_\text{s}$, the rings spreading at $v_\text{w}$ from the point where
  each was born, and two observers who count the rings that reach them.
  Slide $v_s$ up and the rings bunch ahead and stretch behind while the
  readout writes $f_\text{obs}$ for each observer; slide it past
  $v_\text{w}$ and the rings fall behind the source and their envelope
  becomes the cone of 17.16, with $\theta$ shrinking as the source runs.
  The sonic boom then needs no separate explanation; it is what the
  reader just watched happen.
- **Two sounds adding as numbers.** Noise cancellation is two pressure
  graphs and their sum. A figure with a slider on the phase of the second
  sound lets the reader find the position where the sum is flat, and the
  readout writes the total gauge pressure, which is what Pascal's
  principle promised.
- **A tube that keeps only some frequencies.** Send a pulse down a closed
  tube, watch it reflect, and see it meet the fork in step or out of step
  as the reader slides the tube's length; then the standing-wave envelope
  appears at the lengths that work and nowhere else. The harmonic ladder,
  closed on one side of the figure and open on the other, with the same
  $L$ for both, shows why the open tube sounds an octave higher and fills
  in the even harmonics the closed tube skips.
- **A graph the reader stands on.** The phon curves are a lookup, and
  Example 17.6 is three lookups. A crosshair the reader drags over
  frequency and decibels, the phon value interpolated live, makes the
  ear's dip at 4000 Hz something felt: the same 20 dB is loud there and
  inaudible at 60 Hz.
- **Colour that carries the physics.** Intensity in its own hue on the bar
  and the readout, $\beta$ in ink beside it; frequency on the fork and on
  $f_\text{obs}$, velocity on $v_\text{w}$, $v_s$ and $v_\text{obs}$, so
  the Doppler readout reads as a frequency times a ratio of velocities;
  pressure on $\Delta p$ and position on $\lambda$ and $L$. The
  impedances of 17.7 stay in ink beside a coloured reflected intensity,
  which says what the section says: the medium is a property, the wave
  is the quantity.
