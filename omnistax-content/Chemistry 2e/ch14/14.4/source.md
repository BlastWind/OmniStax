## Salts with Acidic Ions
Salts are ionic compounds composed of cations and anions, either of which may be capable of undergoing an acid or base ionization reaction with water. Aqueous salt solutions, therefore, may be acidic, basic, or neutral, depending on the relative acid-base strengths of the salt's constituent ions. For example, dissolving ammonium chloride in water results in its dissociation, as described by the equation

$$ {\text{NH}}_{4}\text{Cl}(s)\rightleftharpoons {\text{NH}}_{4}{}^{+}(aq)+{\text{Cl}}^{-}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idm209439760}

The ammonium ion is the conjugate acid of the base ammonia, NH<sub>3</sub>; its acid ionization (or acid hydrolysis) reaction is represented by

$$ {\text{NH}}_{4}{}^{\text{+}}(aq)+{\text{H}}_{2}\text{O}(l)\;\rightleftharpoons \;{\text{H}}_{3}{\text{O}}^{\text{+}}(aq)+{\text{NH}}_{3}(aq)\hspace{5em}{K}_{\text{a}}={K}_{\text{w}}\text{/}{K}_{\text{b}} $$  {eq:fs-idm137491888a}

Since ammonia is a weak base, *K*<sub>b</sub> is measurable and *K*<sub>a</sub> > 0 (ammonium ion is a weak acid).
The chloride ion is the conjugate base of hydrochloric acid, and so its base ionization (or *base hydrolysis*) reaction is represented by

$$ {\text{Cl}}^{-}(aq)+{\text{H}}_{2}\text{O}(l)\rightleftharpoons \text{HCl}(aq)+{\text{OH}}^{-}(aq)\hspace{5em}{K}_{\text{b}}={K}_{\text{w}}\text{/}{K}_{\text{a}} $$  {eq:fs-idm248062064}

Since HCl is a strong acid, *K*<sub>a</sub> is immeasurably large and *K*<sub>b</sub> ≈ 0 (chloride ions don’t undergo appreciable hydrolysis).
Thus, dissolving ammonium chloride in water yields a solution of weak acid cations (${\text{NH}}_{4}{}^{+}$) and inert anions (Cl<sup>−</sup>), resulting in an acidic solution.

:::example {ex:fs-idp38392128} Calculating the pH of an Acidic Salt Solution
Aniline is an amine that is used to manufacture dyes. It is isolated as anilinium chloride, $[{\text{C}}_{6}{\text{H}}_{5}{\text{NH}}_{3}]\text{Cl},$ a salt prepared by the reaction of the weak base aniline and hydrochloric acid. What is the pH of a 0.233 *M* solution of anilinium chloride

$$ {\text{C}}_{6}{\text{H}}_{5}{\text{NH}}_{3}{}^{\text{+}}(aq)+{\text{H}}_{2}\text{O}(l)\;\rightleftharpoons \;{\text{H}}_{3}{\text{O}}^{\text{+}}(aq)+{\text{C}}_{6}{\text{H}}_{5}{\text{NH}}_{2}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idm149245808}

**Solution**
The *K*<sub>a</sub> for anilinium ion is derived from the *K*<sub>b</sub> for its conjugate base, aniline (see [Appendix I](module:m68867)):

$$ {K}_{\text{a}}\;=\;\frac{{K}_{\text{w}}}{{K}_{\text{b}}}\;=\;\frac{1.0\;\times \;{10}^{−14}}{4.3\;\times \;{10}^{−10}}\;=2.3\;\times \;{10}^{−5} $$  {eq:fs-idm169067840}

## Salts with Basic Ions
As another example, consider dissolving sodium acetate in water:

$$ {\text{NaCH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}(s)\;\leftrightharpoons {\text{Na}}^{+}(aq)+{\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}{}^{-}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idm243373152}

The sodium ion does not undergo appreciable acid or base ionization and has no effect on the solution pH. This may seem obvious from the ion's formula, which indicates no hydrogen or oxygen atoms, but some dissolved metal ions function as weak acids, as addressed later in this section.
The acetate ion, ${\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}{}^{-}\text{,}$ is the conjugate base of acetic acid, CH<sub>3</sub>CO<sub>2</sub>H, and so its base ionization (or *base hydrolysis*) reaction is represented by

$$ {\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}{}^{-}(aq)+{\text{H}}_{2}\text{O}(l)\rightleftharpoons {\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}\text{H}(aq)+\text{OH}-(aq)\hspace{3em}{K}_{\text{b}}={K}_{\text{w}}\text{/}{K}_{\text{a}} $$  {eq:fs-idm641525520}

Because acetic acid is a weak acid, its *K*<sub>a</sub> is measurable and *K*<sub>b</sub> > 0 (acetate ion is a weak base).
Dissolving sodium acetate in water yields a solution of inert cations (Na<sup>+</sup>) and weak base anions ${\text{(CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}{}^{-}),$ resulting in a basic solution.

:::example {ex:fs-idp37320880} Equilibrium in a Solution of a Salt of a Weak Acid and a Strong Base
Determine the acetic acid concentration in a solution with $[{\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}{}^{\text{−}}]=0.050\;M$ and [OH<sup>−</sup>] = 2.5 $\times$ 10<sup>−6</sup> *M* at equilibrium. The reaction is:

$$ {\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}{}^{\text{−}}(aq)+{\text{H}}_{2}\text{O}(l)\;\rightleftharpoons \;{\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}\text{H}(aq)+{\text{OH}}^{\text{−}}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idm148031584}

**Solution**
The provided equilibrium concentrations and a value for the equilibrium constant will permit calculation of the missing equilibrium concentration. The process in question is the base ionization of acetate ion, for which

$$ {K}_{\text{b}}\;(\text{for}\;{\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}{}^{\text{−}})\;=\;\frac{{K}_{\text{w}}}{{K}_{\text{a}}\;(\text{for}\;{\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}\text{H})}\;=\;\frac{1.0\;\times \;{10}^{−14}}{1.8\;\times \;{10}^{−5}}\;=5.6\;\times \;{10}^{−10} $$  {eq:fs-idm62642320}

Substituting the available values into the *K*<sub>b</sub> expression gives

$$ {K}_{\text{b}}=\;\frac{[{\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}\text{H}][{\text{OH}}^{\text{−}}]}{[{\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}{}^{\text{−}}]}\;=5.6\;\times \;{10}^{−10} $$  {eq:fs-idm218140928}

$$ =\;\frac{[{\text{CH}}_{3}{\text{CO}}_{2}\text{H}](2.5\;\times \;{10}^{−6})}{(0.050)}\;=5.6\;\times \;{10}^{−10} $$  {eq:fs-idp923936}

Solving the above equation for the acetic acid molarity yields [CH<sub>3</sub>CO<sub>2</sub>H] = 1.1 $\times$ 10<sup>−5</sup> *M*.

## Salts with Acidic and Basic Ions
Some salts are composed of both acidic and basic ions, and so the pH of their solutions will depend on the relative strengths of these two species. Likewise, some salts contain a single ion that is amphiprotic, and so the relative strengths of this ion’s acid and base character will determine its effect on solution pH. For both types of salts, a comparison of the *K*<sub>a</sub> and *K*<sub>b</sub> values allows prediction of the solution’s acid-base status, as illustrated in the following example exercise.

:::example {ex:fs-idm161229856} Determining the Acidic or Basic Nature of Salts
Determine whether aqueous solutions of the following salts are acidic, basic, or neutral:
(a) KBr
(b) NaHCO<sub>3</sub>
(c) Na<sub>2</sub>HPO<sub>4</sub>
(d) NH<sub>4</sub>F
**Solution**
Consider each of the ions separately in terms of its effect on the pH of the solution, as shown here:
(a) The K<sup>+</sup> cation is inert and will not affect pH. The bromide ion is the conjugate base of a strong acid, and so it is of negligible base strength (no appreciable base ionization). The solution is neutral.
(b) The Na<sup>+</sup> cation is inert and will not affect the pH of the solution; while the ${\text{HCO}}_{3}{}^{\text{−}}$ anion is amphiprotic. The *K*<sub>a</sub> of ${\text{HCO}}_{3}{}^{\text{−}}$ is 4.7 $\times$ 10<sup>−11</sup>,and its *K*<sub>b</sub> is $\frac{1.0\;\times \;{10}^{−14}}{4.3\;\times \;{10}^{−7}}\;=\;2.3\;\times \;{10}^{−8}.$
Since *K*<sub>b</sub> >> *K*<sub>a</sub>, the solution is basic.
(c) The Na<sup>+</sup> cation is inert and will not affect the pH of the solution, while the ${\text{HPO}}_{4}{}^{\text{2−}}$ anion is amphiprotic. The *K*<sub>a</sub> of ${\text{HPO}}_{4}{}^{\text{2−}}$ is 4.2 $\times$ 10<sup>−13</sup>,
and its *K*<sub>b</sub> is $\frac{1.0\;\times \;{10}^{−14}}{6.2\;\times \;{10}^{−8}}\;=\;1.6\;\times \;{10}^{−7}.$ Because *K*<sub>b</sub> >> *K*<sub>a</sub>, the solution is basic.
(d) The ${\text{NH}}_{4}{}^{\text{+}}$ ion is acidic (see above discussion) and the F<sup>−</sup> ion is basic (conjugate base of the weak acid HF). Comparing the two ionization constants: *K*<sub>a</sub> of ${\text{NH}}_{4}{}^{\text{+}}$ is 5.6 $\times$ 10<sup>−10</sup> and the *K*<sub>b</sub> of F<sup>−</sup> is 1.6 $\times$ 10<sup>−11</sup>, so the solution is acidic, since *K*<sub>a</sub> > *K*<sub>b</sub>.

## The Ionization of Hydrated Metal Ions
Unlike the group 1 and 2 metal ions of the preceding examples (Na<sup>+</sup>, Ca<sup>2+</sup>, etc.), some metal ions function as acids in aqueous solutions. These ions are not just loosely solvated by water molecules when dissolved, instead they are covalently bonded to a fixed number of water molecules to yield a complex ion (see chapter on coordination chemistry). As an example, the dissolution of aluminum nitrate in water is typically represented as

$$ \text{Al}({\text{NO}}_{3}{)}_{3}(s)\rightleftharpoons {\text{Al}}^{3+}(aq)+3{\text{NO}}_{3}{}^{-}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idm253215984}

However, the aluminum(III) ion actually reacts with six water molecules to form a stable complex ion, and so the more explicit representation of the dissolution process is

$$ \text{Al}{({\text{NO}}_{3})}_{3}(s)+6{\text{H}}_{2}\text{O}(l)\;\rightleftharpoons \;\text{Al}{({\text{H}}_{2}\text{O})}_{6}{}^{3+}(aq)+3{\text{NO}}_{3}{}^{\text{−}}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idm117678832}

As shown in [ref:CNX_Chem_14_04_hydronium], the $\text{Al}{({\text{H}}_{\text{2}}\text{O})}_{6}{}^{3+}$ ions involve bonds between a central Al atom and the O atoms of the six water molecules. Consequently, the bonded water molecules' O–H bonds are more polar than in nonbonded water molecules, making the bonded molecules more prone to donation of a hydrogen ion:

$$ \text{Al}{({\text{H}}_{2}\text{O})}_{6}{}^{3+}(aq)+{\text{H}}_{2}\text{O}(l)\;\rightleftharpoons \;{\text{H}}_{3}{\text{O}}^{\text{+}}(aq)+\text{Al}{({\text{H}}_{2}\text{O})}_{5}(\text{O}{\text{H})}^{2+}(aq)\hspace{4em}{K}_{\text{a}}=1.4\;\times \;{10}^{−5} $$  {eq:fs-idm108244016}

The conjugate base produced by this process contains five other bonded water molecules capable of acting as acids, and so the sequential or step-wise transfer of protons is possible as depicted in few equations below:

$$ \text{Al}{({\text{H}}_{2}\text{O})}_{6}{}^{3+}(aq)+{\text{H}}_{2}\text{O}(l)\;\rightleftharpoons \;{\text{H}}_{3}{\text{O}}^{\text{+}}(aq)+\text{Al}{({\text{H}}_{2}\text{O})}_{5}({\text{O}\text{H})}^{2+}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idm46178496}

$$ \text{Al}{({\text{H}}_{2}\text{O})}_{5}({\text{OH})}^{2+}(aq)+{\text{H}}_{2}\text{O}(l)\;\rightleftharpoons \;{\text{H}}_{3}{\text{O}}^{\text{+}}(aq)+\text{Al}{({\text{H}}_{2}\text{O})}_{4}({\text{OH})}_{2}{}^{\text{+}}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idm2413712}

$$ \text{Al}{({\text{H}}_{2}\text{O})}_{4}({\text{OH})}_{2}{}^{\text{+}}(aq)+{\text{H}}_{2}\text{O}(l)\;\rightleftharpoons \;{\text{H}}_{3}{\text{O}}^{\text{+}}(aq)+\text{Al}{({\text{H}}_{2}\text{O})}_{3}({\text{OH})}_{3}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idp12750352}

This is an example of a polyprotic acid, the topic of discussion in a later section of this chapter.

## Key Concepts and Summary {section:summary}
The ions composing salts may possess acidic or basic character, ionizing when dissolved in water to yield acidic or basic solutions. Acidic cations are typically the conjugate partners of weak bases, and basic anions are the conjugate partners of weak acids. Many metal ions bond to water molecules when dissolved to yield complex ions that may function as acids.
