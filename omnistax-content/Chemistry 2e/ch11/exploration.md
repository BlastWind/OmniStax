## Why this chapter

Chapter 10 held one substance together by its intermolecular forces; this chapter lets a second substance in. It asks why two substances mix and what heat that costs (11.1), what happens when the solute comes apart into ions (11.2), how much will dissolve and how temperature and pressure move that limit (11.3), what the dissolved particles do to the solvent's vapor pressure, boiling point, freezing point and flow through a membrane, whatever those particles are (11.4), and what happens when the dispersed particles are too large to be a solution and too small to settle (11.5). The thread is the three attractions of Figure 11.4 (solute-solute, solvent-solvent, solute-solvent) weighed against the dispersal of matter.

## Root rule 23, BE INSPIRING

The chapter's surprise is that four properties answer to one number. A single slider of solute particles per kilogram of solvent, in 11.4, should at once lower the vapor pressure in the box above the liquid, push the phase diagram's liquid-vapor curve down and its solid-liquid curve left, and raise the column in the osmosis tube, and a choice of sucrose, NaCl or CaCl₂ at the same molality should multiply every effect by i, with the measured i of Table 11.3 falling short of the prediction as ion pairs form. 11.3's Lake Nyos is the chapter's drama: Henry's law and a warming lake, drawn as a column of water whose dissolved CO₂ comes out of solution as it rises.
