# Lewis Acids and Bases

## Learning Objectives {section:learning-objectives}
By the end of this section, you will be able to:
- Explain the Lewis model of acid-base chemistry
- Write equations for the formation of adducts and complex ions
- Perform equilibrium calculations involving formation constants

In 1923, G. N. {index:Lewis} proposed a generalized definition of acid-base behavior in which acids and bases are identified by their ability to accept or to donate a pair of electrons and form a coordinate covalent bond.
A {term:coordinate covalent bond} (or dative bond) occurs when one of the atoms in the bond provides both bonding electrons. For example, a coordinate covalent bond occurs when a water molecule combines with a hydrogen ion to form a hydronium ion. A coordinate covalent bond also results when an ammonia molecule combines with a hydrogen ion to form an ammonium ion. Both of these equations are shown here.

Reactions involving the formation of coordinate covalent bonds are classified as {term:Lewis acid-base chemistry}. The species donating the electron pair that compose the bond is a {term:Lewis base}, the species accepting the electron pair is a {term:Lewis acid}, and the product of the reaction is a {term:Lewis acid-base adduct}. As the two examples above illustrate, Brønsted-Lowry acid-base reactions represent a subcategory of Lewis acid reactions, specifically, those in which the acid species is H<sup>+</sup>. A few examples involving other Lewis acids and bases are described below.

Another type of Lewis acid-base chemistry involves the formation of a complex ion (or a coordination complex) comprising a central atom, typically a transition metal cation, surrounded by ions or molecules called {term:ligands}. These ligands can be neutral molecules like H<sub>2</sub>O or NH<sub>3</sub>, or ions such as CN<sup>–</sup> or OH<sup>–</sup>. Often, the ligands act as Lewis bases, donating a pair of electrons to the central atom. These types of Lewis acid-base reactions are examples of a broad subdiscipline called *coordination chemistry*—the topic of another chapter in this text.
The equilibrium constant for the reaction of a metal ion with one or more ligands to form a coordination complex is called a {term:formation constant (*K*<sub>f</sub>)} (sometimes called a stability constant). For example, the complex ion $\text{Cu}{(\text{CN})}_{2}{}^{\text{−}}$

> IMAGE {img:fs-idm22905296} src=../../media/CNX_Chem_15_02_CuCN2-_img.jpg
> alt: A Cu atom is bonded to two C atoms. Each of these C atoms is triple bonded to an N atom. Each N atom has two dots on the side of it.

is produced by the reaction

$$ {\text{Cu}}^{\text{+}}(aq)+2{\text{CN}}^{\text{−}}(aq)\rightleftharpoons \text{Cu}{(\text{CN})}_{2}{}^{\text{−}}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idm51220672}

The formation constant for this reaction is

$$ {K}_{\text{f}}=\frac{[\text{Cu}{(\text{CN})}_{2}{}^{\text{−}}]}{[{\text{Cu}}^{+}]{[{\text{CN}}^{\text{−}}]}^{2}} $$  {eq:fs-idm86529488}

Alternatively, the reverse reaction (decomposition of the complex ion) can be considered, in which case the equilibrium constant is a {term:dissociation constant (*K*<sub>d</sub>)}. Per the relation between equilibrium constants for reciprocal reactions described, the dissociation constant is the mathematical inverse of the formation constant, *K<sub>d</sub>* = K<sub>f</sub><sup>–1</sup>. A tabulation of formation constants is provided in [Appendix K](module:m68869).
As an example of dissolution by complex ion formation, let us consider what happens when we add aqueous ammonia to a mixture of silver chloride and water. Silver chloride dissolves slightly in water, giving a small concentration of Ag<sup>+</sup> ([Ag<sup>+</sup>] = 1.3 $\times$ 10<sup>–5</sup> *M*):

$$ \text{AgCl}(s)\rightleftharpoons {\text{Ag}}^{\text{+}}(aq)+{\text{Cl}}^{\text{−}}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idm70908128}

However, if NH<sub>3</sub> is present in the water, the complex ion, $\text{Ag}{({\text{NH}}_{3})}_{2}{}^{+},$ can form according to the equation:

$$ {\text{Ag}}^{\text{+}}(aq)+2{\text{NH}}_{3}(aq)\rightleftharpoons \text{Ag}{({\text{NH}}_{3})}_{2}{}^{\text{+}}(aq) $$  {eq:fs-idm30882864}

with

$$ {K}_{\text{f}}=\frac{[\text{Ag}{({\text{NH}}_{3})}_{2}{}^{+}]}{[{\text{Ag}}^{+}]{[{\text{NH}}_{3}]}^{2}}\;=1.7\;\times \;{10}^{7} $$  {eq:fs-idp57055872}

The large size of this formation constant indicates that most of the free silver ions produced by the dissolution of AgCl combine with NH <sub>3</sub> to form $\text{Ag}{({\text{NH}}_{3})}_{2}{}^{+}.$ As a consequence, the concentration of silver ions, [Ag <sup>+</sup> ], is reduced, and the reaction quotient for the dissolution of silver chloride, [Ag <sup>+</sup> ][Cl <sup>–</sup> ], falls below the solubility product of AgCl:

$$ Q\;=\;[{\text{Ag}}^{+}][{\text{Cl}}^{\text{−}}]\;<\;{K}_{\text{sp}} $$  {eq:fs-idm26618656}

More silver chloride then dissolves. If the concentration of ammonia is great enough, all of the silver chloride dissolves.

Substituting these equilibrium concentration terms into the *K*<sub>f</sub> expression gives

$$ {K}_{\text{f}}=\frac{[\text{Ag}{({\text{NH}}_{3})}_{2}{}^{+}]}{[{\text{Ag}}^{+}]{[{\text{NH}}_{3}]}^{2}} $$  {eq:fs-idp2484016}

$$ 1.7\;\times \;{10}^{7}\;\text{=}\;\frac{0.10-x}{(x){(2x)}^{2}} $$  {eq:fs-idp40680272}

The very large equilibrium constant means the amount of the complex ion that will dissociate, *x*, will be very small. Assuming *x* << 0.1 permits simplifying the above equation:

$$ 1.7\;\times \;{10}^{7}=\frac{0.10}{(x){(2x)}^{2}} $$  {eq:fs-idp73621072}

$$ {x}^{3}=\frac{0.10}{4(1.7\;\times \;{10}^{7})}\;=1.5\;\times \;{10}^{-9} $$  {eq:fs-idm73139120}

$$ x\;=\;\sqrt[3]{1.5\;\times \;{10}^{-9}}\;=\;1.1\;\times \;{10}^{-3} $$  {eq:fs-idm46937952}

Because only 1.1% of the $\text{Ag}{({\text{NH}}_{3})}_{2}{}^{+}$ dissociates into Ag<sup>+</sup> and NH<sub>3</sub>, the assumption that *x* is small is justified.
Using this value of *x* and the relations in the above ICE table allows calculation of all species’ equilibrium concentrations:

$$ [{\text{Ag}}^{+}]\;=\;0+x=1.1\;\times \;{10}^{-3}\;M $$  {eq:fs-idp47227680}

$$ [{\text{NH}}_{3}]=0+2x=2.2\;\times \;{10}^{-3}\;M $$  {eq:fs-idm85370448}

$$ [\text{Ag}{({\text{NH}}_{3})}_{2}{}^{+}]=0.10-x=0.10-0.0011=0.099 $$  {eq:fs-idp15145408}

The concentration of free silver ion in the solution is 0.0011 *M*.

## Key Concepts and Summary {section:summary}
A Lewis acid is a species that can accept an electron pair, whereas a Lewis base has an electron pair available for donation to a Lewis acid. Complex ions are examples of Lewis acid-base adducts and comprise central metal atoms or ions acting as Lewis acids bonded to molecules or ions called ligands that act as Lewis bases. The equilibrium constant for the reaction between a metal ion and ligands produces a complex ion called a formation constant; for the reverse reaction, it is called a dissociation constant.

## Glossary
- {def} **complex ion**: ion consisting of a central atom surrounding molecules or ions called ligands via coordinate covalent bonds
- {def} **coordinate covalent bond**: (also, dative bond) covalent bond in which both electrons originated from the same atom
- {def} **dissociation constant**: (***K*<sub>d</sub>**) equilibrium constant for the decomposition of a complex ion into its components
- {def} **formation constant**: (***K*<sub>f</sub>**) (also, stability constant) equilibrium constant for the formation of a complex ion from its components
- {def} **Lewis acid**: any species that can accept a pair of electrons and form a coordinate covalent bond
- {def} **Lewis acid-base adduct**: compound or ion that contains a coordinate covalent bond between a Lewis acid and a Lewis base
- {def} **Lewis acid-base chemistry**: reactions involving the formation of coordinate covalent bonds
- {def} **Lewis base**: any species that can donate a pair of electrons and form a coordinate covalent bond
- {def} **ligand**: molecule or ion acting as a Lewis base in complex ion formation; bonds to the central atom of the complex
