## What is new

## What would make this chapter stunning (root rule 23)

The chapter's three ideas are each a picture the book draws once and the reader can now move. An eigenvalue is where two curves cross: with the Robin constant h on a slider, the intersections of h/x and tan x slide along the axis and the eigenfunction beside them changes its shape at the ends, so the reader sees that a boundary condition picks the eigenvalues and that a weaker one (h → 0) lets the first one fall to zero. A series "with respect to different eigenfunctions" is the same function built two ways: f(x) = x on [0, π/2] with its eigenfunction series and its regular sine series side by side, agreeing on the interval and parting beyond it as N grows, which is the answer to the book's challenging Exercise 5.1.3. The beam swings in its modes with the vertical scale stated, and its ladder of frequencies 1, 4, 9, 16 stands on one axis beside a string's 1, 2, 3, 4, which is the sentence about the xylophone drawn. The forced string moves under a pulsing uniform force, ω on a slider with the odd multiples of πa/L marked as the resonances the text argues and the even ones as the places where "no resonance is happening", and the coefficient B(ω) graphed over ω shows the poles at odd n and the removable points at even n that the paragraph "let us not jump to conclusions" is about. Underground, a column of soil coloured by temperature breathes with the year while the profile beside it is a damped wave travelling down, the reversal depth and the ±0.66 °C swing read off it: the cellar's refrigerator in one picture.
