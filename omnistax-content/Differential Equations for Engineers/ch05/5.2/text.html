Transversal vibrations of a beam

The eigenfunction series can arise even from higher-order equations. Consider an elastic beam (say made of steel). We will study the transversal vibrations (vibrations where the beam “bends”) of the beam. Suppose the beam lies along the $x$-axis and let $\ky(x,t)$ measure the displacement of the point $x$ on the beam at time $t$. See Figure 5.2.

The equation that governs this setup is

$$ a^4 \kpfouryx + \kptwoyt = 0, $$

for some constant $a > 0$. Let us not worry about the physics.

Suppose the beam is of length 1 and simply supported (hinged) at the ends. The beam is displaced by some function $\kf(x)$ at time $t=0$ and then let go (initial velocity is 0). Then $\ky$ satisfies:

$$ \begin{aligned} & a^4 \kyxxxx + \kytt = 0 \qquad (0 < x < 1, \enspace t > 0), \\ & \ky(0,t) = \kyxx(0,t) = 0 , \\ & \ky(1,t) = \kyxx(1,t) = 0 , \\ & \ky(x,0) = \kf(x), \qquad \kyt(x,0) = 0 . \end{aligned} \tag{5.5} $$

Again we try $\ky(x,t) = \kX(x)\kT(t)$ and plug in to get $a^4 \kXfour\kT + \kX\kTdprime = 0$ or

$$ \frac{\kXfour}{\kX} = \frac{- \kTdprime}{a^4\kT} = \klambda . $$

The equations are

$$ \kTdprime + \klambda a^4 \kT = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \kXfour - \klambda \kX = 0 . $$

The boundary conditions $\ky(0,t) = \kyxx(0,t) = 0$ and $\ky(1,t) = \kyxx(1,t) = 0$ imply

$$ \kX(0) = \kXdprime(0) = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \kX(1) = \kXdprime(1) = 0 . $$

The initial homogeneous condition $\kyt(x,0) = 0$ implies

$$ \kTprime(0) = 0 . $$

As usual, we leave the nonhomogeneous $\ky(x,0) = \kf(x)$ for later.

Considering the equation for $\kT$, that is, $\kTdprime + \klambda a^4 \kT = 0$, physical intuition leads us to the fact that if $\klambda$ is an eigenvalue then $\klambda > 0$: We expect vibration and not exponential growth or decay in the $t$ direction (there is no friction in our model for instance). So there are no negative eigenvalues. Similarly, $\klambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue.

The eigenvalues and the series solution

Let $\komega = \sqrt[4]{\klambda}$, that is, $\komega^4 = \klambda$, to avoid writing the fourth root all the time. Notice $\komega > 0$. The general solution to $\kXfour - \komega^4 \kX = 0$ is

$$ \kX(x) = A e^{\komega x} + B e^{-\komega x} + C \sin (\komega x) + D \cos (\komega x) . $$

Now $0 = \kX(0) = A+B+D$, $0 = \kXdprime(0) = \komega^2 (A + B - D)$. Solving, $D = 0$ and $B = -A$. So

$$ \kX(x) = A e^{\komega x} - A e^{-\komega x} + C \sin (\komega x) . $$

Also $0 = \kX(1) = A (e^{\komega} - e^{-\komega}) + C \sin \komega$, and $0 = \kXdprime(1) = A \komega^2 (e^{\komega} - e^{-\komega}) - C \komega^2 \sin \komega$. This means that $C \sin \komega = 0$ and $A (e^{\komega} - e^{-\komega}) = 2 A \sinh \komega = 0$. If $\komega > 0$, then $\sinh \komega \neq 0$ and so $A = 0$. Thus $C \neq 0$, otherwise $\klambda$ is not an eigenvalue. Also, $\komega$ must be an integer multiple of $\pi$. Hence $\komega = n \pi$ and $n \geq 1$ (as $\komega > 0$). We can take $C=1$. So the eigenvalues are $\klambdan = n^4 \pi^4$, and corresponding eigenfunctions are $\sin (n \pi x)$.

Now $\kTdprime + n^4 \pi^4 a^4 \kT = 0$. The general solution is $\kT(t) = A \sin (n^2 \pi^2 a^2 t) + B \cos (n^2 \pi^2 a^2 t)$. But $\kTprime(0) = 0$ and hence $A=0$. We take $B=1$ to make $\kT(0) = 1$ for convenience. So our solutions are $\kTn(t) = \cos (n^2 \pi^2 a^2 t)$.

The eigenfunctions are just the sines, so we decompose the function $\kf(x)$ using the sine series. That is, we find numbers $\kbn$ such that for $0 < x < 1$,

$$ \kf(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kbn \sin (n \pi x) . $$

Then the solution to (5.5) is

$$ \ky(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kbn \kXn(x) \kTn(t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kbn \sin (n \pi x) \cos ( n^2 \pi^2 a^2 t ) . $$

The point is that $\kXn\kTn$ is a solution that satisfies all the homogeneous conditions (all conditions except the initial position). And since $\kTn(0) = 1$,

$$ \ky(x,0) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kbn \kXn(x) \kTn(0) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kbn \kXn(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kbn \sin (n \pi x) = \kf(x) . $$

So $\ky(x,t)$ solves (5.5).

The natural (angular) frequencies of the system are $n^2 \pi^2 a^2$. These frequencies are all integer multiples of the fundamental frequency $\pi^2 a^2$, so we get a nice musical note. The exact frequencies and their amplitudes are what musicians call the timbre of the note (outside of music it is called the spectrum).

The timbre of a simply supported beam is different from that of a vibrating string, where we get “more” of the lower frequencies since we get all integer multiples, $1,2,3,4,5,\ldots$. For a simply supported beam, we get only the square multiples $1,4,9,16,25,\ldots$, and so we get something much closer to a pure sound. The sound of a xylophone or vibraphone is, therefore, very different from that of a guitar or piano.

Example 5.2.1

Consider $\kf(x) = \frac{x(x-1)}{10}$. On $0 < x < 1$ (you know how to do this by now)

$$ \kf(x) = \sum_{\substack{n=1\\n \text{~odd}}}^\infty \frac{-4}{5\pi^3 n^3} \sin (n \pi x) . $$

Hence, the solution to (5.5) with the given initial position $\kf(x)$ is

$$ \ky(x,t) = \sum_{\substack{n=1\\n \text{~odd}}}^\infty \frac{-4}{5\pi^3 n^3} \sin (n \pi x) \cos ( n^2 \pi^2 a^2 t ) . $$

Other end conditions

There are other boundary conditions than just hinged ends. There are three basic possibilities: hinged, free, or fixed. Consider the end at $x=0$. For the other end, it is the same idea. If the end is hinged, then

$$ \ky(0,t) = \kyxx(0,t) = 0 . $$

If the end is free, that is, it is just floating in air, then

$$ \kyxx(0,t) = \kyxxx(0,t) = 0 . $$

And finally, if the end is clamped or fixed, for example it is welded to a wall, then

$$ \ky(0,t) = \kyx(0,t) = 0 . $$
