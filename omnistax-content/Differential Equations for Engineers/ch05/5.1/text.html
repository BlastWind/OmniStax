Boundary value problems

In chapter 4, we encountered several different eigenvalue problems such as:

$$ \kXdprime(x) + \klambda \kX(x) = 0 , $$

with different boundary conditions

$$ \begin{array}{rrl} \kX(0) = 0 & ~~\kX(L) = 0 & ~~\text{(Dirichlet), or} \\ \kXprime(0) = 0 & ~~\kXprime(L) = 0 & ~~\text{(Neumann), or} \\ \kXprime(0) = 0 & ~~\kX(L) = 0 & ~~\text{(Mixed), or} \\ \kX(0) = 0 & ~~\kXprime(L) = 0 & ~~\text{(Mixed)}, \ldots \end{array} $$

These boundary problems came up in the study of the heat equation $\kut = k \kuxx$ when we were trying to solve the equation by the method of separation of variables in §4.6. Dirichlet conditions correspond to applying a zero temperature at the ends, Neumann means insulating the ends, etc. Other types of endpoint conditions also arise naturally, such as the Robin boundary conditions

$$ h\kX(0) - \kXprime(0) = 0, \qquad h\kX(L) + \kXprime(L) = 0 , $$

for some constant $h$. These conditions come up when the ends are immersed in some medium.

In the separation of variables computation we encountered an eigenvalue problem and found the eigenfunctions $\kXn(x)$. We then found the eigenfunction decomposition of the initial temperature $\kf(x) = \ku(x,0)$,

$$ \kf(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kcn \kXn(x) . $$

Once we had this decomposition and found suitable $\kTn(t)$ such that $\kTn(0) = 1$ and such that $\kTn(t)\kXn(x)$ were solutions to the heat equation, we wrote the solution to the original problem, including the initial condition, as

$$ \ku(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kcn \kTn(t) \kXn(x) . $$

To study more general problems with this method, we must study more general eigenvalue problems. First, we study second-order linear equations of the form

$$ \frac{d}{dx} \left( \kp(x) \kdydx \right) - \kq(x) \ky + \klambda \kr(x) \ky = 0 . \tag{5.1}$$

Essentially any second-order linear equation of the form $a(x) \kydprime + b(x) \kyprime + c(x) \ky + \klambda d(x) \ky = 0$ can be written as (5.1) after multiplying by a proper factor.

Example 5.1.1 · Bessel

Put the following equation into the form (5.1):

$$ x^2 \kydprime + x\kyprime + \left(\klambda x^2 - n^2\right)\ky = 0 . $$

Multiply both sides by $\frac{1}{x}$ to obtain

$$ \begin{split} \frac{1}{x} \left( x^2 \kydprime + x\kyprime + \left(\klambda x^2 - n^2\right)\ky \right) & = x \kydprime + \kyprime + \left(\klambda x - \frac{n^2}{x}\right)\ky \\ & = \frac{d}{dx} \left( x \kdydx \right) - \frac{n^2}{x} \ky + \klambda x \ky = 0. \end{split} $$

The Bessel equation turns up for example in the solution of the two-dimensional wave equation. If you want to see how one solves the equation, you can look at subsection 7.3.3.

The so-called Sturm–Liouville problem is to seek nontrivial solutions to

$$ \boxed{~~ \begin{aligned} &\frac{d}{dx} \left( \kp(x) \kdydx \right) - \kq(x) \ky + \klambda \kr(x) \ky = 0, \qquad a < x < b, \\ &\alpha_1 \ky(a) - \alpha_2 \kyprime(a) = 0, \\ &\beta_1 \ky(b) + \beta_2 \kyprime(b) = 0. \end{aligned} ~~} \tag{5.2}$$

By nontrivial, we again mean a $\ky$ that is not simply the constant zero (which is always a solution). In particular, we ask which $\klambda$s allow such nontrivial solutions. The $\klambda$s that admit nontrivial solutions are called the eigenvalues and the corresponding nontrivial solutions are called eigenfunctions. The constants $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ should not both be zero; the same applies to $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$.

Theorem 5.1.1

Suppose $\kp(x)$, $\kp'(x)$, $\kq(x)$ and $\kr(x)$ are continuous on $[a,b]$ and suppose $\kp(x) > 0$ and $\kr(x) > 0$ for all $x$ in $[a,b]$. Then the Sturm–Liouville problem (5.2) has an increasing sequence of eigenvalues

$$ \klambda_1 < \klambda_2 < \klambda_3 < \cdots $$

such that

$$ \lim_{n \to \infty} \klambdan = +\infty $$

and such that to each $\klambdan$ there is (up to a constant multiple) a single eigenfunction $\kyn(x)$.

Moreover, if $\kq(x) \geq 0$ and $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \beta_1, \beta_2 \geq 0$, then $\klambdan \geq 0$ for all $n$.

Problems satisfying the hypothesis of the theorem (including the “Moreover”) are called regular Sturm–Liouville problems, and we will only consider such problems here. That is, a regular problem is one where $\kp(x)$, $\kp'(x)$, $\kq(x)$ and $\kr(x)$ are continuous, $\kp(x) > 0$, $\kr(x) > 0$, $\kq(x) \geq 0$, and $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \beta_1, \beta_2 \geq 0$, where neither $\alpha_1=\alpha_2=0$ nor $\beta_1=\beta_2=0$. Note: Be careful about the signs. Also be careful about the inequalities for $\kr$ and $\kp$. They must be strict for all $x$ in the interval $[a,b]$, including the endpoints!

When zero is an eigenvalue, we usually start labeling the eigenvalues from 0 rather than from 1 for convenience. That is, we label the eigenvalues $\klambda_0 < \klambda_1 < \klambda_2 < \cdots$.

Example 5.1.2

The problem $\kydprime+\klambda \ky=0$, $0 < x < L$, $\ky(0) = 0$, and $\ky(L) = 0$ is a regular Sturm–Liouville problem: $\kp(x) = 1$, $\kq(x) = 0$, $\kr(x) = 1$, and we have $\kp(x) = 1 > 0$ and $\kr(x) = 1 > 0$. We also have $a=0$, $b=L$, $\alpha_1 = \beta_1 = 1$, $\alpha_2 = \beta_2 = 0$. The eigenvalues are $\klambdan = \frac{n^2 \pi^2}{L^2}$ and eigenfunctions are $\kyn(x) = \sin\bigl(\frac{n\pi}{L} x\bigr)$. All eigenvalues are nonnegative as predicted by the theorem.

Example 5.1.3

Find eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the problem

$$ \begin{align*} & \kydprime+\klambda \ky = 0, \quad 0 < x < 1 , \\ & h\ky(0)- \kyprime(0) = 0, \quad \kyprime(1) = 0, \quad h > 0. \end{align*} $$

These equations give a regular Sturm–Liouville problem.

By Theorem 5.1.1, $\klambda \geq 0$. So the general solution (without boundary conditions) is

$$ \begin{aligned} & \ky(x) = A \cos \bigl( \sqrt{\klambda}\, x\bigr) + B \sin \bigl( \sqrt{\klambda}\, x\bigr) & & \qquad \text{if } \; \klambda > 0 , \\ & \ky(x) = A x + B & & \qquad \text{if } \; \klambda = 0 . \end{aligned} $$

Let us see if $\klambda = 0$ is an eigenvalue: We must satisfy $0 = hB - A$ and $A = 0$, hence $B=0$ (as $h > 0$). Therefore, 0 is not an eigenvalue (no nonzero solution, so no eigenfunction).

Orthogonality

We have seen the notion of orthogonality before. For example, we have shown that $\sin (nx)$ are orthogonal for distinct $n$ on $[0,\pi]$. For general Sturm–Liouville problems we need a more general setup. Let $\kr(x)$ be a weight function (any function, though generally we assume it is positive) on $[a,b]$. Two functions $\kf(x)$ and $\kg(x)$ are said to be orthogonal with respect to the weight function $\kr(x)$ when

$$ \int_a^b \kf(x) \, \kg(x) \, \kr(x) \,dx = 0 . $$

In this setting, we define the inner product as

$$ \kinner \overset{\text{def}}{=} \int_a^b \kf(x) \, \kg(x) \, \kr(x) \,dx , $$

and then say $\kf$ and $\kg$ are orthogonal whenever $\kinner = 0$. The results and concepts are again analogous to finite-dimensional linear algebra.

The idea of the given inner product is that those $x$ where $\kr(x)$ is greater have more weight. Nontrivial (nonconstant) $\kr(x)$ arise naturally, for example from a change of variables. Hence, you could think of a change of variables such that $d\xi = \kr(x)\, dx$.

Eigenfunctions of a regular Sturm–Liouville problem satisfy an orthogonality property, just like the eigenfunctions in §4.1. Its proof is very similar to that of the analogous Theorem 4.1.1.

Theorem 5.1.2

Suppose we have a regular Sturm–Liouville problem

$$ \begin{align*} &\frac{d}{dx} \left( \kp(x) \kdydx \right) - \kq(x) \ky + \klambda \kr(x) \ky = 0 , \\ &\alpha_1 \ky(a) - \alpha_2 \kyprime(a) = 0 , \\ &\beta_1 \ky(b) + \beta_2 \kyprime(b) = 0 . \end{align*} $$

Let $\ky_j$ and $\ky_k$ be two distinct eigenfunctions for two distinct eigenvalues $\klambda_j$ and $\klambda_k$. Then

$$ \int_a^b \ky_j(x) \, \ky_k(x) \, \kr(x) \,dx = 0, $$

that is, $\ky_j$ and $\ky_k$ are orthogonal with respect to the weight function $\kr$.

Fredholm alternative

The Fredholm alternative theorem we talked about before (Theorem 4.1.2) holds for all regular Sturm–Liouville problems. We state it here for completeness.

Theorem 5.1.3

Fredholm alternative

Suppose that we have a regular Sturm–Liouville problem. Then either

$$ \begin{align*} &\frac{d}{dx} \left( \kp(x) \kdydx \right) - \kq(x) \ky + \klambda \kr(x) \ky = 0 , \\ &\alpha_1 \ky(a) - \alpha_2 \kyprime(a) = 0 , \\ &\beta_1 \ky(b) + \beta_2 \kyprime(b) = 0 , \end{align*} $$

has a nonzero solution ($\klambda$ is an eigenvalue), or

$$ \begin{align*} &\frac{d}{dx} \left( \kp(x) \kdydx \right) - \kq(x) \ky + \klambda \kr(x) \ky = \kf(x) , \\ &\alpha_1 \ky(a) - \alpha_2 \kyprime(a) = 0 , \\ &\beta_1 \ky(b) + \beta_2 \kyprime(b) = 0 , \end{align*} $$

has a unique solution for any $\kf(x)$ continuous on $[a,b]$.

This theorem is used in much the same way as we did before in §4.4. It is used when solving more general nonhomogeneous boundary value problems. The theorem does not help us solve the problem. It tells us when a unique solution exists, so that we know when to spend time looking for it. To solve the problem, we decompose $\kf(x)$ and $\ky(x)$ in terms of eigenfunctions of the homogeneous problem, and then solve for the coefficients of the series for $\ky(x)$.

Eigenfunction series

What we want to do with the eigenfunctions once we have them is to compute the eigenfunction decomposition of an arbitrary function $\kf(x)$. That is, we wish to write

$$ \kf(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kcn \kyn(x) , \tag{5.3}$$

where $\kyn(x)$ are eigenfunctions. We wish to find out if we can represent any function $\kf(x)$ in this way, and if so, we wish to calculate the $\kcn$ (and of course we would want to know if the sum converges). OK, so imagine we could write $\kf(x)$ as (5.3). We will assume convergence and the ability to integrate the series term by term. Because of orthogonality we have

Hence,

$$ \boxed{~~ \kc_m = \frac{\langle \kf , \ky_m \rangle}{\langle \ky_m , \ky_m \rangle} = \frac{\int_a^b \kf(x) \, \ky_m (x)\, \kr(x) \, dx} {\int_a^b {\bigl(\ky_m(x)\bigr)}^2 \, \kr(x) \,dx} . ~~} \tag{5.4}$$

Note that $\ky_m$ are known up to a constant multiple, so we could have picked a scalar multiple of an eigenfunction such that $\langle \ky_m , \ky_m \rangle = 1$ (if we had an arbitrary eigenfunction $\tilde{y}_m$, divide it by $\sqrt{\langle \tilde{y}_m , \tilde{y}_m \rangle}$). When $\langle \ky_m , \ky_m \rangle = 1$, we have the simpler form $\kc_m = \langle \kf, \ky_m \rangle$. The following theorem holds more generally, but the statement given is enough for our purposes.

Theorem 5.1.4

Suppose $\kf$ is a piecewise smooth continuous function on $[a,b]$. If $\ky_1, \ky_2, \ldots$ are eigenfunctions of a regular Sturm–Liouville problem, one for each eigenvalue, then there exist real constants $\kc_1, \kc_2, \ldots$ given by (5.4) such that (5.3) converges and holds for $a < x < b$.

Example 5.1.4

Consider

$$ \begin{align*} & \kydprime + \klambda \ky = 0, \quad 0 < x < {\pi}/{2} , \\ & \ky(0) =0, \quad \kyprime({\pi}/{2}) = 0 . \end{align*} $$

The above is a regular Sturm–Liouville problem, and Theorem 5.1.1 says that if $\klambda$ is an eigenvalue then $\klambda \geq 0$.

Suppose $\klambda = 0$. The general solution is $\ky(x) = Ax + B$. We plug in the initial conditions to get $0=\ky(0) = B$, and $0 = \kyprime({\pi}/{2}) = A$. Hence $\klambda = 0$ is not an eigenvalue.

So let us consider $\klambda > 0$, where the general solution is

$$ \ky(x) = A \cos \bigl( \sqrt{\klambda} \, x \bigr) + B \sin \bigl( \sqrt{\klambda} \, x \bigr) . $$

Plugging in the boundary conditions we get $0 = \ky(0) = A$ and $0 = \kyprime({\pi}/{2}) = \sqrt{\klambda} \, B \cos \bigl(\sqrt{\klambda} \, \frac{\pi}{2}\bigr)$. Since $A$ is zero, $B$ cannot be zero. Hence $\cos \bigl( \sqrt{\klambda} \, \frac{\pi}{2}\bigr) = 0$. This means that $\sqrt{\klambda} \,\frac{\pi}{2}$ is an odd integral multiple of ${\pi}/{2}$, i.e. $(2n-1)\frac{\pi}{2} = \sqrt{\klambdan} \,\frac{\pi}{2}$. Solving for $\klambdan$ we get

$$ \klambdan = {(2n-1)}^2 . $$

We can take $B = 1$. Our eigenfunctions are

$$ \kyn(x) = \sin \bigl( (2n-1)x \bigr) . $$

A little bit of calculus shows

$$ \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} {\Bigl( \sin \bigl( (2n-1)x \bigr) \Bigr)}^2 \, dx = \frac{\pi}{4} . $$

So any piecewise smooth function $\kf(x)$ on $[0,{\pi}/{2}]$ can be written as

$$ \kf(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kcn \sin \bigl( (2n-1)x \bigr) , $$

where

$$ \kcn = \frac{\langle \kf , \kyn \rangle}{\langle \kyn , \kyn \rangle} = \frac{\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \kf(x) \, \sin \bigl( (2n-1)x \bigr) \, dx }{\int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} {\Bigl(\sin \bigl((2n-1)x\bigr)\Bigr)}^2 \, dx} = \frac{4}{\pi} \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \kf(x) \,\sin \bigl( (2n-1)x \bigr) \, dx . $$

Note that the series converges to an odd 2π-periodic extension of $\kf(x)$. With the regular sine series, we would expect a function with period $2 \, \frac{\pi}{2} = \pi$.

Let us compute an example. Consider $\kf(x) = x$ for $0 < x < {\pi}/{2}$. Some calculus later we find

$$ \kcn = \frac{4}{\pi} \int_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \kf(x) \,\sin \bigl( (2n-1)x \bigr) \, dx = \frac{4{(-1)}^{n+1}}{\pi {(2n-1)}^2} , $$

and so for $x$ in $[0,{\pi}/{2}]$,

$$ \kf(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{4{(-1)}^{n+1}}{\pi {(2n-1)}^2} \sin \bigl( (2n-1)x \bigr) . $$

This is different from the π-periodic regular sine series which can be computed to be

$$ \kf(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{{(-1)}^{n+1}}{n} \sin ( 2nx ) . $$

Both series converge to $\kf(x)$ for $0 < x < {\pi}/{2}$, but the eigenfunctions involved come from different eigenvalue problems.
