Equilibria and stability

Consider problems of the form

$$ \kdxdt = \kf(\kx) , $$

where the derivative of solutions depends only on $\kx$ (the dependent variable). Such equations are called autonomous equations. If we think of $t$ as time, the naming comes from the fact that the equation is independent of time.

We return to the cooling coffee problem (Example 1.3.3). Newton’s law of cooling says

$$ \kdxdt = k (A-\kx) , $$

where $\kx$ is the temperature, $t$ is time, $k$ is some positive constant, and $A$ is the ambient temperature. See Figure 1.11 for an example with $k=0.3$ and $A=5$.

Note the solution $\kx=A$ (in the figure $\kx=5$). We call these constant solutions the equilibrium solutions. The points on the $\kx$-axis where $\kf(\kx) = 0$ are called critical points or equilibria. The point $\kx=A$ is a critical point. In fact, each critical point corresponds to an equilibrium solution. Note also, by looking at the graph, that the solution $\kx=A$ is “stable” in that small perturbations in $\kx$ do not lead to substantially different solutions as $t$ grows. If we change the initial condition a little bit, then as $t \to \infty$, we still get $\kx(t) \to A$. We call such a critical point stable. In this simple example, all solutions in fact go to $A$ as $t \to \infty$. If a critical point is not stable, we say it is unstable.

The logistic equation and the phase diagram

Consider now the logistic equation

$$ \kdxdt = k\kx(M-\kx) , $$

for some positive $k$ and $M$. This equation is commonly used to model population if we know the limiting population $M$, that is, the maximum sustainable population. The logistic equation leads to less catastrophic predictions about world population than $\kxprime=k\kx$. In the real world, there is no such thing as negative population, but we will still consider negative $\kx$ for the purposes of the math.

See Figure 1.12 for an example, $\kxprime = 0.1 \kx(5-\kx)$. There are two critical points, $\kx=0$ and $\kx=5$. The critical point at $\kx=5$ is stable, while the critical point at $\kx=0$ is unstable. It is not necessary to find the exact solutions to understand their long-term behavior, that is, behavior as time goes to infinity. From the slope field above of $\kxprime = 0.1 \kx(5-\kx)$, we see that

$$ \lim_{t\to \infty} \kx(t) = \begin{cases} 5 & \text{if } \; \kx(0) > 0 , \\ 0 & \text{if } \; \kx(0) = 0 , \\ \text{DNE or } {-\infty} & \text{if } \; \kx(0) < 0 . \\ \end{cases} $$

Here DNE means “does not exist.” From just looking at the slope field, we cannot quite decide what happens if $\kx(0) < 0$. It could be that the solution does not exist for $t$ all the way to $\infty$. Think of the equation $\kxprime = \kx^2$. We have seen that solutions only exist for some finite period of time. Same can happen here. In our example equation above it turns out that the solution does not exist for all time, but to see that we would have to solve the equation. In any case, the solution does go to $-\infty$, but it may get there rather quickly.

If we are interested only in the long-term behavior of the solution, we would be doing unnecessary work if we solved the equation exactly. We could draw the slope field, but it is easier to just look at the phase diagram or phase portrait, which is a simple way to visualize the behavior of autonomous equations. In this case there is one dependent variable, the $\kx$. We draw the $\kx$-axis, we mark all the critical points, and then we draw arrows in between. Since $\kx$ is the dependent variable, we draw the axis vertically, as it appears in the slope field diagrams above. If $\kf(\kx) > 0$, we draw an up arrow. If $\kf(\kx) < 0$, we draw a down arrow. To figure out the sign, we could just plug in some $\kx$ between the critical points because $\kf(\kx)$ will have the same sign at all $\kx$ between two critical points as long as $\kf(\kx)$ is continuous. For example, $\kf(6) = -0.6 < 0$, so $\kf(\kx) < 0$ for $\kx > 5$, and the arrow above $\kx=5$ is a down arrow. Next, $\kf(1) = 0.4 > 0$, so $\kf(\kx) > 0$ whenever $0 < \kx < 5$, and the arrow points up. Finally, $\kf(-1) = -0.6 < 0$, so $\kf(\kx) < 0$ when $\kx < 0$, and the arrow points down.

Armed with the phase diagram, it is easy to sketch the solutions approximately: As time $t$ moves from left to right, the graph of a solution goes up if the arrow is up, and it goes down if the arrow is down.

Harvesting

Let us think about the logistic equation with harvesting. Suppose an alien race really likes to eat humans. They keep a planet with humans and harvest the humans at a rate of $h$ million humans per year. Suppose $\kx$ is the number of humans in millions on the planet and $t$ is time in years. Let $M$ be the limiting population when no harvesting is done. The number $k > 0$ is a constant depending on how fast humans multiply. Our equation becomes

$$ \kdxdt = k\kx(M-\kx) - h . $$

We expand the right-hand side and set it to zero.

$$ k\kx(M-\kx) - h = -k\kx^2+kM\kx - h = 0. $$

Solving for the critical points, let us call them $A$ and $B$, we get

$$ A = \frac{kM + \sqrt{{(kM)}^2 - 4hk}}{2k}, \qquad B = \frac{kM - \sqrt{{(kM)}^2 - 4hk}}{2k} . $$

For example, let $M=8$ and $k=0.1$. When $h=1$, then $A$ and $B$ are distinct and positive. See Figure 1.13 for the slope field. As long as the population starts above $B$, which is approximately 1.55 million, then the population will not die out, it will tend towards $A \approx 6.45$ million. If ever a catastrophe happens and the population drops below $B$, humans will die out, and the fast food restaurant serving them will go out of business.

When $h = 1.6$, then $A=B=4$. There is only one critical point and it is unstable. When the population starts above 4 million, it will tend towards 4 million. However, if it ever drops below 4 million, perhaps due to a worse than normal hurricane season one year, then humans will die out on the planet. This scenario is not one that we (as the human fast food proprietor) want to be in. A small perturbation of the equilibrium state and we are out of business. There is no room for error. See Figure 1.14.

Finally, if we are harvesting at 2 million humans per year, there are no critical points. The population will always plummet towards zero, no matter how well stocked the planet starts. See Figure 1.15.
