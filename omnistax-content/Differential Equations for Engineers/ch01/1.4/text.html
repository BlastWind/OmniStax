The integrating factor

Linear equations are one of the most important types of equations we will learn to solve. In fact, the majority of the course is about linear equations. In this section, we focus on the first-order linear equation. A first-order equation is linear if we can put it into the form:

$$ \kyprime + \kp(x) \ky = \kf(x) . \tag{1.3}$$

The word “linear” means linear in $\ky$ and $\kyprime$: No higher powers or functions of $\ky$ or $\kyprime$ appear. The dependence on $x$ can be more complicated.

Solutions of linear equations have nice properties. For example, the solution exists wherever $\kp(x)$ and $\kf(x)$ are continuous, and it has essentially the same regularity (read: it is just as nice). Most importantly for us right now, there is a method for solving linear first-order equations.

The trick is to rewrite the left-hand side of (1.3) as a derivative of a product of $\ky$ with another function. To this end, we wish to find a function $\kr(x)$ such that

$$ \kr(x) \kyprime + \kr(x) \kp(x) \ky = \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[ \kr(x) \ky \Bigr] . $$

This is the left-hand side of (1.3) multiplied by $\kr(x)$. If we multiply (1.3) by $\kr(x)$, we obtain

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\Bigl[ \kr(x) \ky \Bigr] = \kr(x)\kf(x) . $$

We can now integrate both sides, which we can do as the right-hand side does not depend on $\ky$ and the left-hand side is written as a derivative of a function. After the integration, we solve for $\ky$ by dividing by $\kr(x)$. The function $\kr(x)$ is called the integrating factor and the method is called the integrating factor method.

We are looking for a function $\kr(x)$, such that if we differentiate it, we get the same function back multiplied by $\kp(x)$. That seems like a job for the exponential function! Let

$$ \kr(x) = e^{\int \kp(x) \,dx} , $$

where $\int \kp(x)\, dx$ is any antiderivative of $\kp(x)$. We compute:

Of course, to get a closed-form formula for $\ky$, we need to be able to find a closed-form formula for the integrals appearing above.

Example 1.4.1

Solve

$$ \kyprime + 2x\ky = e^{x-x^2}, \qquad \ky(0) = -1 . $$

First note that $\kp(x) = 2x$ and $\kf(x) = e^{x-x^2}$. The integrating factor is $\kr(x) = e^{\int \kp(x)\, dx} = e^{x^2}$. We multiply both sides of the equation by $\kr(x)$ to get

$$ \begin{aligned} e^{x^2} \kyprime + 2xe^{x^2}\ky & = e^{x^2} e^{x-x^2} , \\ \frac{d}{dx} \left[ e^{x^2} \ky \right] &= e^x . \end{aligned} $$

We integrate

$$ \begin{aligned} e^{x^2} \ky &= e^x +C , \\ \ky &= e^{x-x^2} + C e^{-x^2} . \end{aligned} $$

Next, we solve for the initial condition $-1 = \ky(0) = 1 + C$, so $C=-2$. The solution is

$$ \ky = e^{x-x^2} - 2 e^{-x^2} . $$

Note that we do not care which antiderivative we take when computing $e^{\int \kp(x) \, dx}$. You can always add a constant of integration, but those constants will not matter in the end.

Advice: Do not try to remember the formula for $\ky$ itself—that is way too hard. It is easier to remember the process and repeat it.

Since we cannot always evaluate the integrals in closed form, it is useful to know how to write the solution in definite integral form. A definite integral is something that you can plug into a computer or a calculator. Suppose we are given

$$ \kyprime + \kp(x) \ky = \kf(x) , \qquad \ky(x_0) = y_0 . $$

Look at the solution and write the integrals as definite integrals.

$$ \boxed{~~ \ky(x) = e^{-\int_{x_0}^x \kp(s)\, ds} \left( \int_{x_0}^x e^{\int_{x_0}^t \kp(s)\, ds} \kf(t) \,dt + y_0 \right). ~~} \tag{1.4}$$

You should be careful to properly use dummy variables here. If you now plug such a formula into a computer or a calculator, it will be happy to give you numerical answers.

Remark 1.4.1

Before we move on, we should note some interesting properties of linear equations. First, for the linear initial value problem $\kyprime + \kp(x) \ky = \kf(x)$, $\ky(x_0) = y_0$, there is an explicit formula (1.4) for the solution. Second, it follows from the formula (1.4) that if $\kp(x)$ and $\kf(x)$ are continuous on some interval $(a,b)$, then the solution $\ky(x)$ exists and is differentiable on $(a,b)$. Compare with the simple nonlinear example we have seen previously, $\kyprime=\ky^2$, and compare to Theorem 1.2.1.

Mixing problems

Example 1.4.2

We get to a common simple application of linear equations. Real-life applications of this type of problem include computing the concentration of chemicals in bodies of water (rivers and lakes).

A 100-liter tank contains 10 kilograms of salt dissolved in 60 liters of water. A solution of water and salt (brine) with a concentration of 0.1 kilograms per liter is flowing in at the rate of 5 liters a minute. The solution in the tank is well stirred and flows out at a rate of 3 liters a minute. How much salt is in the tank when the tank is full?

To solve this problem, we need to find the differential equation for this setup. Let $\kx$ denote the kilograms of salt in the tank, and let $t$ denote the time in minutes. For a small change $\Delta t$ in time, the change in $\kx$ (denoted $\Delta x$) is approximately

$$ \Delta x \approx (\text{rate in} \times \text{concentration in}) \Delta t - (\text{rate out} \times \text{concentration out}) \Delta t . $$

Dividing through by $\Delta t$ and taking the limit $\Delta t \to 0$, we see that

$$ \kdxdt = (\text{rate in} \times \text{concentration in}) - (\text{rate out} \times \text{concentration out}) . $$

In our example,

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{rate in} &= 5 , \\ \text{concentration in} &= 0.1 , \\ \text{rate out} &= 3 , \\ \text{volume} &= \text{initial volume} + (\text{rate in} - \text{rate out})t = 60+(5-3)t, \\ \text{concentration out} &= \frac{\kx}{\text{volume}} = \frac{\kx}{60+(5-3)t} . \end{aligned} $$

Our differential equation is

$$ \kdxdt = (5 \times 0.1) - \left(3 \frac{\kx}{60+2t}\right) . $$

In the form (1.3), it is

$$ \kdxdt + \frac{3}{60+2t} \kx = 0.5 . $$

Let us solve. The integrating factor is

$$ \kr(t) = \exp \left( \int \frac{3}{60+2t} dt \right) = \exp \left( \frac{3}{2} \ln (60+2t) \right) = {(60+2t)}^{3/2} . $$

We multiply both sides of the equation by $\kr(t)$ and simplify,

$$ \begin{aligned} {(60+2t)}^{3/2} \kdxdt + {(60+2t)}^{3/2} \frac{3}{60+2t} \kx & = 0.5{(60+2t)}^{3/2} ,\\ \frac{d}{dt}\left[ {(60+2t)}^{3/2} \kx \right] & = 0.5{(60+2t)}^{3/2} . \end{aligned} $$

We integrate and solve,

$$ \begin{aligned} {(60+2t)}^{3/2} \kx & = \int 0.5{(60+2t)}^{3/2} dt +C ,\\ \kx & = {(60+2t)}^{-3/2} \int \frac{ {(60+2t)}^{3/2} }{2} dt +C{(60+2t)}^{-3/2} ,\\ \kx & = {(60+2t)}^{-3/2} \frac{1}{10}{(60+2t)}^{5/2} +C{(60+2t)}^{-3/2} ,\\ \kx & = \frac{60+2t}{10} +C{(60+2t)}^{-3/2} . \end{aligned} $$

To find $C$, note that at $t=0$, we have $\kx=10$. That is,

$$ 10 = \kx(0) = \frac{60}{10} +C{(60)}^{-3/2} = 6 +C{(60)}^{-3/2} , $$

or

$$ C=4 ({60}^{3/2}) \approx 1859.03 . $$

As $5$ liters per minute are flowing in and $3$ liters per minute are flowing out, the volume is increasing by $2$ liters a minute. So the tank is full when $60+2t = 100$, or when $t=20$. We want the value of $\kx$ when the tank is full, that is, we compute $\kx(20)$:

$$ \begin{split} \kx(20) & = \frac{60+40}{10} +C{(60+40)}^{-3/2} \\ & \approx 10 +1859.03 {(100)}^{-3/2} \approx 11.86 . \end{split} $$

There are 11.86 kg of salt in the tank when it is full. See Figure 1.10 for the graph of $\kx(t)$. The concentration when the tank is full is approximately ${11.86}/{100} = 0.1186\,\mathrm{kg}/\mathrm{liter}$, and we started with ${1}/{6}$ or approximately 0.1667 kg/liter.
