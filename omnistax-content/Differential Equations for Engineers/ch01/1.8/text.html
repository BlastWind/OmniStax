Potential functions and exact equations

A type of equation that comes up quite often in physics and engineering is an exact equation. Suppose $\kF(x,y)$ is a function of two variables, which we call the potential function. The naming should suggest potential energy, or electric potential. Exact equations and potential functions appear when there is a conservation law at play, such as conservation of energy. Let us make up a simple example. Consider

$$ \kF(x,y) = x^2+y^2 . $$

We are interested in the lines of constant potential: curves given by $\kF(x,y) = C$, for some constant $C$. In our example, the curves $x^2+y^2=C$ are circles. See Figure 1.18.

We take the total derivative of $\kF$:

$$ \kdF = \frac{\partial \kF}{\partial x} dx + \frac{\partial \kF}{\partial y} dy . $$

For convenience, we will use the notation $\kFx = \frac{\partial \kF}{\partial x}$ and $\kFy = \frac{\partial \kF}{\partial y}$. In our example,

$$ \kdF = 2x \, dx + 2y \, dy . $$

If we apply the total derivative to the equation $\kF(x,y) = C$, we find the differential equation $\kdF = 0$. This differential equation has the form

$$ \kM \, dx + \kN \, dy = 0, \qquad \text{or} \qquad \kM + \kN \, \kdydx = 0 . $$

An equation of this form is called exact if it was obtained as $\kdF = 0$ for some potential function $\kF$. In our simple example, we obtain the equation

$$ 2x \, dx + 2y \, dy = 0, \qquad \text{or} \qquad 2x + 2y \, \kdydx = 0 . $$

Since we obtained this equation by differentiating $x^2+y^2=C$, the equation is exact. We often wish to solve for $y$ in terms of $x$. In our example,

$$ y = \pm \sqrt{C-x^2} . $$

In terms of multivariable calculus, at each point $(x,y)$ in the plane, $\kvecv = (\kM,\kN)$ is a vector, that is, a direction and a magnitude. As $\kM$ and $\kN$ are functions of $(x,y)$, we have a vector field. A vector field $\kvecv$ that comes from an exact equation is a so-called conservative vector field, that is, a vector field that comes with a potential function $\kF(x,y)$, such that

$$ \kvecv = \left( \frac{\partial \kF}{\partial x} ,\frac{\partial \kF}{\partial y} \right) . $$

Let $\gamma$ be a path in the plane starting at $(x_1,y_1)$ and ending at $(x_2,y_2)$. If we think of $\kvecv$ as force, then the work required to move along $\gamma$ is the path integral

$$ \int_\gamma \kvecv(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{r} = \int_\gamma \kM \, dx + \kN \, dy = \kF(x_2,y_2) - \kF(x_1,y_1) . $$

In other words, the work done depends only on the endpoints—where we start and where we end. For example, suppose $\kF$ is gravitational potential (physicists use the convention that $-\kF$ is the potential, but the idea is the same). The derivative of $\kF$ given by $\kvecv$ is the gravitational force. What we are saying is that the work required to move a heavy box from the ground floor to the roof only depends on the change in potential energy. The work done is the same no matter what path we took; if we took the stairs or the elevator. Although if we took the elevator, the elevator did the work for us. Movement along the curves $\kF(x,y) = C$ requires no work done, such as the heavy box sliding along without accelerating or braking on a perfectly flat roof on a cart with incredibly well-oiled wheels.

An exact equation is a conservative vector field, and the implicit solution of this equation is the potential function.

Solving exact equations

Now you, the reader, should ask: Where did we solve a differential equation? Well, in applications we generally know $\kM$ and $\kN$, but we do not know $\kF$. That is, we may have just started with $2x + 2y \kdydx = 0$, or perhaps even

$$ x + y \kdydx = 0 . $$

It is up to us to find some potential $\kF$ that works. Many different $\kF$ will work; adding a constant to $\kF$ does not change the equation. Once we have a potential function $\kF$, the equation $\kF\bigl(x,y(x)\bigr) = C$ gives an implicit solution of the ODE.

Example 1.8.1

Let us find the general solution to $2x + 2y \kdydx = 0$. Forget we know $\kF$.

If we know that this is an exact equation, we start looking for a potential function $\kF$. We have $\kM = 2x$ and $\kN=2y$. If $\kF$ exists, it must be such that $\kFx (x,y) = 2x$. Integrate in the $x$ variable to find

$$ \kF(x,y) = x^2 + A(y) , \tag{1.5}$$

for some function $A(y)$. The function $A$ is the “constant of integration,” though it is only constant as far as $x$ is concerned, and may still depend on $y$. Now differentiate (1.5) in $y$ and set it equal to $\kN$, which is what $\kFy$ is supposed to be:

$$ 2y = \kFy (x,y) = A'(y) . $$

Integrating, we find $A(y) = y^2$. We could add a constant of integration if we wanted to, but there is no need. We found $\kF(x,y) = x^2+y^2$. Next for a constant $C$, we solve

$$ \kF\bigl(x,y(x)\bigr) = C $$

for $y$ in terms of $x$. In this case, we obtain $y = \pm \sqrt{C-x^2}$ as we did before.

The procedure, once we know that the equation is exact, is:

  1. Integrate $\kFx = \kM$ in $x$ resulting in $\kF(x,y) = \text{something} + A(y)$.
  2. Differentiate this $\kF$ in $y$ and set that equal to $\kN$. Then find $A(y)$ by integration.

The procedure can also be done by first integrating in $y$ and then differentiating in $x$. Pretty easy, huh? Let’s try this again.

Example 1.8.2

Consider now $2x+y + xy \kdydx = 0$.

OK, so $\kM = 2x+y$ and $\kN=xy$. We try to proceed as before. Suppose $\kF$ exists. Then $\kFx (x,y) = 2x+y$. We integrate:

$$ \kF(x,y) = x^2 + xy + A(y) $$

for some function $A(y)$. Differentiate in $y$ and set equal to $\kN$:

$$ \kN = xy = \kFy (x,y) = x+A'(y) . $$

But there is no way to satisfy this requirement! The function $xy$ cannot be written as $x$ plus a function of $y$. The equation is not exact; no potential function $\kF$ exists.

Is there an easier way to check for exactness, other than failing in trying to find $\kF$? Yes there is. Suppose $\kM = \kFx$ and $\kN = \kFy$. As long as the second derivatives are continuous,

If $\kMy \neq \kNx$, the equation is not exact. For example, in Example 1.8.2, where $\kM = 2x + y$ and $\kN = xy$, the equation is not exact. Indeed, $\kMy = 1$ and $\kNx = y$ are not equal.

What is interesting is that this idea works in reverse too. If $\kMy=\kNx$, then a potential exists, at least near an initial point. This result is called the Poincaré Lemma.

Theorem 1.8.1

Poincaré Lemma

If $\kM$ and $\kN$ are continuously differentiable functions of $(x,y)$, and $\frac{\partial \kM}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial \kN}{\partial x}$, then near any point there is a function $\kF(x,y)$ such that $\kM = \frac{\partial \kF}{\partial x}$ and $\kN = \frac{\partial \kF}{\partial y}$.

Example 1.8.3

Solve

$$ \kdydx = \frac{-2x-y}{x-1}, \qquad y(0) = 1. $$

Write the equation as

$$ (2x+y) + (x-1)\kdydx = 0 , $$

so $\kM = 2x+y$, $\kN = x-1$, and

$$ \kMy = 1 = \kNx . $$

The Poincaré Lemma applies, so let us look for $\kF$. Integrating $\kM$ in $x$, we find

$$ \kF(x,y) = x^2+xy + A(y) . $$

Differentiating in $y$ and setting to $\kN$, we find

$$ x-1 = x + A'(y) . $$

So $A'(y) = -1$, and $A(y) = -y$ will work. We obtain $\kF(x,y) = x^2+xy-y$, so the implicit solution is $x^2+xy-y = C$. First we find $C$. As $y(0)=1$, we have $\kF(0,1) = C$. Therefore, $0^2+0\times 1 - 1 = C$, so $C=-1$. Now we solve $x^2+xy-y = -1$ for $y$ to get

$$ y = \frac{-x^2-1}{x-1} . $$

Example 1.8.4

Solve

$$ \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2} dx + \frac{x}{x^2+y^2} dy = 0 , \qquad y(1) = 2. $$

We leave to the reader to check that $\kMy = \kNx$, so the Poincaré Lemma applies.

This vector field $(\kM,\kN)$ is not conservative (the equation is not exact) if considered as a vector field of the entire plane minus the origin. The problem is that if the curve $\gamma$ is a circle around the origin, say starting at $(1,0)$ and ending at $(1,0)$ going counterclockwise, then if $\kF$ existed, we would expect

$$ 0 = \kF(1,0) - \kF(1,0) = \int_\gamma \kFx \, dx + \kFy \, dy = \int_\gamma \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2} \, dx + \frac{x}{x^2+y^2} \, dy = 2\pi . $$

That is nonsense! We leave the computation of the path integral to the interested reader, or you can consult your multivariable calculus textbook. So there is no potential function $\kF$ defined everywhere outside the origin $(0,0)$.

The Poincaré Lemma does not guarantee such an $\kF$ anyway. It only guarantees a potential function $\kF$ locally—only in some region containing the initial point. As $y(1) = 2$, we start at the point $(1,2)$. Considering $x > 0$ and integrating $\kM$ in $x$ or $\kN$ in $y$, we find

$$ \kF(x,y) = \arctan \bigl( {y}/{x} \bigr) . $$

This $\kF$ is defined on the region $x > 0$ and so the equation is exact on that region. The implicit solution is $\arctan \bigl( {y}/{x} \bigr) = C$. Solving, $y = \tan(C) x$. That is, the solution is a straight line. Solving $y(1) = 2$ gives us that $\tan(C) = 2$, and so $y= 2x$ is the desired solution. See Figure 1.19, and note again that the solution only exists for $x > 0$.

If the region where $\kM$ and $\kN$ are defined and where $\kMy=\kNx$ has “no holes,” such as, for instance, the entire $xy$-plane, then the equation is exact and an $\kF$ defined on that region can be found. For example, in Example 1.8.3, $\kM=2x+y$ and $\kN=x-1$ are defined in the entire plane and $\kMy = 1 = \kNx$, so a global $\kF$ exists there. Similarly, in Example 1.8.4 the half-plane where $x > 0$ also has “no holes” and a potential function exists on that region. So in such cases, checking $\kMy = \kNx$ is good enough to check for exactness.

Example 1.8.5

Solve

$$ x^2+y^2 + 2y(x+1) \kdydx = 0 . $$

The reader should check that this equation is exact. Let $\kM= x^2+y^2$ and $\kN=2y(x+1)$. We follow the procedure for exact equations

$$ \kF(x,y) = \frac{1}{3}x^3 + xy^2 + A(y) , $$

and

$$ 2y(x+1) = 2xy + A'(y) . $$

Therefore $A'(y) = 2y$ or $A(y) = y^2$ and $\kF(x,y) = \frac{1}{3}x^3 + xy^2 + y^2$. We try to solve $\kF(x,y) = C$. We easily solve for $y^2$ and then just take the square root:

$$ y^2 = \frac{C-({1}/{3})x^3}{x+1}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad y = \pm \sqrt{\frac{C-({1}/{3})x^3}{x+1}} . $$

When $x=-1$, the term in front of $\kdydx$ is zero, and our explicit solution is not valid. The given equation has no solution (for $y(x)$) near $x=-1$, but the equation $(x^2+y^2) \, dx + 2y(x+1) \, dy = 0$ does have a solution $x=-1$. In fact, one could solve for $x$ in terms of $y$ for any initial condition. The solution is messy, so we leave it as $\frac{1}{3}x^3 + xy^2 + y^2 = C$.

Integrating factors

Sometimes an equation $\kM\, dx + \kN \, dy = 0$ is not exact, but it can be made exact by multiplying with a function $\ku(x,y)$. That is, perhaps for some nonzero function $\ku(x,y)$,

$$ \ku(x,y) \kM(x,y) \, dx + \ku(x,y) \kN(x,y) \, dy = 0 $$

is exact. Any solution to this new equation is also a solution to $\kM\, dx + \kN \, dy = 0$.

In fact, a linear equation

$$ \kdydx + \kp(x) \ky = \kf(x), \qquad \text{or} \qquad \bigl( \kp(x) \ky - \kf(x) \bigr)\, dx + dy = 0, $$

is always such an equation. Let $\kr(x) = e^{\int \kp(x)\,dx}$ be the integrating factor for the linear equation. Multiply the equation by $\kr(x)$ and write it in the form of $\kM + \kN \kdydx = 0$.

$$ \kr(x) \kp(x) \ky - \kr(x) \kf(x) + \kr(x) \kdydx = 0 . $$

Then $\kM = \kr(x) \kp(x) \ky - \kr(x) \kf(x)$, so $\kMy = \kr(x) \kp(x)$, while $\kN = \kr(x)$, so $\kNx = \kr'(x) = \kr(x) \kp(x)$. In other words, we have an exact equation. Integrating factors for linear functions are just a special case of integrating factors for exact equations.

But how do we find the integrating factor $\ku$? Well, for $\ku\kM \, dx + \ku\kN \, dy = 0$ to be exact, the function $\ku$ should satisfy

At first it may seem we replaced one differential equation by another. Even worse, the new equation is a PDE. True, but all hope is not lost.

A strategy that often works is to look for a $\ku$ that is a function of $x$ alone, or a function of $y$ alone. After all, that is what worked for linear equations. If $\ku$ is a function of $x$ alone, that is, $\ku(x)$, then we write $\ku'(x)$ instead of $\kux$, and $\kuy$ is just zero. Then

In particular, $\frac{\kMy-\kNx}{\kN}$ ought to be a function of $x$ alone (not depend on $y$). If so, then we have a linear equation $\ku' - \frac{\kMy-\kNx}{\kN} \ku = 0$. Letting $\kP(x) = \frac{\kMy-\kNx}{\kN}$, we solve the linear equation to find $\ku(x) = C e^{\int \kP(x) \, dx}$. The constant in the solution is not relevant, we need any nonzero solution, so we take $C=1$. Then $\ku(x) = e^{\int \kP(x) \, dx}$ is the integrating factor making the equation exact.

Similarly, we could try a function of the form $\ku(y)$. Then $\frac{\kMy-\kNx}{\kM} \ku = - \ku'$. In particular, $\frac{\kMy-\kNx}{\kM}$ ought to be a function of $y$ alone. If so, we have a linear equation $\ku' + \frac{\kMy-\kNx}{\kM} \ku = 0$. Letting $\kQ(y) = \frac{\kMy-\kNx}{\kM}$, we find $\ku(y) = C e^{-\int \kQ(y) \, dy}$. We take $C=1$. So $\ku(y) = e^{-\int \kQ(y) \, dy}$ is the integrating factor.

Example 1.8.6

Solve

$$ \frac{x^2+y^2}{x+1} + 2y \kdydx = 0 . $$

Let $\kM= \frac{x^2+y^2}{x+1}$ and $\kN=2y$. Compute

$$ \kMy-\kNx = \frac{2y}{x+1} - 0 = \frac{2y}{x+1} . $$

As this is not zero, the equation is not exact. We notice

$$ \kP(x) = \frac{\kMy-\kNx}{\kN} = \frac{2y}{x+1} \, \frac{1}{2y} = \frac{1}{x+1} $$

is a function of $x$ alone. We compute the integrating factor

$$ e^{\int \kP(x) \, dx} = e^{\ln (x+1)} = x+1 . $$

We multiply our given equation by $(x+1)$ to obtain

$$ x^2+y^2 + 2y(x+1) \kdydx = 0 , $$

which is an exact equation that we solved in Example 1.8.5. The solution was

$$ y = \pm \sqrt{\frac{C-({1}/{3})x^3}{x+1}} . $$

Example 1.8.7

Solve

$$ y^2 + (xy+1) \kdydx = 0 . $$

First compute

$$ \kMy-\kNx = 2y-y = y . $$

As this is not zero, the equation is not exact. We observe

$$ \kQ(y) = \frac{\kMy-\kNx}{\kM} = \frac{y}{y^2} = \frac{1}{y} $$

is a function of $y$ alone. We compute the integrating factor

$$ e^{-\int \kQ(y) \, dy} = e^{-\ln y} = \frac{1}{y} . $$

Therefore, we look at the exact equation

$$ y + \frac{xy+1}{y} \kdydx = 0 . $$

The reader should double check that this equation is exact. We follow the procedure for exact equations

$$ \kF(x,y) = xy + A(y) , $$

and

$$ \frac{xy+1}{y} = x+\frac{1}{y} = x+ A'(y) . $$

Consequently, $A'(y) = {1}/{y}$ or $A(y) = \ln \, \lvert y \rvert$. Thus $\kF(x,y) = xy + \ln \, \lvert y \rvert$. It is not possible to solve $\kF(x,y)=C$ for $y$ in terms of elementary functions, so let us be content with the implicit solution:

$$ xy + \ln \, \lvert y \rvert = C . $$

We are looking for the general solution, and we divided by $y$ above. We should check what happens when $y=0$, as the equation itself makes perfect sense in that case. We plug in $y=0$ to find the equation is satisfied. So $y=0$ is also a solution.
