## What is new

The chapter leaves linear equations for the plane's nonlinear flows and reads them from their critical points: the two-dimensional autonomous system and its phase portrait, critical points and equilibrium solutions, the Jacobian matrix and the linearization at a critical point (8.1); isolated critical points, almost linear systems, the classification of Table 8.1 with stability and asymptotic stability, the trouble with centers, conservative equations with their Hamiltonian (energy) and the saddle-or-center dichotomy (8.2); the nonlinear pendulum with its swinging and spinning trajectories, its energy, its period as an elliptic-type integral with the relative error of the linear period, and the Lotka–Volterra predator–prey model with its constant of motion (8.3); closed trajectories, limit cycles and periodic orbits, the Van der Pol oscillator and relaxation oscillations, the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem, simply connected regions, the divergence and the Bendixson–Dulac theorem (8.4); chaos as sensitivity to initial conditions, the Duffing equation, Poincaré sections and strange attractors, and the Lorenz system and attractor in three dimensions (8.5).

Calculus's ids are reused where the concept is the same: `simply-connected-region` (Calculus 16.3) and `divergence` (16.5). Calculus's `jacobian` (15.7) is the determinant of the change of variables, `linear-approximation` (4.2) a function's tangent line and `critical-point` (4.3) a point where a derivative vanishes: each is a cousin, not the same concept, so this chapter's ids are `jacobian-matrix`, `linearization-of-a-system` and `critical-point-of-a-system`. Within the book, 1.6's `equilibrium-point` (the critical point of x′ = f(x)) and 3.5's six portrait types are the prerequisites; the book says the concepts are "exactly the same" and lists Table 8.1 as Table 3.1 less the center.

## What would make this chapter stunning (root rule 23)

The chapter is where the book's pictures finally move on their own terms: every figure is a flow, and the reader can put a point into it. Drop a start anywhere in Figure 8.1 and watch it circle the center or escape past the saddle; zoom into a critical point and see the nonlinear portrait become its linearization; slide an energy level up the well ½x² − ⅓x³ until the closed orbit opens at the saddle. The pendulum swings beside its own phase point (the 3Blue1Brown survivor of 8.3): release it from an angle and the point circles; push it harder and it crosses the separatrix into the wavy spinning curves; add friction and both spiral home. Predator and prey chase each other round the center while their constant of motion stays fixed in the readout, and bringing in two hundred foxes drives the hares to one. The Van der Pol limit cycle grows its relaxation corners as μ rises, and every trajectory lands on it within a loop. Two Duffing solutions a hair apart (the second survivor) run together, then part, and the gap between their starts can be made as small as the reader likes without changing the outcome; the strobe builds the strange attractor point by point and a phase shift twists it; and the Lorenz trajectory is drawn into space as it flies, the reader turning it, while its x(t) below flips between the two lobes.
