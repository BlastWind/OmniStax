Ordinary and singular points

Consider a linear second-order homogeneous ODE of the form

$$ P(x) \kydprime + Q(x) \kyprime + R(x) \ky = 0 . $$

Suppose that $P(x)$, $Q(x)$, and $R(x)$ are polynomials. We will try a solution of the form

$$ \ky = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k {(x-x_0)}^k , $$

and solve for the $\ka_k$ to obtain a solution defined in some interval around $x_0$.

The point $x_0$ is called an ordinary point if $P(x_0) \neq 0$. That is, the functions

$$ \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{R(x)}{P(x)} $$

are defined for $x$ near $x_0$. If $P(x_0) = 0$, then $x_0$ is a singular point. Handling singular points is harder than handling ordinary points and so we now focus only on ordinary points.

Airy’s equation

Example 7.2.2

Let us do a more complex example. Consider Airy’s equation:

$$ \kydprime - x\ky = 0 , $$

near the point $x_0 = 0$. Note that $x_0 = 0$ is an ordinary point.

We try

$$ \ky = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^k . $$

We differentiate twice (as above) to obtain

$$ \kydprime = \sum_{k=2}^\infty k\,(k-1) \, \ka_k x^{k-2} . $$

We plug $\ky$ into the equation:

$$ \begin{split} 0 = \kydprime-x\ky &= \Biggl( \sum_{k=2}^\infty k\,(k-1) \, \ka_k x^{k-2} \Biggr) - x \Biggl( \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^k \Biggr) \\ &= \Biggl( \sum_{k=2}^\infty k\,(k-1) \, \ka_k x^{k-2} \Biggr) - \Biggl( \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^{k+1} \Biggr) . \end{split} $$

We reindex and synchronize the start indexes to make the series possible to sum:

$$ \begin{split} 0 = \kydprime-x\ky &= \Biggl( 2 \ka_2 + \sum_{k=1}^\infty (k+2)\,(k+1) \, \ka_{k+2} x^k \Biggr) - \Biggl( \sum_{k=1}^\infty \ka_{k-1} x^k \Biggr) \\ &= 2 \ka_2 + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \Bigl( (k+2)\,(k+1) \, \ka_{k+2} - \ka_{k-1} \Bigr) \, x^k . \end{split} $$

As $\kydprime-x\ky$ is supposed to be $0$, we have $\ka_2 = 0$, and

$$ (k+2)\,(k+1) \,\ka_{k+2} - \ka_{k-1} = 0 , \qquad \text{or} \qquad \ka_{k+2} = \frac{\ka_{k-1}}{(k+2)(k+1)} . $$

We have arbitrary constants $\ka_0$ and $\ka_1$, and $\ka_2 = 0$. We compute a few higher coefficients:

$$ \begin{gather*} \ka_3 = \frac{\ka_0}{3 \cdot 2}, \quad \ka_4 = \frac{\ka_1}{4 \cdot 3}, \quad \ka_5 = \frac{\ka_2}{5 \cdot 4} = 0, \quad \ka_6 = \frac{\ka_3}{6 \cdot 5} = \frac{\ka_0}{6 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 \cdot 2}, \\ \ka_7 = \frac{\ka_4}{7 \cdot 6} = \frac{\ka_1}{7 \cdot 6 \cdot 4 \cdot 3}, \quad \ka_8 = \frac{\ka_5}{8 \cdot 7} = 0, \quad \ka_9 = \frac{\ka_6}{9 \cdot 8} = \frac{\ka_0}{9 \cdot 8 \cdot 6 \cdot 5 \cdot 3 \cdot 2}, \quad \ldots \end{gather*} $$

We jump in steps of three. For $\ka_k$ where $k$ is a multiple of $3$, that is, $k=3n$, we notice that

$$ \ka_{3n} = \frac{\ka_0}{2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 \cdots (3n-1) \cdot (3n)} . $$

For $\ka_k$ where $k = 3n+1$, we notice

$$ \ka_{3n+1} = \frac{\ka_1}{3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot 7 \cdots (3n) \cdot (3n+1)} . $$

Finally, since $\ka_2 = 0$, we have $\ka_5 = 0$, $\ka_8 = 0$, $\ka_{11}=0$, etc. In general, $\ka_{3n+2} = 0$.

In other words, if we write down the series for $\ky$, it has two parts

$$ \begin{split} \ky &= \left( \ka_0 + \frac{\ka_0}{6} x^3 + \frac{\ka_0}{180} x^6 + \cdots + \frac{\ka_0}{2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 \cdots (3n-1) \cdot (3n)} x^{3n} + \cdots \right) \\ &\phantom{=} + \left( \ka_1 x + \frac{\ka_1}{12} x^4 + \frac{\ka_1}{504} x^7 + \cdots + \frac{\ka_1}{3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot 7 \cdots (3n) \cdot (3n+1)} x^{3n+1} + \cdots \right) \\ & = \ka_0 \left( 1 + \frac{1}{6} x^3 + \frac{1}{180} x^6 + \cdots + \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 \cdots (3n-1) \cdot (3n)} x^{3n} + \cdots \right) \\ &\phantom{=} + \ka_1 \left( x + \frac{1}{12} x^4 + \frac{1}{504} x^7 + \cdots + \frac{1}{3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot 7 \cdots (3n) \cdot (3n+1)} x^{3n+1} + \cdots \right) . \end{split} $$

We define

$$ \begin{align*} \kyone(x) &= 1 + \frac{1}{6} x^3 + \frac{1}{180} x^6 + \cdots + \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 6 \cdots (3n-1) \cdot (3n)} x^{3n} + \cdots, \\ \kytwo(x) &= x + \frac{1}{12} x^4 + \frac{1}{504} x^7 + \cdots + \frac{1}{3 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdot 7 \cdots (3n) \cdot (3n+1)} x^{3n+1} + \cdots , \end{align*} $$

and write the general solution to the equation as $\ky(x)= \ka_0 \kyone(x) + \ka_1 \kytwo(x)$. If we plug $x=0$ into the power series for $\kyone$ and $\kytwo$, we find $\kyone(0) = 1$ and $\kytwo(0) = 0$. Similarly, $\kyoneprime(0) = 0$ and $\kytwoprime(0) = 1$. Therefore $\ky = \ka_0 \kyone + \ka_1 \kytwo$ is a solution that satisfies the initial conditions $\ky(0) = \ka_0$ and $\kyprime(0) = \ka_1$.

The functions $\kyone$ and $\kytwo$ cannot be written in terms of the elementary functions that you know. See Figure 7.3 for the plot of the solutions $\kyone$ and $\kytwo$. These functions have many interesting properties. For example, they are oscillatory for negative $x$ (like solutions to $\kydprime+\ky=0$) and for positive $x$ they grow without bound (like solutions to $\kydprime-\ky=0$).

Hermite’s equation

Sometimes a series solution may turn out to be a polynomial. Let us see an example.

Example 7.2.3

Let us find a solution to the so-called Hermite’s equation of order $n$:

$$ \kydprime -2x\kyprime + 2n \ky = 0 . $$

Let us find a solution around the point $x_0 = 0$. We try

$$ \ky = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^k . $$

We differentiate (as above) to obtain

$$ \kyprime = \sum_{k=1}^\infty k \ka_k x^{k-1} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \kydprime = \sum_{k=2}^\infty k\,(k-1) \, \ka_k x^{k-2} . $$

Now we plug into the equation

$$ \begin{split} 0 &= \kydprime-2x\kyprime+2n\ky \\ &= \Biggl( \sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1) \ka_k x^{k-2} \Biggr) - 2x \Biggl( \sum_{k=1}^\infty k \ka_k x^{k-1} \Biggr) + 2n \Biggl( \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^k \Biggr) \\ &= \Biggl( \sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1) \ka_k x^{k-2} \Biggr) - \Biggl( \sum_{k=1}^\infty 2k \ka_k x^k \Biggr) + \Biggl( \sum_{k=0}^\infty 2n \ka_k x^k \Biggr) \\ &= \Biggl(2\ka_2+ \sum_{k=1}^\infty (k+2)(k+1) \ka_{k+2} x^k \Biggr) - \Biggl( \sum_{k=1}^\infty 2k \ka_k x^k \Biggr) + \Biggl( 2n\ka_0 + \sum_{k=1}^\infty 2n \ka_k x^k \Biggr) \\ &= 2\ka_2+2n\ka_0+ \sum_{k=1}^\infty \bigl( (k+2)(k+1) \ka_{k+2} - 2k\ka_k + 2n \ka_k \bigr) x^k . \end{split} $$

As $\kydprime-2x\kyprime+2n\ky = 0$, we have

$$ (k+2)(k+1) \ka_{k+2} + ( - 2k+ 2n) \ka_k = 0 , \qquad \text{or} \qquad \ka_{k+2} = \frac{(2k-2n)}{(k+2)(k+1)} \ka_k . $$

This recurrence relation actually includes $\ka_2 = -n\ka_0$ (which comes about from the constant term $2\ka_2+2n\ka_0 = 0$). Again, $\ka_0$ and $\ka_1$ are arbitrary.

$$ \begin{align*} & \ka_2 = \frac{-2n}{2 \cdot 1}\ka_0, \\ & \ka_3 = \frac{2(1-n)}{3 \cdot 2} \ka_1, \\ & \ka_4 = \frac{2(2-n)}{4 \cdot 3} \ka_2 = \frac{2^2(2-n)(-n)}{4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} \ka_0 , \\ & \ka_5 = \frac{2(3-n)}{5 \cdot 4} \ka_3 = \frac{2^2(3-n)(1-n)}{5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2} \ka_1 , \quad \ldots \end{align*} $$

We separate the even and odd coefficients to find that

$$ \begin{align*} \ka_{2m} &=\frac{2^m(-n)(2-n)\cdots(2m-2-n)}{(2m)!} \ka_0, \\ \ka_{2m+1} &=\frac{2^m(1-n)(3-n)\cdots(2m-1-n)}{(2m+1)!} \ka_1 . \end{align*} $$

We write down the two series, one with the even powers and one with the odd:

$$ \begin{align*} \kyone(x) & = 1+\frac{2(-n)}{2!} x^2 + \frac{2^2(-n)(2-n)}{4!} x^4 + \frac{2^3(-n)(2-n)(4-n)}{6!} x^6 + \cdots , \\ \kytwo(x) & = x+\frac{2(1-n)}{3!} x^3 + \frac{2^2(1-n)(3-n)}{5!} x^5 + \frac{2^3(1-n)(3-n)(5-n)}{7!} x^7 + \cdots . \end{align*} $$

Then

$$ \ky(x) = \ka_0 \kyone(x) + \ka_1 \kytwo(x) . $$

We remark that if $n$ is a positive even integer, then $\kyone(x)$ is a polynomial as all the coefficients in the series beyond degree $n$ are zero. If $n$ is a positive odd integer, then $\kytwo(x)$ is a polynomial. For example, if $n=4$, then

$$ \kyone(x) = 1 + \frac{2(-4)}{2!} x^2 + \frac{2^2(-4)(2-4)}{4!} x^4 = 1 - 4x^2 + \frac{4}{3} x^4 . $$
