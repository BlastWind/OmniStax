The behavior of ODEs at singular points can be complicated. For certain singular points, we can find a solution on at least one side of the singular point using a modification of the power series. Let us look at some examples before giving a general method.

Examples

Example 7.3.1

Consider the simple first-order equation

$$ 2 x \kyprime - \ky = 0 . $$

Note that $x=0$ is a singular point. Setting $x=0$ in the equation, we find that any solution defined near zero satisfies $\ky(0)=0$, but it is even worse. If we try to plug in

$$ \ky = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^k , $$

we obtain

$$ \begin{split} 0 = 2 x\kyprime-\ky &= 2x \, \Biggl( \sum_{k=1}^\infty k \ka_k x^{k-1} \Biggr) - \Biggl( \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^k \Biggr) \\ & = -\ka_0 + \sum_{k=1}^\infty (2 k \ka_k - \ka_k) \, x^{k} . \end{split} $$

First, $\ka_0 = 0$. Next, the only way to solve $0 = 2 k \ka_k - \ka_k = (2k-1) \, \ka_k$ for $k = 1,2,3,\dots$ is for $\ka_k = 0$ for all $k$. Therefore, in this manner we only get the trivial solution $\ky=0$. We need a nonzero solution to get the general solution to the equation.

Let us try $\ky=x^{\kr}$ for some real number $\kr$. Consequently our solution—if we can find one—may only make sense for positive $x$. Then $\kyprime = \kr x^{\kr-1}$. So

$$ 0 = 2 x \kyprime - \ky = 2 x \kr x^{\kr-1} - x^{\kr} = (2\kr-1) x^{\kr} . $$

Thus $\kr= {1}/{2}$ and so $\ky = x^{1/2}$. As the equation is linear, the general solution for positive $x$ is

$$ \ky = C x^{1/2} . $$

If $C \neq 0$, then the derivative of the solution “blows up” at $x=0$ (the singular point). There is only one solution that is differentiable at $x=0$ and that’s the trivial solution $\ky=0$.

Not every problem with a singular point has a solution of the form $\ky=x^{\kr}$, of course. But perhaps we can combine the methods. What we will do is to try a solution of the form

$$ \ky = x^{\kr} \kf(x) $$

for positive $x$, where $\kf(x)$ is an analytic function (a power series).

Example 7.3.2

Consider the equation

$$ 4 x^2 \kydprime - 4 x^2 \kyprime + (1-2x)\ky = 0, $$

and again note that $x=0$ is a singular point.

Let us try

$$ \ky = x^{\kr} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^k = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^{k+\kr} , $$

where $\kr$ is a real number, not necessarily an integer. Again if such a solution exists, it may only exist for positive $x$. First we find the derivatives

First, to have a solution we must have $\bigl( 4\kr(\kr-1) + 1 \bigr) \, \ka_0 = 0$. Supposing $\ka_0 \neq 0$,

$$ 4\kr(\kr-1) + 1 = 0 . $$

This equation is called the indicial equation. This particular indicial equation has a double root at $\kr = {1}/{2}$.

OK, so we know what $\kr$ has to be. That knowledge we obtained simply by looking at the coefficient of $x^{\kr}$. All other coefficients of $x^{k+\kr}$ also have to be zero so

$$ \bigl( 4 (k+\kr)\,(k+\kr-1) + 1 \bigr) \, \ka_k - \bigl( 4 (k+\kr-1) + 2 \bigr) \, \ka_{k-1} = 0 . $$

If we plug in $\kr={1}/{2}$ and solve for $\ka_k$, we get

$$ \ka_k = \frac{4 (k+{1}/{2}-1) + 2}{4 (k+{1}/{2})\,(k+{1}/{2}-1) + 1} \, \ka_{k-1} = \frac{1}{k} \, \ka_{k-1} . $$

Let us set $\ka_0 = 1$. Then

$$ \ka_1 = \frac{1}{1} \ka_0 = 1 , \quad \ka_2 = \frac{1}{2} \ka_1 = \frac{1}{2} , \quad \ka_3 = \frac{1}{3} \ka_2 = \frac{1}{3 \cdot 2} , \quad \ka_4 = \frac{1}{4} \ka_3 = \frac{1}{4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2} , \quad \cdots $$

Extrapolating, we notice that

$$ \ka_k = \frac{1}{k(k-1)(k-2) \cdots 3 \cdot 2} = \frac{1}{k!} . $$

In other words,

$$ \ky = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^{k+\kr} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k!} x^{k+1/2} = x^{1/2} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k!} x^{k} = x^{1/2} e^x . $$

That was lucky! In general, we will not be able to write the series in terms of elementary functions.

We have one solution, let us call it $\kyone = x^{1/2} e^x$. But what about a second solution? If we want a general solution, we need two linearly independent solutions. Picking $\ka_0$ to be a different constant only gets us a constant multiple of $\kyone$, and we do not have any other $\kr$ to try; we only have one solution to the indicial equation. Well, there are powers of $x$ floating around and we are taking derivatives, perhaps the logarithm (the antiderivative of $x^{-1}$) is around as well. It turns out we want to try for another solution of the form

$$ \kytwo = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \kb_k x^{k+\kr} + (\ln x) \kyone , $$

which in our case is

$$ \kytwo = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \kb_k x^{k+1/2} + (\ln x) x^{1/2} e^x . $$

We now differentiate this equation, substitute into the differential equation and solve for $\kb_k$. A long computation ensues and we obtain some recursion relation for $\kb_k$. The reader can (and should) try this to obtain for example the first three terms

$$ \kb_1 = \kb_0 -1 , \qquad \kb_2 = \frac{2\kb_1-1}{4} , \qquad \kb_3 = \frac{6\kb_2-1}{18} , \qquad \ldots $$

We then fix $\kb_0$ and obtain a solution $\kytwo$. Then we write the general solution as $\ky = A \kyone + B \kytwo$.

The method of Frobenius

Before giving the general method, let us clarify when the method applies. Let

$$ P(x) \kydprime + Q(x) \kyprime + R(x) \ky = 0 $$

be an ODE for polynomials $P(x)$, $Q(x)$, and $R(x)$. As before, if $P(x_0) = 0$, then $x_0$ is a singular point. If we divide by $P(x)$ to put the equation in standard form $\kydprime + \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \kyprime + \frac{R(x)}{P(x)} \ky = 0$, perhaps the singularities introduced are not too bad. More specifically, if the limits

$$ \lim_{x \to x_0} ~ (x-x_0) \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \lim_{x \to x_0} ~ (x-x_0)^2 \frac{R(x)}{P(x)} $$

both exist and are finite, then we say that $x_0$ is a regular singular point.

Example 7.3.3

Often, and for the rest of this section, $x_0 = 0$. Consider

$$ x^2\kydprime + x(1+x)\kyprime + (\pi+x^2)\ky = 0 . $$

Write

$$ \begin{align*} & \lim_{x \to 0} ~x \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} = \lim_{x \to 0} ~x \frac{x(1+x)}{x^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} ~(1+x) = 1 , \\ & \lim_{x \to 0} ~x^2 \frac{R(x)}{P(x)} = \lim_{x \to 0} ~x^2 \frac{(\pi+x^2)}{x^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} ~(\pi+x^2) = \pi . \end{align*} $$

So $x = 0$ is a regular singular point.

On the other hand, if we make the slight change

$$ x^2\kydprime + (1+x)\kyprime + (\pi+x^2)\ky = 0 , $$

then

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} ~x \frac{Q(x)}{P(x)} = \lim_{x \to 0} ~x \frac{(1+x)}{x^2} = \lim_{x \to 0} ~\frac{1+x}{x} = \text{DNE}. $$

Here DNE stands for does not exist. The point $0$ is singular, but not a regular singular point.

We now discuss the general Method of Frobenius. We only consider the method at the point $x=0$ for simplicity. If $x_0 \neq 0$, then in the solution, we would replace every $x$ with $(x-x_0)$. The main idea is the following theorem.

Theorem 7.3.1

Method of Frobenius

Suppose that

$$ P(x) \kydprime + Q(x) \kyprime + R(x) \ky = 0 \tag{7.3}$$

has a regular singular point at $x=0$, then there exists at least one solution of the form

$$ \ky = x^{\kr} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^k . $$

A solution of this form is called a Frobenius-type solution.

The method usually breaks down like this:

  1. We seek a Frobenius-type solution of the form $$ \ky = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^{k+\kr} . $$ We plug this $\ky$ into the left-hand side of equation (7.3), collect terms, and write everything as a single series.
  2. The obtained series must be zero. Setting the first coefficient (the one with the smallest power) in the series to zero we obtain the indicial equation, which is a quadratic polynomial in $\kr$.
  3. If the indicial equation has two real roots $\krone$ and $\krtwo$ such that $\krone - \krtwo$ is not an integer, then we have two linearly independent Frobenius-type solutions. Using the first root, we plug in $$ \kyone = x^{\krone} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^{k} , $$ and we solve for all $\ka_k$ to obtain the first solution. Then using the second root, we plug in $$ \kytwo = x^{\krtwo} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \kb_k x^{k} , $$ and solve for all $\kb_k$ to obtain the second solution.
  4. If the indicial equation has a doubled root $\kr$, then there we find one solution $$ \kyone = x^{\kr} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^{k} , $$ and then we obtain a new solution by plugging
    $$ \kytwo = x^{\kr} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \kb_k x^{k} + (\ln x) \kyone , $$
    into equation (7.3) and solving for the constants $\kb_k$.
  5. If the indicial equation has two real roots such that $\krone-\krtwo$ is an integer, then, assuming $\krone$ is the larger root, one solution is $$ \kyone = x^{\krone} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^{k} , $$ and the second linearly independent solution is of the form
    $$ \kytwo = x^{\krtwo} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \kb_k x^{k} + C (\ln x) \kyone , $$
    where we plug $\kytwo$ into (7.3) and solve for the constants $\kb_k$ and $C$.
  6. Finally, if the indicial equation has complex roots, then solving for $\ka_k$ in the solution $$ \ky = x^{\krone} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^{k} $$ results in a complex-valued function—the $\ka_k$ are complex numbers. We obtain our two linearly independent solutions by taking the real and imaginary parts of $\ky$.

The main idea is to find at least one Frobenius-type solution. If we are lucky and find two, we are done. If we only get one, we either use the ideas above or even a different method such as reduction of order (see §2.1) to obtain a second solution.

Bessel functions

An important class of functions that arise commonly in physics are the Bessel functions. For example, these functions appear when solving the wave equation in two and three dimensions. First consider Bessel’s equation of order $p$:

$$ x^2 \kydprime + x\kyprime + \left(x^2 - p^2\right)\ky = 0 . $$

We allow $p$ to be any number, not just an integer, although integers and multiples of ${1}/{2}$ are most important in applications.

When we plug

$$ \ky = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \ka_k x^{k+\kr} $$

into Bessel’s equation of order $p$, we obtain the indicial equation

$$ \kr(\kr-1)+\kr-p^2 = (\kr-p)(\kr+p) = 0 . $$

Unless $p=0$, we obtain two roots, $\krone = p$ and $\krtwo = -p$. If $p$ is not an integer, then following the method of Frobenius and setting $\ka_0 = 1$, we find linearly independent solutions of the form

$$ \begin{align*} & \kyone = x^p \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{{(-1)}^k x^{2k}}{2^{2k} k! (k+p)(k-1+p)\cdots (2+p)(1+p)} , \\ & \kytwo = x^{-p} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{{(-1)}^k x^{2k}}{2^{2k} k! (k-p)(k-1-p)\cdots (2-p)(1-p)} . \end{align*} $$

Bessel functions are convenient constant multiples of $\kyone$ and $\kytwo$. First, we define the gamma function

$$ \kGamma(x) = \int_0^\infty t^{x-1} e^{-t} \, dt . $$

Notice that $\kGamma(1) = 1$. The gamma function also has a wonderful property

$$ \kGamma(x+1) = x \kGamma(x) . $$

From this property, it follows that $\kGamma(n) = (n-1)!$ when $n$ is a positive integer. So the gamma function is a continuous version of the factorial. We compute:

$$ \begin{align*} & \kGamma(k+p+1)=(k+p)(k-1+p)\cdots (2+p)(1+p) \kGamma(1+p) , \\ & \kGamma(k-p+1)=(k-p)(k-1-p)\cdots (2-p)(1-p) \kGamma(1-p) . \end{align*} $$

Using $\kGamma(x+1) = x \kGamma(x)$, we define the gamma function for all $x$ except nonpositive integers. The reciprocal $\frac{1}{\kGamma(x)}$ can be defined for all $x$ by letting $\frac{1}{\kGamma(x)}$ be zero for integers $x \leq 0$.

We define the Bessel functions of the first kind of order $p$ and $-p$ as

$$ \begin{align*} & \kJ_p(x) = \frac{1}{2^p\kGamma(1+p)} \kyone = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{{(-1)}^k}{k! \, \kGamma(k+p+1)} {\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}^{2k+p} , \\ & \kJ_{-p}(x) = \frac{1}{2^{-p}\kGamma(1-p)} \kytwo = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{{(-1)}^k}{k! \,\kGamma(k-p+1)} {\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}^{2k-p} . \end{align*} $$

As these are constant multiples of the solutions we found above, these are both solutions to Bessel’s equation of order $p$. The constants are picked for convenience. As the reciprocal of $\kGamma$ is defined for all $x$, the formulas on the right hold even for integer $p$. When $n$ is a nonnegative integer,

$$ \kJ_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{{(-1)}^k}{k! \,(k+n)!} {\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}^{2k+n} . $$

When $p$ is not an integer, $\kJ_p$ and $\kJ_{-p}$ are linearly independent. For integer orders, one can check that

$$ \kJ_n(x) = {(-1)}^n\kJ_{-n}(x) . $$

So $\kJ_{-n}$ is not a second linearly independent solution. The other solution is the so-called Bessel function of second kind. These make sense only for integer orders $n$ and are defined as limits of linear combinations of $\kJ_p(x)$ and $\kJ_{-p}(x)$, as $p$ approaches $n$ in the following way:

$$ \kY_n(x) = \lim_{p\to n} \frac{\cos(p \pi) \kJ_p(x) - \kJ_{-p}(x)}{\sin(p \pi)} . $$

Each linear combination of $\kJ_p(x)$ and $\kJ_{-p}(x)$ is a solution to Bessel’s equation of order $p$. Then as we take the limit as $p$ goes to $n$, we see that $\kY_n(x)$ is a solution to Bessel’s equation of order $n$. It also turns out that $\kY_n(x)$ and $\kJ_n(x)$ are linearly independent. Therefore, when $n$ is an integer, we have the general solution to Bessel’s equation of order $n$:

$$ \ky = A \kJ_n(x) + B \kY_n(x) , $$

for arbitrary constants $A$ and $B$. Note that $\kY_n(x)$ goes to negative infinity at $x=0$. Many mathematical software packages have these functions $\kJ_n(x)$ and $\kY_n(x)$ defined, so they can be used just like say $\sin(x)$ and $\cos(x)$. In fact, Bessel functions have some similar properties. For example, $-\kJ_1(x)$ is a derivative of $\kJ_0(x)$, and in general the derivative of $\kJ_n(x)$ can be written as a linear combination of $\kJ_{n-1}(x)$ and $\kJ_{n+1}(x)$. Furthermore, these functions oscillate, although they are not periodic. See Figure 7.4 for graphs of Bessel functions.

Example 7.3.4

Other equations can sometimes be solved in terms of the Bessel functions. For example, given a positive constant $\lambda$,

$$ x \kydprime + \kyprime + \lambda^2 x \ky = 0 , $$

can be changed to $x^2 \kydprime + x \kyprime + \lambda^2 x^2 \ky = 0$. Changing variables $t = \lambda x$, we obtain, via the chain rule, the equation in $\ky$ and $t$:

$$ t^2 \kydprime + t \kyprime + t^2 \ky = 0 , $$

which we recognize as Bessel’s equation of order $0$. Therefore the general solution is $\ky(t) = A \kJ_0(t) + B \kY_0(t)$, or in terms of $x$:

$$ \ky = A \kJ_0(\lambda x) + B \kY_0(\lambda x) . $$

This equation comes up, for example, when finding the fundamental modes of vibration of a circular drum, but we digress.
