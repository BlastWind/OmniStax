Periodic functions and motivation

As motivation for studying Fourier series, consider the problem

$$ \kxdprime + \komegazero^2 \kx = \kf(t) , \tag{4.6}$$

for some periodic function $\kf(t)$. In §2.6, we found the general solution to

$$ \kxdprime + \komegazero^2 \kx = F_0 \cos ( \komega t) . \tag{4.7}$$

One way to solve (4.6) is to decompose $\kf(t)$ as a sum of cosines (and sines) and then solve many problems of the form (4.7). We then use the principle of superposition to sum up all these solutions to get a solution to (4.6).

Before we proceed, let us talk a little bit more in detail about periodic functions. A function is said to be periodic with period $P$ if $\kf(t) = \kf(t+P)$ for all $t$. For brevity, we say $\kf(t)$ is $P$-periodic. Note that a $P$-periodic function is also $2P$-periodic, $3P$-periodic and so on. For example, $\cos (t)$ and $\sin (t)$ are $2\pi$-periodic. So are $\cos (kt)$ and $\sin (kt)$ for all integers $k$. The constant functions are an extreme example. They are periodic for any period (exercise).

Normally, we start with a function $\kf(t)$ defined on some interval $[-L,L]$, and we want to extend $\kf(t)$ periodically to make it a $2L$-periodic function. We do this extension by defining a new function $\kF(t)$ such that for $t$ in $[-L,L]$, $\kF(t) = \kf(t)$. For $t$ in $[L,3L]$, we define $\kF(t) = \kf(t-2L)$, for $t$ in $[-3L,-L]$, $\kF(t) = \kf(t+2L)$, and so on. To make that work, we needed $\kf(-L) = \kf(L)$. We could have also started with $\kf$ defined only on the half-open interval $(-L,L]$ and then define $\kf(-L) = \kf(L)$.

Example 4.2.1

Define $\kf(t) = 1-t^2$ on $[-1,1]$. Extend $\kf(t)$ periodically to a 2-periodic function. For $1 \leq t \leq 3$, we get $\kf(t) = 1-{(t-2)}^2$. For $-3 \leq t \leq -1$, we get $\kf(t) = 1-{(t+2)}^2$. For $3 \leq t \leq 5$, we get $\kf(t) = 1-{(t-4)}^2$. And so on. See Figure 4.3.

You should be careful to distinguish between $\kf(t)$ and its extension. A common mistake is to assume that a formula for $\kf(t)$ holds for its extension. It can be confusing when the formula for $\kf(t)$ is periodic, but with perhaps a different period.

Inner product and eigenvector decomposition

Suppose $\kA$ is a symmetric matrix, that is, $\kA^T = \kA$. As we remarked before, eigenvectors of $\kA$ are then orthogonal. Here the word orthogonal means that if $\kvecv$ and $\vec{w}$ are two eigenvectors of $\kA$ for distinct eigenvalues, then $\langle \kvecv , \vec{w} \rangle = 0$. In this case, the inner product $\langle \kvecv , \vec{w} \rangle$ is the dot product, which can be computed as $\kvecv^T\vec{w}$.

To decompose a vector $\kvecv$ in terms of mutually orthogonal vectors $\vec{w}_1$ and $\vec{w}_2$, we write

$$ \kvecv = a_1 \vec{w}_1 + a_2 \vec{w}_2 . $$

Let us find the formula for $a_1$ and $a_2$. We compute,

$$ \langle \kvecv , \vec{w_1} \rangle = \langle a_1 \vec{w}_1 + a_2 \vec{w}_2 , \vec{w_1} \rangle = a_1 \langle \vec{w}_1 , \vec{w_1} \rangle + a_2 \underbrace{\langle \vec{w}_2 , \vec{w_1} \rangle}_{=0} = a_1 \langle \vec{w}_1 , \vec{w_1} \rangle . $$

Therefore,

$$ a_1 = \frac{\langle \kvecv , \vec{w_1} \rangle}{ \langle \vec{w}_1 , \vec{w_1} \rangle} . $$

Similarly,

$$ a_2 = \frac{\langle \kvecv , \vec{w_2} \rangle}{ \langle \vec{w}_2 , \vec{w_2} \rangle} . $$

You probably remember this formula from vector calculus.

Example 4.2.2

Write $\kvecv = \left[ \begin{smallmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{smallmatrix} \right]$ as a linear combination of $\vec{w_1} = \left[ \begin{smallmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{smallmatrix} \right]$ and $\vec{w_2} = \left[ \begin{smallmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{smallmatrix} \right]$.

Note that $\vec{w}_1$ and $\vec{w}_2$ are orthogonal as $\langle \vec{w}_1 , \vec{w}_2 \rangle = 1(1) + (-1)1 = 0$. Then

$$ \begin{align*} & a_1 = \frac{\langle \kvecv , \vec{w_1} \rangle}{ \langle \vec{w}_1 , \vec{w_1} \rangle} = \frac{2(1) + 3(-1)}{1(1) + (-1)(-1)} = \frac{-1}{2} , \\ & a_2 = \frac{\langle \kvecv , \vec{w_2} \rangle}{ \langle \vec{w}_2 , \vec{w_2} \rangle} = \frac{2 + 3}{1 + 1} = \frac{5}{2} . \end{align*} $$

Hence,

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{-1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{5}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} . $$

The trigonometric series

Instead of decomposing a vector in terms of eigenvectors of a matrix, we decompose a function in terms of eigenfunctions of a certain eigenvalue problem. The eigenvalue problem we use for the Fourier series is

$$ \kxdprime + \klambda \kx = 0, \quad \kx(-\pi) = \kx(\pi), \quad \kxprime(-\pi) = \kxprime(\pi) . $$

We computed that eigenfunctions are 1, $\cos (k t)$, $\sin (k t)$. That is, we want to find a representation of a $2\pi$-periodic function $\kf(t)$ as

$$ \boxed{~~ \begin{aligned} \kf(t) &= \frac{\kazero}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \Bigl[ \kan \cos (n t) + \kbn \sin (n t) \Bigr] \\ & = \frac{\kazero}{2} + a_1 \cos (t) + b_1 \sin (t) + a_2 \cos (2 t) + b_2 \sin (2 t) + \cdots \end{aligned} ~~} $$

This series is called the Fourier series or the trigonometric series for $\kf(t)$. The term $\kan \cos (n t) + \kbn \sin (n t)$ is sometimes called the $n^{\text{th}}$ harmonic. We write the coefficient of the eigenfunction 1 as $\frac{\kazero}{2}$ for convenience. We could also think of $1 = \cos (0t)$, so that we only need to look at $\cos (kt)$ and $\sin (kt)$.

As for matrices, we want to find a projection of $\kf(t)$ onto the subspaces given by the eigenfunctions. So we want to define an inner product of functions. For example, to find $\kan$, we want to compute $\langle \, \kf(t) \, , \, \cos (nt) \, \rangle$. We define the inner product as

$$ \langle \, \kf(t)\, , \, \kg(t) \, \rangle \overset{\text{def}}{=} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \kf(t) \, \kg(t) \, dt . $$

With this inner product, we saw in the previous section that the eigenfunctions $\cos (kt)$ (including the constant eigenfunction), and $\sin (kt)$ are orthogonal, that is,

$$ \begin{align*} \langle \, \cos (mt)\, , \, \cos (nt) \, \rangle = 0 & \qquad \text{for } m \neq n , \\ \langle \, \sin (mt)\, , \, \sin (nt) \, \rangle = 0 & \qquad \text{for } m \neq n , \\ \langle \, \sin (mt)\, , \, \cos (nt) \, \rangle = 0 & \qquad \text{for all } m \text{ and } n . \end{align*} $$

For $n=1,2,3,\ldots$, we have

$$ \begin{align*} \langle \, \cos (nt) \, , \, \cos (nt) \, \rangle &= \int_{-\pi}^\pi \cos(nt)\cos(nt) \, dt = \pi, \\ \langle \, \sin (nt) \, , \, \sin (nt) \, \rangle &= \int_{-\pi}^\pi \sin(nt)\sin(nt) \, dt = \pi, \end{align*} $$

by elementary calculus. For the constant, we get

$$ \langle \, 1 \, , \, 1 \, \rangle = \int_{-\pi}^\pi 1 \cdot 1 \, dt = 2\pi. $$

The coefficients are given by

$$ \boxed{~~ \begin{aligned} & \kan = \frac{\langle \, \kf(t) \, , \, \cos (nt) \, \rangle}{\langle \, \cos (nt) \, , \, \cos (nt) \, \rangle} = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \kf(t) \cos (nt) \, dt , \\ & \kbn = \frac{\langle \, \kf(t) \, , \, \sin (nt) \, \rangle}{\langle \, \sin (nt) \, , \, \sin (nt) \, \rangle} = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \kf(t) \sin (nt) \, dt . \end{aligned} ~~} $$

Compare these expressions with the finite-dimensional example. For $\kazero$, we get a similar formula

$$ \boxed{~~ \kazero = 2 \frac{\langle \, \kf(t) \, , \, 1 \, \rangle}{\langle \, 1 \, , \, 1 \, \rangle} = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \kf(t) \, dt . ~~} $$

Let us check the formulas via the orthogonality properties.

Example 4.2.3

Take the function

$$ \kf(t) = t $$

for $t$ in $(-\pi,\pi]$. Extend $\kf(t)$ periodically and write it as a Fourier series. This function is called the sawtooth, and it finds many applications, for example, in electronic music.

The plot of the extended periodic function is given in Figure 4.4. Let us compute the coefficients. We start with $\kazero$,

$$ \kazero = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi t \,dt = 0 . $$

We will often use the result from calculus that says that the integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval is zero. Recall that an odd function is a function $\varphi(t)$ such that $\varphi(-t) = -\varphi(t)$. For example, the functions $t$, $\sin t$, or (importantly for us) $t \cos (nt)$ are all odd functions. Thus

$$ \kan = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi t \cos (nt) \,dt = 0 . $$

We move to $\kbn$. Another useful fact from calculus is that the integral of an even function over a symmetric interval is twice the integral of the same function over half the interval. An even function is a function $\varphi(t)$ such that $\varphi(-t) = \varphi(t)$. For example, $t \sin (nt)$ is even.

$$ \begin{split} \kbn & = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi t \sin (nt) \,dt \\ & = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^\pi t \sin (nt) \,dt \\ & = \frac{2}{\pi} \left( \left[ \frac{-t \cos (nt)}{n} \right]_{t=0}^{\pi} + \frac{1}{n} \int_{0}^\pi \cos (nt) \,dt \right) \\ & = \frac{2}{\pi} \left( \frac{-\pi \cos (n\pi)}{n} + 0 \right) \\ & = \frac{-2 \cos (n\pi)}{n} = \frac{2 \,{(-1)}^{n+1}}{n} . \end{split} $$

We have used that

$$ \cos (n\pi) = {(-1)}^n = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } n \text{ even} , \\ -1 & \text{if } n \text{ odd} . \end{cases} $$

The series, therefore, is

$$ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{2 \,{(-1)}^{n+1}}{n} \, \sin (n t) . $$

More explicitly, the first 3 harmonics of the series for $\kf(t)$ are

$$ 2 \, \sin (t) - \sin (2t) +\frac{2}{3} \sin (3t) + \cdots $$

The plot of these first three terms of the series, along with a plot of the first 20 terms is given in Figure 4.5.

Example 4.2.4

Take the function

$$ \kf(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \;{-\pi} < t \leq 0 , \\ \pi & \text{if } \;\phantom{-}0 < t \leq \pi . \end{cases} $$

Extend $\kf(t)$ periodically and write it as a Fourier series. This function or its variants appear often in applications and the function is called the square wave. It is a signal generated by simply periodically flipping a switch on or off.

The plot of the extended periodic function is given in Figure 4.6. Now we compute the coefficients. We start with $\kazero$

$$ \kazero = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \kf(t) \,dt = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{0}^\pi \pi \,dt = \pi . $$

Next,

$$ \kan = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \kf(t) \cos (nt) \,dt = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{0}^\pi \pi \cos (nt) \,dt = 0 . $$

And finally,

$$ \begin{split} \kbn & = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \kf(t) \sin (nt) \,dt \\ & = \frac{1}{\pi} \int_{0}^\pi \pi \sin (nt) \,dt \\ & = \left[ \frac{- \cos (nt)}{n} \right]_{t=0}^\pi \\ & = \frac{1 - \cos (\pi n)}{n} = \frac{1 - {(-1)}^n}{n} = \begin{cases} \frac{2}{n} & \text{if } n \text{ is odd} , \\ 0 & \text{if } n \text{ is even} . \end{cases} \end{split} $$

The Fourier series is

$$ \frac{\pi}{2} + \sum_{\substack{n=1\\n \text{ odd}}}^\infty \frac{2}{n} \sin (n t) = \frac{\pi}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{2}{2k-1} \sin \bigl( (2k-1)\, t \bigr) . $$

The first 3 harmonics of the series for $\kf(t)$ are

$$ \frac{\pi}{2} + 2 \, \sin (t) + \frac{2}{3} \sin (3t) + \cdots $$

The plot of these first three and also of the first 20 terms of the series is given in Figure 4.7.

We have so far skirted the issue of convergence. For example, if $\kf(t)$ is the square wave function, the equation

$$ \kf(t) = \frac{\pi}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{2}{2k-1} \sin \bigl( (2k-1)\, t \bigr) . $$

is only an equality for such $t$ where $\kf(t)$ is continuous. We do not get an equality for $t=-\pi,0,\pi$ and all the other discontinuities of $\kf(t)$. It is not hard to see that when $t$ is an integer multiple of $\pi$ (which gives all the discontinuities), then $\sin \bigl( (2k-1)\, t \bigr) = 0$ and so

$$ \frac{\pi}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac{2}{2k-1} \sin \bigl( (2k-1)\, t \bigr) = \frac{\pi}{2} . $$

We redefine $\kf(t)$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$ as

$$ \kf(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \; {-\pi} < t < 0 , \\ \pi & \text{if } \; \phantom{-}0 < t < \pi , \\ {\pi}/{2} & \text{if } \; \phantom{-}t = -\pi, t = 0,\text{ or } t = \pi, \end{cases} $$

and extend periodically. The series equals this new extended $\kf(t)$ everywhere, including the discontinuities. We will generally not worry about changing the function values at several (finitely many) points.

We will say more about convergence in the next section. Roughly speaking, if $\kf(t)$ is a nice enough function then the series converges to $\kf(t)$ wherever $\kf(t)$ is continuous. Let us, however, briefly mention an effect of the discontinuity. We zoom in near the discontinuity in the square wave and we plot the first 100 harmonics. See Figure 4.8. While the series is a very good approximation away from the discontinuities, the error (the overshoot) near the discontinuity at $t=\pi$ does not seem to be getting any smaller as we take more and more harmonics. This behavior is known as the Gibbs phenomenon. The region where the error is large does get smaller, however, the more terms in the series we take.

We can think of a periodic function as a “signal” that is a superposition of many signals of pure frequency. For example, we could think of the square wave as a tone of certain base frequency coming through the speaker of your computer. This base frequency is called the fundamental frequency and that is the “musical note” that you identify this sound as. But the square wave will also be a superposition of many different pure tones of frequencies that are multiples of the fundamental frequency. In music, the higher frequencies are called the overtones. The set of all the frequencies that appear is called the spectrum of the signal. On the other hand, if the signal were a simple sine wave instead of the square wave, then it would be only the pure tone of fundamental frequency (no overtones). The simplest way to make sound using a computer is the square wave, and the sound is very different from a pure tone. If you ever played video games from the 1980s or so, then you heard what square waves sound like.
