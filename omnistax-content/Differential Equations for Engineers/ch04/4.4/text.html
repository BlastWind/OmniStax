Odd and even periodic functions

You may have noticed by now that an odd function has no cosine terms in the Fourier series and an even function has no sine terms in the Fourier series. This observation is not a coincidence. Let us look at even and odd periodic functions in more detail.

Recall that a function $f(t)$ is odd if $f(-t) = -f(t)$. A function $f(t)$ is even if $f(-t) = f(t)$. For example, $\cos (n t)$ is even and $\sin (n t)$ is odd. Similarly, the function $t^k$ is even if $k$ is even and odd if $k$ is odd.

If $f(t)$ and $g(t)$ are both odd, then $f(t)+g(t)$ is odd. Similarly for even functions. On the other hand, if $f(t)$ is odd and $g(t)$ even, then we cannot say anything about the sum $f(t) + g(t)$. In fact, the Fourier series of any function is a sum of an odd function (the sine terms) and an even function (the cosine terms).

In this section, we consider odd and even periodic functions. We have previously defined the $2L$-periodic extension of a function defined on the interval $[-L,L]$. Sometimes we are only interested in the function on the interval $[0,L]$, and it would be convenient to have an odd (resp. even) function. If the function is odd (resp. even), all the cosine (resp. sine) terms disappear. What we will do is take the odd (resp. even) extension of the function to $[-L,L]$ and then extend periodically to a $2L$-periodic function.

Take a function $\kf(t)$ defined on $[0,L]$. On $(-L,L]$ define the functions

$$ \kFodd(t) \overset{\text{def}}{=} \begin{cases} \kf(t) & \text{if } \; \phantom{-}0 \leq t \leq L , \\ -\kf(-t) & \text{if } \; {-L} < t < 0 , \end{cases} $$
$$ \kFeven(t) \overset{\text{def}}{=} \begin{cases} \kf(t) & \text{if } \; \phantom{-}0 \leq t \leq L , \\ \kf(-t) & \text{if } \; {-L} < t < 0 . \end{cases} $$

Extend $\kFodd(t)$ and $\kFeven(t)$ to be $2L$-periodic. Then $\kFodd(t)$ is called the odd periodic extension of $\kf(t)$, and $\kFeven(t)$ is called the even periodic extension of $\kf(t)$. For the odd extension, we generally assume that $\kf(0) = \kf(L) = 0$.

Sine and cosine series

Let us write the Fourier series for an odd $2L$-periodic function $\kf(t)$. First, we compute the coefficients $\kan$ (including $n=0$) and get

$$ \kan = \frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^L \kf(t) \cos \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right) \, dt = 0 . $$

That is, the Fourier series of an odd function has no cosine terms. The integral is zero as $\kf(t) \cos \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right)$ is an odd function (product of an odd and an even function is odd) and the integral of an odd function over a symmetric interval is zero. The function $\kf(t) \sin \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right)$ is even as it is the product of two odd functions. The integral of an even function over a symmetric interval $[-L,L]$ is twice the integral of the function over the interval $[0,L]$. So

$$ \kbn = \frac{1}{L} \int_{-L}^L \kf(t) \sin \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right) \, dt = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^L \kf(t) \sin \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right) \, dt . $$

The Fourier series of an odd $\kf(t)$ is then

$$ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kbn \sin \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right) . $$

Now suppose $\kf(t)$ is an even $2L$-periodic function. For the same exact reasons as above, we find that $\kbn = 0$ and

$$ \kan = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^L \kf(t) \cos \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right) \, dt . $$

The formula still works for $n=0$, in which case it becomes

$$ \kazero = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^L \kf(t) \, dt . $$

The Fourier series of an even $\kf(t)$ is then

$$ \frac{\kazero}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kan \cos \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right) . $$

An interesting consequence is that the coefficients of the Fourier series of an odd (or even) function can be computed by just integrating over the half-interval $[0,L]$. Therefore, we can compute the Fourier series of the odd (or even) extension of a function by computing certain integrals over the interval where the original function is defined.

Theorem 4.4.1

Let $\kf(t)$ be a piecewise smooth function defined on $[0,L]$. Then the odd periodic extension of $\kf(t)$ has the Fourier series

$$ \boxed{~~ \kFodd(t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kbn \sin \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right) , ~~} $$

where

$$ \boxed{~~ \kbn = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^L \kf(t)\, \sin \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right) \, dt . ~~} $$

The even periodic extension of $\kf(t)$ has the Fourier series

$$ \boxed{~~ \kFeven(t) = \frac{\kazero}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kan \cos \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right) , ~~} $$

where

$$ \boxed{~~ \kan = \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^L \kf(t)\, \cos \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right) \, dt . ~~} $$

We call the series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty \kbn \sin \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t\right)$ the sine series of $\kf(t)$ and we call the series $\frac{\kazero}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kan \cos \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right)$ the cosine series of $\kf(t)$. We often do not actually care what happens outside of $[0,L]$. We simply pick whichever series fits our problem better.

It is not necessary to start with the full Fourier series to obtain the sine and cosine series. The sine series is really the eigenfunction expansion of $\kf(t)$ using eigenfunctions of the eigenvalue problem $\kxdprime+\klambda \kx = 0$, $\kx(0) = 0$, $\kx(L) = 0$. The cosine series is the eigenfunction expansion of $\kf(t)$ using eigenfunctions of the eigenvalue problem $\kxdprime+\klambda \kx = 0$, $\kxprime(0) = 0$, $\kxprime(L) = 0$. We would, therefore, get the same formulas by defining the inner product

$$ \langle \kf(t), g(t) \rangle = \int_0^L \kf(t) g(t) \, dt , $$

and following the procedure of §4.2. This point of view is useful, as we commonly use a specific series that arose because our underlying question led to a certain eigenvalue problem. If the eigenvalue problem is not one of the three we covered so far, you can still do an eigenfunction expansion, generalizing the results of this chapter. We will deal with such a generalization in chapter 5.

Example 4.4.2

Find the Fourier series of the even periodic extension of the function $\kf(t) = t^2$ for $0 \leq t \leq \pi$.

We want to write

$$ \kf(t) = \frac{\kazero}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kan \cos (n t) , $$

where

$$ \kazero = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi t^2 \, dt = \frac{2 \pi^2}{3} , $$

and

$$ \begin{split} \kan & = \frac{2}{\pi} \int_0^\pi t^2 \cos (n t) \, dt = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ t^2 \frac{1}{n} \sin (nt) \right]_0^\pi - \frac{4}{n\pi} \int_0^\pi t \sin (n t) \, dt \\ & = \frac{4}{n^2\pi} \Bigl[ t \cos (n t) \Bigr]_0^\pi - \frac{4}{n^2\pi} \int_0^\pi \cos (n t) \, dt = \frac{4{(-1)}^n}{n^2} . \end{split} $$

Note that we “detected” the continuity of the extension since the coefficients decay as $\frac{1}{n^2}$. That is, the even periodic extension of $t^2$ has no jump discontinuities. It does have corners, since the derivative, which is an odd function and a sine series, has jumps; it has a Fourier series whose coefficients decay only as $\frac{1}{n}$.

Explicitly, the first few terms of the series for $\kf(t)$ are

$$ \frac{\pi^2}{3} - 4 \cos (t) + \cos (2t) - \frac{4}{9} \cos (3t) + \cdots $$

Application

Fourier series ties in to the boundary value problems that we studied earlier. Consider the boundary value problem

$$ \kxdprime(t) + \klambda\, \kx(t) = \kf(t) , \quad 0 < t < L, $$

with the Dirichlet boundary conditions $\kx(0) = 0$, $\kx(L) = 0$. The Fredholm alternative (Theorem 4.1.2) says that as long as $\klambda$ is not an eigenvalue of the underlying homogeneous problem, there exists a unique solution. Eigenfunctions of this eigenvalue problem are the functions $\sin \left( \frac{n \pi}{L} t \right)$. To find the solution, we first find the Fourier sine series for $\kf(t)$. We write $\kx(t)$ also as a sine series, but with unknown coefficients. We substitute the series for $\kx(t)$ into the equation and solve for the unknown coefficients. If we have the Neumann boundary conditions $\kxprime(0) = 0$, $\kxprime(L) = 0$, we do the same procedure using the cosine series. Let us see how this method works on examples.

Example 4.4.3

Take the boundary value problem

$$ \kxdprime(t) + 2 \kx(t) = \kf(t) , \quad 0 < t < 1, $$

where $\kf(t) = t$ on $0 < t < 1$, and satisfying the Dirichlet boundary conditions $\kx(0) = 0$, $\kx(1)=0$. We write $\kf(t)$ as a sine series

$$ \kf(t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kcn \sin (n \pi t) . $$

Compute

$$ \kcn = 2 \int_0^1 t \sin (n \pi t) \,dt = \frac{2 \, {(-1)}^{n+1}}{n \pi} . $$

We write $\kx(t)$ as

$$ \kx(t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kbn \sin (n \pi t) . $$

We plug in to obtain

That $2-n^2\pi^2$ is not zero for any $n$, and that we can solve for $\kbn$, is precisely because $2$ is not an eigenvalue of the problem. We have thus obtained a Fourier series for the solution

$$ \kx(t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{2\,{(-1)}^{n+1}}{n \pi \,(2 - n^2 \pi^2)} \sin (n \pi t) . $$

See Figure 4.13 for a graph of the solution. Notice that because the eigenfunctions satisfy the boundary conditions, and $\kx$ is written in terms of the eigenfunctions, then $\kx$ satisfies the boundary conditions.

Example 4.4.4

We handle the Neumann conditions with cosine series. Take the boundary value problem

$$ \kxdprime(t) + 2 \kx(t) = \kf(t) , \quad 0 < t < 1, $$

where again $\kf(t) = t$ on $0 < t < 1$, but now satisfying the Neumann boundary conditions $\kxprime(0) = 0$, $\kxprime(1)=0$. We write $\kf(t)$ as a cosine series

$$ \kf(t) = \frac{\kczero}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kcn \cos (n \pi t) , $$

where

$$ \kczero = 2 \int_0^1 t \,dt = 1 , $$

and

$$ \kcn = 2 \int_0^1 t \cos (n \pi t) \,dt = \frac{2\bigl({(-1)}^n-1\bigr)}{\pi^2 n^2} = \begin{cases} \frac{-4}{\pi^2 n^2} & \text{if } n \text{ odd} , \\ 0 & \text{if } n \text{ even}. \end{cases} $$

We write $\kx(t)$ as a cosine series

$$ \kx(t) = \frac{\kazero}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kan \cos (n \pi t) . $$

We plug in to obtain

$$ \begin{split} \kxdprime(t) + 2 \kx(t) & = \left( \sum_{n=1}^\infty - \kan n^2 \pi^2 \cos (n \pi t) \right) + \kazero + 2 \left( \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kan \cos (n \pi t) \right) \\ & = \kazero + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \kan \bigl(2 - n^2 \pi^2 \bigr) \cos (n \pi t) \\ & = \kf(t) = \frac{1}{2} + \sum_{\substack{n=1\\n~\text{odd}}}^\infty \frac{-4}{\pi^2 n^2} \cos (n \pi t) . \end{split} $$

Therefore, $\kazero = \frac{1}{2}$, $\kan = 0$ for $n$ even ($n \geq 2$), and for $n$ odd, we have

$$ \kan \bigl(2 - n^2 \pi^2\bigr) = \frac{-4}{\pi^2 n^2} , $$

or

$$ \kan = \frac{-4}{n^2 \pi^2 (2 - n^2 \pi^2)} . $$

The Fourier series for the solution $\kx(t)$ is

$$ \kx(t) = \frac{1}{4} + \sum_{\substack{n=1\\n~\text{odd}}}^\infty \frac{-4}{n^2 \pi^2 (2 - n^2 \pi^2)} \cos (n \pi t) . $$
