Solving ODEs with the Laplace transform

Notice that the Laplace transform turns differentiation into multiplication by $s$. It is this property that makes it useful to apply the transform to differential equations.

Example 6.2.1

Consider the problem

$$ \kxdprime(t) + \kx(t) = \cos (2t), \quad \kx(0) = 0, \quad \kxprime(0) = 1 . $$

We will take the Laplace transform of both sides of the equation. By $\kX(s)$, we will, as usual, denote the Laplace transform of $\kx(t)$.

$$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{L} \bigl\{ \kxdprime(t) + \kx(t) \bigr\} & = \mathcal{L} \bigl\{ \cos (2t) \bigr\} , \\ s^2 \kX(s) -s\kx(0)-\kxprime(0) + \kX(s) & = \frac{s}{s^2 + 4} . \end{align*} $$

We plug in the initial conditions now—making computations more streamlined—to find

$$ s^2 \kX(s) -1 + \kX(s) = \frac{s}{s^2 + 4} . $$

We solve for $\kX(s)$,

$$ \kX(s) = \frac{s}{(s^2+1)(s^2 + 4)} + \frac{1}{s^2+1} . $$

We use partial fractions (exercise) to write

$$ \kX(s) =\frac{1}{3} \, \frac{s}{s^2+1} - \frac{1}{3}\, \frac{s}{s^2+4} + \frac{1}{s^2+1} . $$

Now take the inverse Laplace transform to obtain

$$ \kx(t) =\frac{1}{3} \cos (t) - \frac{1}{3} \cos (2t) + \sin (t) . $$

The procedure for linear constant-coefficient equations is as follows: Take an ordinary differential equation in the time variable $t$. Apply the Laplace transform to transform the equation into an algebraic (non differential) equation in the frequency domain. All the $\kx(t)$, $\kxprime(t)$, $\kxdprime(t)$, and so on, will be converted to $\kX(s)$, $s\kX(s) - \kx(0)$, $s^2\kX(s) - s\kx(0) - \kxprime(0)$, and so on. Solve the equation for $\kX(s)$. Then taking the inverse transform, if possible, find $\kx(t)$.

It should be noted that since not every function has a Laplace transform, not every equation can be solved in this manner. Also if the equation is not a linear constant-coefficient ODE, then applying the Laplace transform may not give us an algebraic equation.

Using the Heaviside function

Before we move on to more general equations than those we could solve before, we want to consider the Heaviside function. See Figure 6.1 for the graph.

$$ \ku(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \; t < 0 , \\ 1 & \text{if } \; t \geq 0 . \end{cases} $$

The Heaviside function is useful for putting other functions together or cutting functions off. Usually we use $\ku(t-a)$ for some constant $a$; we just shift the graph to the right by $a$. That is, $\ku(t-a) = 0$ when $t < a$ and $\ku(t-a) = 1$ when $t \geq a$. For example, suppose $\kf(t)$ is a “signal”, and we started receiving $\sin t$ at time $t=\pi$. The function $\kf(t)$ is then defined as

$$ \kf(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \; t < \pi , \\ \sin t & \text{if } \; t \geq \pi . \end{cases} $$

Using the Heaviside function, $\kf(t)$ can be written as

$$ \kf(t) = \ku(t - \pi) \, \sin t . $$

Similarly, the step function that is $1$ on the interval $[1,2)$ and $0$ elsewhere is written as

$$ \ku(t - 1) - \ku(t-2) . $$

With the Heaviside function we can express functions defined piecewise. If $\kf(t) = t$ when $t$ is in $[0,1]$, $\kf(t) = -t+2$ when $t$ is in $[1,2]$, and $\kf(t) = 0$ otherwise, then you write

$$ \kf(t) = t \, \bigl( \ku(t) - \ku(t-1) \bigr) + (-t+2) \, \bigl( \ku(t - 1) - \ku(t-2) \bigr) . $$

How does the Heaviside function interact with the Laplace transform? We saw that (for $a \geq 0$)

$$ \mathcal{L} \bigl\{ \ku(t-a) \bigr\} = \frac{e^{-as}}{s} . $$

This computation can be generalized into a shifting property or second shifting property:

$$ \boxed{~~ \mathcal{L} \bigl\{ \kf(t-a) \, \ku(t-a) \bigr\} = e^{-as} \mathcal{L} \bigl\{ \kf(t) \bigr\} \qquad (a \geq 0). ~~} \tag{6.1}$$

For example,

$$ \mathcal{L} \bigl\{ t \, \ku(t-1) \bigr\} = \mathcal{L} \bigl\{ \bigl( (t-1) + 1\bigr) \, \ku(t-1) \bigr\} = e^{-s} \mathcal{L} \bigl\{ t+1 \bigr\} = e^{-s} \left(\frac{1}{s^2} + \frac{1}{s} \right) . $$

Example 6.2.2

Consider the mass-spring system

$$ \kxdprime(t) + \kx(t) = \kf(t) , \quad \kx(0) = 0, \quad \kxprime(0) = 0, $$

where $\kf(t) = 1$ if $1 \leq t < 5$ and zero otherwise. The function $\kf(t)$ is not periodic and is defined piecewise; no problem for Laplace. Imagine a rocket attached to the mass is fired for 4 seconds starting at $t=1$. Or perhaps imagine an RLC circuit, where the voltage is raised at a constant rate for 4 seconds starting at $t=1$ and then held steady again starting at $t=5$ (recall that $\kf(t)$ represents the derivative of the voltage in the RLC circuit).

We write $\kf(t) = \ku(t-1) - \ku(t-5)$. We transform the equation and we plug in the initial conditions as before

$$ s^2 \kX(s) + \kX(s) = \frac{e^{-s}}{s} - \frac{e^{-5s}}{s} . $$

We solve for $\kX(s)$,

$$ \kX(s) = \frac{e^{-s}}{s(s^2+1)} - \frac{e^{-5s}}{s(s^2+1)} . $$

We leave it as an exercise to the reader to show that

$$ {\mathcal{L}}^{-1} \left\{ \frac{1}{s(s^2+1)} \right\} = 1 - \cos t . $$

In other words, $\mathcal{L} \{ 1 - \cos t \} = \frac{1}{s(s^2+1)}$. Using the second shifting property (6.1), we find

$$ {\mathcal{L}}^{-1} \left\{ \frac{e^{-s}}{s(s^2+1)} \right\} = {\mathcal{L}}^{-1} \bigl\{ e^{-s} \mathcal{L} \{ 1 - \cos t \} \bigr\} = \bigl( 1 - \cos (t-1) \bigr) \, \ku(t-1) . $$

Similarly,

$$ {\mathcal{L}}^{-1} \left\{ \frac{e^{-5s}}{s(s^2+1)} \right\} = {\mathcal{L}}^{-1} \bigl\{ e^{-5s} \mathcal{L} \{ 1 - \cos t \} \bigr\} = \bigl( 1 - \cos (t-5) \bigr) \, \ku(t-5) . $$

Hence, the solution is

$$ \kx(t) = \bigl( 1 - \cos (t-1) \bigr) \, \ku(t-1) - \bigl( 1 - \cos (t-5) \bigr) \, \ku(t-5) . $$

Transfer functions

The Laplace transform leads to the following useful concept for studying the steady-state behavior of a linear system. Consider an equation of the form

$$ L \kx = \kf(t) , $$

where $L$ is a linear constant-coefficient differential operator. Then $\kf(t)$ is usually thought of as the input of the system and $\kx(t)$ is thought of as the output of the system. For example, for a mass-spring system the input is the forcing function and the output is the behavior of the mass. We would like to have a convenient way to study the behavior of the system for different inputs.

Let us suppose that all the initial conditions are zero. We take the Laplace transform of the equation to obtain, for some $A(s)$,

$$ A(s) \kX(s) = \kF(s) . $$

Solving for the ratio ${\kX(s)}/{\kF(s)}$ gives the so-called transfer function $\kH(s) = {1}/{A(s)}$, that is,

$$ \kH(s) = \frac{\kX(s)}{\kF(s)} . $$

In other words, $\kX(s) = \kH(s) \kF(s)$. We get an algebraic dependence of the output of the system based on the input. We can now easily study the steady-state behavior of the system given different inputs by simply multiplying by the transfer function. Moreover, it is possible to compute the $\kH(s)$ without knowing exactly what the equation is by observing the output $\kX(s)$ for a given input $\kF(s)$. Once $\kH(s)$ is known, you can find the output for any input.

Example 6.2.3

Given $\kxdprime + \komegazero^2 \kx = \kf(t)$ (assume the initial conditions are zero), let us find the transfer function.

First, we take the Laplace transform of the equation,

$$ s^2 \kX(s) + \komegazero^2 \kX(s) = \kF(s) . $$

Now we solve for the transfer function ${\kX(s)}/{\kF(s)}$,

$$ \kH(s) = \frac{\kX(s)}{\kF(s)} = \frac{1}{s^2 + \komegazero^2} . $$

Let us see how to use the transfer function. Suppose we have the constant input $\kf(t) = 1$. Hence $\kF(s) = {1}/{s}$, and

$$ \kX(s) = \kH(s) \kF(s) = \frac{1}{s^2+\komegazero^2} \, \frac{1}{s} . $$

Taking the inverse Laplace transform of $\kX(s)$, we obtain

$$ \kx(t) = \frac{1-\cos(\komegazero t)}{\komegazero^2} . $$

Similarly, for any other input $\kF(s)$, the output is $\kX(s) = \kH(s) \kF(s) = \frac{1}{s^2+\komegazero^2} \, \kF(s)$.

Transforms of integrals

A feature of the Laplace transform is that it is able to deal with integral equations. These are equations in which integrals rather than derivatives of functions appear. The basic property, which can be proved by applying the definition and doing integration by parts, is

$$ \boxed{~~ \mathcal{L} \left\{ \int_0^t \kf(\tau) \, d\tau \right\} = \frac{1}{s} \, \kF(s) . ~~} $$

It is sometimes useful (e.g. for computing the inverse transform) to write this as

$$ \int_0^t \kf(\tau) \, d\tau = {\mathcal{L}}^{-1} \left\{ \frac{1}{s} \, \kF(s) \right\} . $$

Example 6.2.4

To compute ${\mathcal{L}}^{-1} \left\{\frac{1}{s(s^2+1)}\right\}$, we could apply this integration rule:

$$ {\mathcal{L}}^{-1} \left\{ \frac{1}{s} \, \frac{1}{s^2+1} \right\} = \int_0^t {\mathcal{L}}^{-1} \left\{ \frac{1}{s^2+1} \right\} \, d\tau = \int_0^t \sin \tau \, d\tau = 1 - \cos t . $$

Example 6.2.5

An equation containing an integral of the unknown function is called an integral equation. Consider

$$ \kx(t) - t = \int_0^t \kx(\tau) \, d\tau , $$

where we wish to solve for $\kx(t)$. We apply the Laplace transform to get

$$ \kX(s) - \frac{1}{s^2} = \frac{1}{s} \kX(s) , $$

where $\kX(s) = \mathcal{L} \bigl\{ \kx(t) \bigr\}$. Thus

$$ \kX(s) = \frac{1}{s(s-1)} = \frac{1}{s-1} - \frac{1}{s} . $$

The inverse Laplace transform gives

$$ \kx(t) = e^t - 1 . $$

Periodic functions

The reader might ask: What about periodic functions as our input $\kf(t)$? That is, a function $\kf(t)$ where $\kf(t) = \kf(t+P)$ for some constant $P$ (the period). Well, let us compute $\kF(s)$:

Solving for $\kF(s)$, we get

$$ \kF(s) = \frac{1}{1-e^{-Ps}} \int_0^P e^{-st} \kf(t) \, dt . $$

As before, computing the inverse would be more complex and possibly involve consulting a table. Let us not worry about computing the inverse here.

Example 6.2.6

Suppose $\kf(t)$ is a version of the sawtooth, that is, let $\kf(t) = t$ for $0 \leq t < 1$ and use $\kf(t)=\kf(t+1)$ to extend it periodically. So $\kf(t) = t-1$ for $1 \leq t < 2$, $\kf(t) = t-2$ for $2 \leq t < 3$, etc. Then $P=1$ and a short computation with integration by parts gets

$$ \kF(s) = \frac{1}{1-e^{-s}} \int_0^1 e^{-st} t \, dt = \frac{1}{1-e^{-s}} \left( \frac{-e^{-s}}{s}-\frac{e^{-s}}{s^2} + \frac{1}{s^2} \right) = \frac{-e^{-s}}{(1-e^{-s})s} + \frac{1}{s^2} . $$
