Consider the general second-order linear differential equation

$$ A(x) \kydprime + B(x)\kyprime + C(x)\ky = \kF(x) . $$

We often divide through by $A(x)$ to get

$$ \kydprime + \kp(x)\kyprime + \kq(x)\ky = \kf(x) , \tag{2.1}$$

where $\kp(x) = {B(x)}/{A(x)}$, $\kq(x) = {C(x)}/{A(x)}$, and $\kf(x) = {\kF(x)}/{A(x)}$. The word linear means that the equation contains no powers or functions of $\ky$, $\kyprime$, and $\kydprime$.

Homogeneous equations and superposition

In the special case when $\kf(x) = 0$, we have a so-called homogeneous equation

$$ \kydprime + \kp(x)\kyprime + \kq(x)\ky = 0 . \tag{2.2}$$

We have already seen some second-order linear homogeneous equations.

$$ \begin{align*} \qquad \kydprime + k^2 \ky & = 0 & & \text{Two solutions are:} \quad \kyone = \cos (kx), \quad \kytwo = \sin(kx) . \qquad \\ \qquad \kydprime - k^2 \ky & = 0 & & \text{Two solutions are:} \quad \kyone = e^{kx}, \quad \kytwo = e^{-kx} . \qquad \end{align*} $$

If we know two solutions of a linear homogeneous equation, we know many more of them.

Theorem 2.1.1

Superposition

Suppose $\kyone$ and $\kytwo$ are two solutions of the homogeneous equation (2.2). Then

$$ \ky(x) = C_1 \kyone(x) + C_2 \kytwo(x) , $$

also solves (2.2) for arbitrary constants $C_1$ and $C_2$.

That is, we can add solutions together and multiply them by constants to obtain new and different solutions. We call the expression $C_1 \kyone + C_2 \kytwo$ a linear combination of $\kyone$ and $\kytwo$. Let us prove this theorem; the proof is very enlightening and illustrates how linear equations work.

The proof becomes even simpler to state if we use the operator notation. An operator is an object that eats functions and spits out functions (kind of like what a function is, but a function eats numbers and spits out numbers). Define the operator $L$ by

$$ L\ky = \kydprime + \kp\kyprime + \kq\ky . $$

The differential equation now becomes $L\ky=0$. The operator (and the equation) $L$ being linear means that $L(C_1\kyone + C_2\kytwo) = C_1 L\kyone + C_2 L\kytwo$. It is almost as if we were “multiplying” by $L$. The proof above becomes

$$ L\ky = L(C_1\kyone + C_2\kytwo) = C_1 L\kyone + C_2 L\kytwo = C_1 \cdot 0 + C_2 \cdot 0 = 0 . $$

Two different solutions to the second equation $\kydprime - k^2\ky = 0$ are $\kyone = \cosh (kx)$ and $\kytwo = \sinh (kx)$. Recalling the definition of $\sinh$ and $\cosh$, we note that these are solutions by superposition as they are linear combinations of the two exponential solutions: $\cosh(kx) = \frac{e^{kx} + e^{-kx}}{2} = ({1}/{2}) e^{kx} + ({1}/{2}) e^{-kx}$ and $\sinh(kx) = \frac{e^{kx} - e^{-kx}}{2} = ({1}/{2}) e^{kx} + ({-1}/{2}) e^{-kx}$.

The functions $\sinh$ and $\cosh$ are sometimes more convenient to use than the exponential. Let us review some of their properties:

$$ \begin{align*} & \cosh 0 = 1 , & & \sinh 0 = 0 , \\ & \frac{d}{dx} \Bigl[ \cosh x \Bigr] = \sinh x , & & \frac{d}{dx} \Bigl[ \sinh x \Bigr] = \cosh x , \\ & \cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = 1 . \end{align*} $$

Existence and uniqueness

Linear equations have nice and simple answers to the existence and uniqueness question.

Theorem 2.1.2

Existence and uniqueness

Suppose $\kp, \kq, \kf$ are continuous functions on some interval $I$, $a$ is a number in $I$, and $b_0, b_1$ are constants. Then the equation

$$ \kydprime + \kp(x) \kyprime + \kq(x) \ky = \kf(x) , $$

has exactly one solution $\ky(x)$ defined on the interval $I$ satisfying the initial conditions

$$ \ky(a) = b_0 , \qquad \kyprime(a) = b_1 . $$

For example, the equation $\kydprime + k^2 \ky = 0$ with $\ky(0) = b_0$ and $\kyprime(0) = b_1$ has the solution

$$ \ky(x) = b_0 \cos (kx) + \frac{b_1}{k} \sin (kx) . $$

The equation $\kydprime - k^2 \ky = 0$ with $\ky(0) = b_0$ and $\kyprime(0) = b_1$ has the solution

$$ \ky(x) = b_0 \cosh (kx) + \frac{b_1}{k} \sinh (kx) . $$

Using $\cosh$ and $\sinh$ in this solution allows us to solve for the initial conditions in a cleaner way than if we had used the exponentials.

The initial conditions for a second-order ODE consist of two equations. Common sense tells us that if we have two arbitrary constants and two equations, then we should be able to solve for the constants and find a solution to the differential equation satisfying the initial conditions.

Linear independence

Question: Suppose we find two different solutions $\kyone$ and $\kytwo$ to the homogeneous equation (2.2). Can every solution be written (using superposition) in the form $\ky = C_1 \kyone + C_2 \kytwo$?

The answer is affirmative! Provided that $\kyone$ and $\kytwo$ are different enough in the following sense. We say $\kyone$ and $\kytwo$ are linearly independent if one is not a constant multiple of the other.

Theorem 2.1.3

Let $\kp, \kq$ be continuous functions. Let $\kyone$ and $\kytwo$ be two linearly independent solutions to the homogeneous equation (2.2). Then every other solution is of the form

$$ \ky = C_1 \kyone + C_2 \kytwo . $$

That is, $\ky = C_1 \kyone + C_2 \kytwo$ is the general solution.

For example, we found the solutions $\kyone = \cos x$ and $\kytwo = \sin x$ for the equation $\kydprime + \ky = 0$. It is not hard to see that sine and cosine are not constant multiples of each other. Indeed, if $\sin x = A \cos x$ for some constant $A$, plugging in $x=0$ would imply $A = 0$. But then $\sin x = 0$ for all $x$, which is preposterous. So $\kyone$ and $\kytwo$ are linearly independent. Hence,

$$ \ky = C_1 \cos x + C_2 \sin x $$

is the general solution to $\kydprime + \ky = 0$.

For two functions, checking linear independence is rather simple. Here is another example. Consider $\kydprime-2x^{-2}\ky = 0$. Then $\kyone = x^2$ and $\kytwo = {1}/{x}$ are solutions. To see that they are linearly independent, suppose one is a multiple of the other: $\kyone = A \kytwo$, we just have to show that $A$ cannot be a constant. In this case, we have $A = {\kyone}/{\kytwo} = x^3$, which is most decidedly not a constant. So $\ky = C_1 x^2 + C_2 {1}/{x}$ is the general solution.

Reduction of order

If you have one nonzero solution to a second-order linear homogeneous equation, then you can find another one. This is the reduction of order method. The idea is that if we somehow found $\kyone$ as a solution of $\kydprime + \kp(x) \kyprime + \kq(x) \ky = 0$, then we try a second solution of the form $\kytwo(x) = \kyone(x) \kv(x)$. After solving this equation for $\kw$ (integrating factor), we find $\kv$ by antidifferentiating $\kw$. We then form $\kytwo$ by computing $\kyone \kv$. For example, suppose we somehow know $\kyone = x$ is a solution to $\kydprime+x^{-1}\kyprime-x^{-2} \ky=0$. The equation for $\kw$ is then $x\kwprime + 3 \kw = 0$. We find a solution, $\kw = Cx^{-3}$, and we find an antiderivative $\kv = \frac{-C}{2x^2}$. Hence $\kytwo = \kyone \kv = \frac{-C}{2x}$. Any $C$ works and so $C=-2$ makes $\kytwo = {1}/{x}$. Thus, the general solution is $\ky = C_1 x + C_2{1}/{x}$.
